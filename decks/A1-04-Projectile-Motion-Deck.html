Starter · question 1 of 5

Displacement-time graph

A displacement-time graph is a horizontal straight line. What does this tell you about the object?

A It is moving at a constant velocity
B It is moving with constant acceleration
C It is at rest, with zero velocity
D It is moving horizontally at a steady speed
Fingers up on three

Answer: C

The displacement is not changing, so the velocity is zero. The object is at rest. A horizontal line never means horizontal motion.

Why the others fail

AConstant velocity gives a sloped straight line, not a flat one
BConstant acceleration gives a curve, not a straight line
DThe axes are displacement and time; the graph says nothing about direction of travel

Starter · question 2 of 5

Reading a velocity-time graph

A velocity-time graph is a straight line from \(v=8.0\,\text{m s}^{-1}\) at \(t=0\) down to \(v=0\) at \(t=4.0\,\text{s}\). What are the acceleration and the distance travelled?

A −2.0 m s⁻², 32 m
B −2.0 m s⁻², 16 m
C −0.50 m s⁻², 16 m
D +2.0 m s⁻², 32 m
Fingers up on three

Answer: B

\(a = \dfrac{0-8.0}{4.0} = -2.0\,\text{m s}^{-2};\ \text{area} = \tfrac12\times4.0\times8.0 = 16\,\text{m}\)

Why the others fail

AUses the rectangle 8.0×4.0 = 32 m instead of the triangle
C8.0/4.0² instead of 8.0/4.0 for the gradient
DWrong sign, and the rectangle area again

Starter · question 3 of 5

Equal areas, opposite sign

An object's velocity-time graph is above the axis for the first 5 s and below it, with an equal area, for the next 5 s. At \(t=10\,\text{s}\):

A the total distance travelled is zero
B the object is at rest with zero acceleration
C the displacement equals the distance travelled
D the displacement is zero, though the distance is not
Fingers up on three

Answer: D

Equal areas above and below the axis cancel, so the displacement is zero, but the object travelled through both, so the distance travelled is their sum.

Why the others fail

AConfuses zero displacement with zero distance travelled
BBeing back at the start says nothing about the velocity or acceleration at that instant
CThey are only equal when the motion is one-directional throughout

Starter · question 4 of 5

Instantaneous velocity from a curve

To find the instantaneous velocity at \(t=4.0\,\text{s}\) from a curved displacement-time graph you should:

A read the displacement at \(t=4.0\,\text{s}\) and divide by 4.0
B find the area under the curve up to \(t=4.0\,\text{s}\)
C draw a tangent at \(t=4.0\,\text{s}\) and find its gradient
D join the origin to the point at \(t=4.0\,\text{s}\) and find that gradient
Fingers up on three

Answer: C

Only the gradient of a tangent gives the velocity at an instant. A and D both give average velocities; area under a displacement-time graph has no physical meaning.

Why the others fail

AGives an average velocity from the origin, not an instantaneous one
BArea under a displacement-time graph means nothing
DA chord gives an average velocity, not the value at that instant

Starter · question 5 of 5

Steepest section of a v-t graph

On a velocity-time graph, the steepest section corresponds to the object having

A the greatest speed
B the greatest acceleration
C zero acceleration
D the greatest displacement
Fingers up on three

Answer: B

The gradient of a velocity-time graph is the acceleration, so the steepest section is where the acceleration is greatest. The fastest point is simply the highest point on the line.

Why the others fail

ASpeed is read from the height of the line, not its steepness
CA flat, not steep, section has zero acceleration
DDisplacement comes from the area under the line, not its gradient

Drop a ball from a height, and at the same instant fire an identical ball horizontally from the same height. Ignoring air resistance, both balls hit the ground at exactly the same moment — no matter how fast the second one was launched sideways.

A.1 · Projectile Motion10
The definition we are working from

A projectile is an object that has been projected through the air — fired, launched, thrown, kicked or hit, and which then moves only under the action of gravity, and air resistance if significant. It has no ability to power or control its own motion.

Chunk 1 · equation

Resolving velocity into components

Fig 2: Resolving a projectile's velocity \(v\) into components at angle \(\theta\) above the horizontal.

The instantaneous velocity of a projectile at any time can be resolved into vertical and horizontal components, \(v_V\) and \(v_H\).

Components of velocity
\(v_V = v\sin\theta\)
\(v_H = v\cos\theta\)
A.1 · Projectile Motion13
Chunk 1 · common mistake

Getting θ right

⚠️ Make sure the angle \(\theta\) is always the angle between the velocity and the horizontal, never the vertical, never the trajectory itself.

Get the angle wrong and \(v_H\) and \(v_V\) swap, and sinθ and cosθ end up on the wrong component.

🔑 Top tip: the vertical component tells you the times (to maximum height, and to hit the ground) and the maximum height reached; the horizontal component then gives you the range.

A tennis player strikes the ball so it leaves the racket at velocity \(v\) at angle \(\theta\) below the horizontal. Find the vertical and horizontal components of this velocity.

Chunk 2 · the key idea

Horizontal and vertical motion are independent

Fig 3: Stroboscopic image of a bouncing ball: equal time intervals between images.

Components perpendicular to each other can be analysed separately. If there is no air resistance, the horizontal component of velocity stays constant until the projectile hits something.

Horizontal spacing between images is always the same (\(v_H\) constant); vertical spacing decreases going up, increases coming down.

✓ This answers the diagnostic: horizontal speed stays constant, because there is no horizontal force acting on the stone.

A.1 · Projectile Motion23
Chunk 2 · vocabulary

Trajectory and range

Trajectory

The path followed by a projectile. It is parabolic if air resistance is negligible.

Range

The horizontal distance covered by the projectile.

Any object close to the Earth's surface, affected only by gravity, accelerates towards the Earth at 9.8 m s⁻². This remains true even while it is also moving sideways.

A.1 · Projectile Motion24
Chunk 2 · equation

Time of flight and range from a height

Fig 4: Investigating range \(x\) as height \(h\) is varied.

For a projectile launched horizontally from height \(h\), the time of flight comes entirely from the vertical motion, starting from rest vertically.

Time to fall from height h
\(h = \tfrac12 gt^2\)
Range, from the constant horizontal velocity
\(x = v_H \cdot t\)

A.1 · Projectile Motion26
Worked example A1.10 · solution

Identify → Data → Equation → Answer

Fig 8: bullet fired horizontally at 524 m/s from 22.0 m, landing 1.11 km away.

A bullet is fired horizontally from height \(h\) above the ground. Air resistance is negligible. Find where it hits the ground.

1Identify: the vertical motion gives the time to fall; the range then follows from the constant horizontal velocity.
2Data: \(v_H=\)524 m s⁻¹, \(h=\)22.0 m, \(g=\)9.81 m s⁻².
3Equation: \(h=\tfrac12 gt^2 \Rightarrow t=\sqrt{2h/g}\), then \(x=v_H t\)
4Answer: \(t=\sqrt{2(22.0)/9.81}=\)2.12 s. \(x=524\times2.12=\)1.11×10³ m (1.11 km).

✓ Examiner tip: the bullet's own mass and its huge horizontal speed never enter the time calculation at all; only \(h\) and \(g\) decide how long it falls for.

A stone is thrown upwards from a height above the ground at a given speed and angle. Ignoring air resistance, find (a) its maximum height, (b) its vertical velocity on impact, (c) the time to reach the ground, (d) the horizontal range, and (e) the velocity of impact.

A.1 · Projectile Motion36
Worked example A1.11 · solution

Identify → Data → Equation → Answer

Fig 9: launched at 18.0 m/s, 52°, from 1.6 m.

(a) max height, (b) impact \(v_V\), (c) time to ground, (d) range, (e) impact velocity.

1Identify: resolve first; use \(v_V\) for times and heights, \(v_H\) for range, and combine both for the final impact velocity.
2Data: \(v_V=18.0\sin52^\circ=\)14.2 m s⁻¹ up, \(v_H=18.0\cos52^\circ=\)11.1 m s⁻¹.
3Equation: (a) \(v^2=u^2+2as\), \(v=0\) at the top. (b) \(v^2=u^2+2as\) over the full fall. (c) \(v=u+at\). (d) \(x=v_H t\). (e) \(v_i^2=v_H^2+v_V^2\), \(\tan\theta=v_V/v_H\).
4Answer: (a) 10.3 m above release (11.9 m above ground). (b) 15.3 m s⁻¹ downwards. (c) 3.00 s. (d) 33.3 m. (e) 18.9 m s⁻¹ at 54° below horizontal.

✓ Examiner tip: five sub-parts, but only two ideas: everything vertical uses \(v_V\) and \(g\); everything horizontal uses the unchanging \(v_H\).

How does air resistance affect the range of a projectile compared with the no-drag case?

A It increases it
B It reduces it
C It has no effect
D It makes the trajectory circular

A.1 · Projectile Motion46
Context · HL extension

Projectiles with air resistance

Fluid resistance affects a projectile's time of flight, trajectory, velocity, acceleration, range and can bring it to a terminal speed.

Drag opposes the velocity at every instant, so it no longer acts only vertically: it removes energy from both components, and the trajectory is no longer a symmetric parabola.

💡 Challenge: how does air resistance affect the range of a projectile compared with the no-drag case? It reduces it, because \(v_H\) is no longer constant and the projectile's speed is smaller throughout the flight.

A.1 · Projectile Motion47
Context · real-world investigation

Finding the range-height relationship

A steel ball projected horizontally from a table edge lands further away the higher the table is. Measuring range \(x\) for several heights \(h\) gives a concave-down curve; plotting \(x\) against \(\sqrt{h}\) straightens it into a line through the origin, gradient \(v_H\sqrt{2/g}\).

✓ Examiner tip: always state the assumption of negligible air resistance before drawing conclusions from an \(x\)–\(h\) investigation; the whole \(x\propto\sqrt{h}\) relationship depends on it.
