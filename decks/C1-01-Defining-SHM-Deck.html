Equilibrium

A book rests in equilibrium on a horizontal bench. The resultant force acting on the book is

A equal to the weight of the book
B zero
C directed vertically upwards
D constantly changing in size
Fingers up on three

Answer — B

The equilibrium position is the position in which there is no resultant force acting on the object. This is the position an oscillator returns to, and the definition we use all lesson.

Why the others fail

AThe weight is balanced by the normal contact force — it is one of the forces, not the resultant
CIf there were a net upward force the book would accelerate upwards
DIn equilibrium the resultant stays zero; nothing is changing

Hooke's law

A spring extends by 4.0 cm when a force of 2.0 N is applied to it. The spring constant is

A 0.50 N m⁻¹
B 8.0 N m⁻¹
C 50 N m⁻¹
D 500 N m⁻¹
Fingers up on three

Answer — C

k = F / x = 2.0 / 0.040 = 50 N m⁻¹

The spring constant is a measure of the spring's stiffness: force divided by deformation.

Why the others fail

Ax / F instead of F / x — inverted
BF × x — multiplying rather than dividing
D4.0 cm read as 4.0 mm; a factor of ten in the unit conversion

Energy in a swing

A immediately after release
B at the lowest point of the swing
C at the highest point of the swing
D at the moment it reverses direction
Fingers up on three

Chunk 1 · oscillations

Oscillations, and why we model them

Oscillation

A regularly repeated backwards-and-forwards movement about the same central point, along the same path.

Where we find them

planetsthe heartclocksenginesatoms in moleculesinstrumentsspeech · eardrumwater waveslighttidesa.c. currents

Simple harmonic motion

A simplified theoretical model of oscillations — the starting point for all of them. Perfect SHM: no resistive forces, constant amplitude, every oscillation the same time (isochronous).

🔬 Nature of science — a model is a simplified representation of something more complex. Almost no real oscillator is perfectly simple harmonic; we understand the simple version first.

C.1 · Defining Simple Harmonic Motion13
Chunk 1 · the mechanism

Equilibrium, and the restoring force

Fig 2 — In a the spring forces balance; in b the displaced mass feels a resultant restoring force.

Equilibrium position

Where the resultant force is zero. A perfect oscillator is treated as a point mass.

Restoring force

Once displaced, a resultant force always pulls the mass back towards equilibrium.

Released at B: accelerates inwards → fastest at the centre → inertia carries it through → decelerates → stops and reverses. KE ⇄ PE.

Period and frequency

Period, frequency and angular frequency
T = 1/f
T = 2π/ω

T and f carry the same information; quote whichever is greater than one. Both stay constant even as amplitude decays — isochronous.

Units in common use

Identify → Data → Equation → Answer

1
Identify: A and B are the two extreme positions of the same end of the mass, so the 18 cm span includes the 12 cm length of the mass itself. Amplitude is measured from equilibrium, so it is half of the remaining distance.

C.1 · Defining Simple Harmonic Motion29
Chunk 2 · equation

Period of a mass–spring system

Time period of a mass–spring system
T = 2π√(m/k)
Same relationship, using ω = 2π/T
ω² = k/m

Only m and k appear: no g, no amplitude. ×4 mass → ×2 period; ×4 stiffness → ÷2 period.

Exact only for a point mass on a massless spring of well-defined k. Buildings, bridges and a clamped oscillating ruler are the same idea, more complex.

C.1 · Defining Simple Harmonic Motion31
Chunk 2 · oscillator 2

The simple pendulum

Fig 6 — Rigid frictionless support, rod or string of negligible mass, mass m.

Simple pendulum

A spherical mass — the bob — on a rod or string of negligible mass, from a rigid frictionless support.

All the mass acts as a point mass at the centre of the sphere. Displacement is the angle to the vertical. Gravity provides the restoring force.

C.1 · Defining Simple Harmonic Motion32
Chunk 2 · where the restoring force comes from

Resolving the weight

Fig 7 — mg sinθ restores, mg cosθ tensions the string.

Resolve the weight mg into two perpendicular components:

mg sinθ → restoring forcemg cosθ → tension

Only two forces act — tension and weight. Their resultant is the restoring force mg sinθ.

C.1 · Defining Simple Harmonic Motion33
Chunk 2 · equation

Period of a simple pendulum

Fig 8 — T against l for a pendulum: a curve of decreasing gradient.

Small amplitudes only. T depends on length l alone, measured from the support to the centre of mass. Not affected by mass — doubling m doubles the restoring force too.

Time period of a simple pendulum
T = 2π√(l/g)

📈 Linearise: T² against l is straight through the origin, gradient 4π²/g — so a pendulum measures g.

C.1 · Defining Simple Harmonic Motion34
Chunk 2 · Tool 3 — approximation and estimation

How small is a "small angle"?

SHM needs mg sinθ ∝ θ. For small angles sinθ, tanθ and θ (in rad) are almost identical; the difference grows with angle.

Task: find the minimum angle at which sinθ, tanθ and θ differ by at least 1%.

HL extension

sinθ ≈ θ − θ³/6   tanθ ≈ θ + θ³/3

Errors grow as θ², so the approximation fails suddenly, not gradually.

✓ tanθ is 1% above θ at ≈ 10°; sinθ is 1% below θ at ≈ 14°. Keep amplitudes under about 10°.

C.1 · Defining Simple Harmonic Motion36
Worked example C1.2 · solution

Identify → Data → Equation → Answer

1
Identify: At the new equilibrium the spring force balances the weight, so Hooke's law gives k; then the mass–spring period equation gives T and hence f.
2
Data: m = 0.200 kg; x = 5.4 × 10⁻² m; g = 9.8 N kg⁻¹.
3
Equation: F_H = −kx → 0.200 × 9.8 = −k × (5.4 × 10⁻²) → k = −36 N m⁻¹ · T = 2π√(0.200/36) = 0.47 s · f = 1/0.47 = 2.1 Hz
4
Answer: (a) k = −36 N m⁻¹ — the negative sign shows that force and displacement are in opposite directions. (b) f = 2.1 Hz. (c) The spring may become overstretched, so Hooke's law is no longer applicable.

C.1 · Defining Simple Harmonic Motion38
Worked example C1.3 · solution

Identify → Data → Equation → Answer

1
Identify: Only l and g matter — the bob's mass is irrelevant. Apply the pendulum period equation twice, then compare ratios.
2

C.1 · Defining Simple Harmonic Motion45
Chunk 3 · the condition

The condition that produces SHM

SHM occurs if the restoring force F is proportional to the displacement x, and always acts back towards equilibrium.

Force condition
F ∝ −x

The minus sign matters: an increasing force opposes increasing displacement.

Spring: F_H = −kx satisfies it exactly. Pendulum: mg sinθ ∝ θ only while θ is small.

C.1 · Defining Simple Harmonic Motion46
Chunk 3 · the definition

From force to motion

Fig 9 — a against x: straight line through the origin, negative gradient, from a_max at −x₀ to −a_max at +x₀.

Define SHM by the motion, not the force. Since F = ma:

Definition of SHM
a ∝ −x

SHM: the acceleration is proportional to the displacement, in the opposite direction, always towards equilibrium. A straight line through the origin here proves SHM.

C.1 · Defining Simple Harmonic Motion47
Chunk 3 · the consequence

Why amplitude does not affect period

Double the amplitude → the restoring force and the acceleration double.

Twice the distance at twice the average speed → the same time per oscillation.

✓ Amplitude does not affect time period — and that answers the diagnostic question.

Write a = −constant × x. The constant must grow with frequency; it can be shown to equal ω².

Defining equation of SHM
a = −ω²x

C.1 · Defining Simple Harmonic Motion51
Chunk 3 · graphs

Displacement, velocity, acceleration

Fig 11 — Starting at x = 0: (a) x–t, (b) v–t, (c) a–t. Zero gradient on one graph gives zero on the next.

v is the gradient of x–t; a is the gradient of v–t.

v = Δx/Δta = Δv/Δt

v maximum at x = 0, zero at ±x₀. a maximum at ±x₀, zero at x = 0 — the restoring force is greatest where displacement is greatest.

⚠️ The three amplitudes are arbitrary — do not compare them.

Chunk 3 · phase

Phase difference

Fig 12 — Four oscillations out of phase: same frequency, different points in the cycle.

Identical swings, so the same frequency — but each at a different point of its oscillation. They are out of phase.

Phase difference

Between two oscillations of the same frequency whose maximum values do not occur at the same time.

C.1 · Defining Simple Harmonic Motion53
Chunk 3 · phase

Quantifying the phase difference

Measure it as the fraction of a period between the peaks. One cycle = 2π rad, so phase differences are quoted in π.

Fraction of a cyclePhase differenceDegrees
T/4π/2 rad90°
T/2π rad180°
3T/43π/2 rad270°

3π/2 is equal in magnitude to π/2, but sometimes which peak came first matters. Phase differences run from 0 to 2π rad.

✓ Learn these two: x to v is π/2; x to a is π.

The definition

Which statement defines simple harmonic motion?

A The velocity is proportional to the displacement and in the same direction
B The acceleration is constant and directed towards the equilibrium position
C The acceleration is proportional to the displacement and in the opposite direction
D The period is proportional to the amplitude of the oscillation
Fingers up on three

Answer — C

SHM is defined by a ∝ −x: the acceleration is proportional to displacement and directed back towards equilibrium, written a = −ω²x.

Why the others fail

AVelocity is π/2 out of phase with displacement, not proportional to it
BThe acceleration is not constant — it grows with displacement
DPeriod is independent of amplitude; that is the defining feature

At maximum displacement

A particle in SHM is at maximum displacement. At that instant

A velocity and acceleration are both maximum
B velocity and acceleration are both zero
C velocity is maximum and acceleration is zero
D velocity is zero and acceleration is maximum
Fingers up on three

Answer — D

At ±x₀ the oscillator has momentarily stopped, so v = 0, but the restoring force and therefore the acceleration are greatest.

Why the others fail

AThe velocity is zero at the extremes
BZero acceleration would mean no restoring force at the point where it is largest
CThat describes the equilibrium position instead

Phase difference

The phase difference between the displacement and the acceleration of a particle in SHM is

A 0
B π/2 rad
C π rad
D 2π rad
Fingers up on three

Answer — C

Acceleration is always opposite in sign to displacement, so the two curves are exactly antiphase: π rad (180°).

Why the others fail

AThat would mean the two peak together
Bπ/2 is displacement against velocity
D2π is a whole cycle — indistinguishable from being in phase

Common mistakes

MisconceptionCorrect view
Amplitude is the distance between the extreme positionsThat distance equals twice the amplitude; amplitude is measured from the equilibrium position
Moving from one extreme to the other is one oscillationThat is half an oscillation; a cycle ends when the mass returns to a position moving in the same direction
A bigger amplitude means a longer periodAmplitude does not affect period: the force and acceleration scale with displacement, so the mass covers twice the distance at twice the average speed
A vertical mass–spring period depends on gWeight is constant, so it shifts the equilibrium position but not the period; only m and k matter. The pendulum is the one that depends on g

Common mistakes

MisconceptionCorrect view
Heavier pendulum bob, longer periodPeriod is independent of mass — doubling the mass doubles the restoring force too. Only l and g matter
Acceleration is zero at the extremes because the mass has stoppedAt ±x₀ the velocity is zero but the acceleration is maximum, since the restoring force is greatest there

Context · why this mattered

Solving the problem of longitude

Galileo found that pendulums keep constant time. Huygens built the first pendulum clock in 1656. By 1800 the best were out by under a second a day — the world's most accurate timekeepers for over 250 years.

Longitude needed the exact time of a star sighting, but a pendulum cannot cope with a moving ship. The British reward was won by the clockmaker James Harrison, using oscillating spheres on springs instead of a pendulum.
