Resonance
What happens when a driver matches a system’s natural rhythm – and why bridges can collapse in a gentle wind
Fig 1 — The Tacoma Narrows Bridge in 1940. A steady wind, matching the bridge’s natural frequency, caused catastrophic oscillations.

Natural frequency – the system’s own rhythm

Every oscillating system has a natural frequency, f₀. It is determined by two things: the stiffness (springiness) that tries to restore equilibrium, and the inertia (mass) that resists changes in motion. For a mass‑spring system:

f₀ = (1/(2π)) √(k/m)

When you displace the system and release it, it vibrates at this frequency – these are free vibrations. In the perfect (ideal) case with no energy loss, the motion is simple harmonic and never dies out.

Fig 2 — A mass–spring oscillator. The natural frequency depends on the spring constant k and the mass m.

Damping – energy leaks out

Real systems lose energy through friction, air resistance, or fluid drag. These damping forces remove mechanical energy, causing the amplitude to decrease. Three important cases appear on a displacement–time graph:

Fig 3 — Damping types. Under‑damped: oscillations with exponentially decaying amplitude. Critically damped: fastest return to equilibrium without overshoot. Over‑damped: slow, non‑oscillatory return.
💡 Examiner insight: Critical damping is the most efficient way to stop oscillations – used in car shock absorbers and fire‑door closers. Over‑damping actually slows the system down.

Forced vibrations and resonance

When an external driver (e.g. a motor, a push, a vibrating base) applies a periodic force, the system performs forced vibrations. The amplitude depends on how close the driving frequency is to the natural frequency.

When the driving frequency equals (or is very close to) the natural frequency, energy is transferred most efficiently – the amplitude becomes very large. This is resonance.

Fig 4 — Barton’s pendulum demonstration. The lighter pendulum with the same length as the heavy driver (pendulum 3) resonates with the largest amplitude. Cones provide adjustable damping.
✓ Key exam fact: At resonance, the driver leads the driven system by 90° (π/2 rad). Below resonance they are in phase; above resonance they are 180° out of phase.
Resonance curves – amplitude and phase
Fig 5 — Amplitude–frequency resonance curves. With increasing damping the peak flattens, broadens, and shifts to a slightly lower frequency.
Fig 6 — Phase difference between driver and driven system. The transition sharpens for low damping. At resonance the phase difference is 90°.
Fig 7 — Energy–frequency resonance curves. When resonance is defined by maximum energy (rather than amplitude), all curves peak at the natural frequency f₀, regardless of damping.
⚠️ Common trap: Students often think the resonant frequency (amplitude peak) is exactly f₀. In fact, with damping the amplitude peak shifts below f₀. The energy peak, however, remains at f₀.

Worked Example — Resonance in a mass‑spring system

1
Known values: mass m = 0.250 kg, spring constant k = 25.0 N m⁻¹, negligible damping.
2
Calculate natural frequency: f₀ = (1/(2π))√(k/m) = (1/(2π))√(25.0 / 0.250) = 1.59 Hz.
3
Resonance condition: With negligible damping, resonance occurs when driving frequency fd = f₀ = 1.59 Hz.
4
Phase: At resonance the driver leads the driven mass by 90° (π/2 rad).

Resonance in practice

Resonance is both useful and dangerous:

Fig 8 — Microwave ovens use resonance to excite water molecules; MRI scanners detect proton resonance; tuned mass dampers suppress unwanted bridge vibrations.

Microwave ovens: the 2.45 GHz radiation matches the rotational resonance of water molecules, heating food efficiently. In MRI, hydrogen nuclei in the body resonate at radio frequencies in a strong magnetic field, producing images. The Millennium Footbridge in London swayed because pedestrians inadvertently walked in step with the bridge’s natural frequency – the problem was solved by adding damping.

Graph-based analysis – gong sound decay

The intensity of a struck gong decays exponentially. A convenient way to analyse this is to plot sound pressure level (in dB) versus time, because an exponential decay in amplitude gives a straight line on a dB–time graph. The slope tells us how quickly the energy leaks away.

Fig 9 — Gong sound pressure data. A drop of 3 dB corresponds to the sound intensity halving. The gradient of the best‑fit line gives the decay rate.

From the line L = 88 − 1.9t (where L is in dB and t in seconds): the slope magnitude is 1.9 dB s⁻¹, so the time for a 3 dB drop (half intensity) is Δt = 3/1.9 ≈ 1.6 s. If the background noise is 30 dB, the gong is audible for about 30.5 s.

Exam-style questions
Exam question · explain & calculate
A car suspension can be modelled as a mass‑spring‑damper system. The body has a natural frequency of 1.2 Hz. When driving over evenly spaced bumps at 15 m s⁻¹, the car bounces noticeably.
(a) Explain why the shock absorber is designed to provide near‑critical damping. [2 marks]
(b) Estimate the distance between bumps. [3 marks]
(a) Energy dissipated as quickly as possible without oscillation ✓; ensures car body returns to equilibrium in shortest time after a bump, improving comfort and control ✓.
(b) At resonance, bump frequency = natural frequency = 1.2 Hz → period T = 1/1.2 ≈ 0.833 s ✓; bump spacing = v × T = 15 × 0.833 = 12.5 m ✓✓ (accept 12–13 m).
Exam question · data analysis
A gong’s sound pressure level L (in dB) is recorded over time. The graph of L vs t is a straight line with equation L = 90 − 2.0t (t in seconds).
(a) Show that the sound intensity halves every 1.5 s. [2 marks]
(b) Determine the time taken for the intensity to fall to one‑eighth of its initial value. [2 marks]
(c) Discuss how an uncertainty of ±1 dB in each measurement affects the gradient uncertainty. [2 marks]
(a) A drop of 3 dB halves the intensity. Slope = 2.0 dB s⁻¹, so time for 3 dB drop = 3/2.0 = 1.5 s ✓✓.
(b) One‑eighth = 3 half‑lives (1/2 → 1/4 → 1/8) ✓ → 3 × 1.5 s = 4.5 s ✓.
(c) Error bars of ±1 dB allow a range of possible slopes; max slope from worst‑case steep line, min slope from shallow line; gradient uncertainty = (max – min)/2; this propagates to an uncertainty in the half‑life ✓✓.
⚠️ Don’t confuse amplitude and intensity: A 3 dB drop halves the intensity (power per area). The amplitude is proportional to the square root of intensity, so it decreases by a factor of √2.
