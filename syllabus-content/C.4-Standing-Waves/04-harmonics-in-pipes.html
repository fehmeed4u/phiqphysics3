Harmonics in Pipes
Standing wave patterns in open and closed pipes — boundary conditions, harmonic series, and resonant frequencies

🎵 The Big Question

Why does your voice sound like a chipmunk when you breathe in helium, but like Darth Vader with sulfur hexafluoride? The speed of sound changes — but your vocal tract (a pipe!) stays the same length. The resonant frequencies shift because f = v / λ, and only v has changed.

This lesson is about how the length of a pipe and the conditions at its ends determine which standing wave patterns — and therefore which frequencies — can exist inside it.

Fig 1 — A flute is open at both ends (all harmonics), while a panpipe is closed at one end (odd harmonics only). The closed-end instrument produces a characteristically "hollow" timbre.
Core knowledge — boundary conditions

Key Definitions

Node (N)A point of zero displacement amplitude — air molecules are stationary. Always found at a closed end.
Antinode (A)A point of maximum displacement amplitude — air molecules oscillate with greatest amplitude. Always found at an open end.
HarmonicA standing wave pattern whose frequency is an integer multiple of the fundamental frequency.
Fundamental (f₁)The lowest possible resonant frequency that fits the boundary conditions of the pipe. Unit: Hz.

Why Do Pipes Resonate?

A sound wave travels down a pipe, reflects off the ends, and travels back. For a loud, sustained sound (resonance), the reflected wave must constructively interfere with the incoming wave — forming a standing wave. Whether this "fit" works depends entirely on the boundary conditions at each end:

Closed end → a rigid wall. Air molecules cannot move freely here → displacement node (N).

Open end → air is free to move. Molecules oscillate with maximum amplitude → displacement antinode (A).

⚠️ The sine-curve diagram inside a pipe represents displacement amplitude vs position — not a transverse wave. Sound in a pipe is longitudinal. The curve is a graph, not a photograph of the wave shape.
Fig 2 — A tuning fork vibrates over a tube partly filled with water. As the tube is raised, the effective air column lengthens and resonance occurs at specific lengths where a standing wave fits.
Fig 3 — At a node, molecules are stationary (zero displacement). At an antinode, molecules oscillate back and forth with maximum amplitude. Closed ends enforce nodes; open ends enforce antinodes.
The big relationship — fitting waves into pipes

Building the Logic Step by Step

The pipe has a fixed length L. We must fit a standing wave between the boundary conditions (nodes and/or antinodes) at each end. Two key distances govern how standing waves fit:

The distance between two adjacent nodes (or two adjacent antinodes) is λ/2. The distance between a node and the nearest antinode is λ/4.

From these two facts, every formula in this topic follows.

Open–Open Pipe (e.g. Flute)

Both ends are open → antinode at each end. The simplest pattern that fits is one half-wavelength: A—N—A.

The general condition is:

L = n (λ / 2)   where n = 1, 2, 3 …

Rearranging for frequency:

fₙ = n v / (2L)

All harmonics (n = 1, 2, 3 …) are present.

Closed–Open Pipe (e.g. Clarinet)

One end closed (node), one end open (antinode). The simplest pattern is one quarter-wavelength: N—A.

The general condition is:

L = n (λ / 4)   where n = 1, 3, 5 … (odd only)

Rearranging for frequency:

fₙ = n v / (4L)   (n odd only)

Only odd harmonics (n = 1, 3, 5 …) are present.

💡 Why does a closed pipe sound "hollower"? The missing even harmonics remove a large portion of the overtone series, producing a thinner, more hollow timbre compared to an open pipe of the same length.
Fig 4 — Open–open pipe: the first four harmonics (n = 1, 2, 3, 4). Antinodes at both ends. Each successive harmonic adds one more node inside the pipe.
Fig 5 — Closed–open pipe: harmonics n = 1, 3, 5. Node at the closed end, antinode at the open end. Even harmonics (n = 2, 4, …) cannot satisfy these boundary conditions and are absent.

Proportionality Statements

These are essential for qualitative exam answers:

f ∝ 1/L   ·   f ∝ v   ·   f ∝ n

Longer pipe → lower pitch. Faster sound speed → higher pitch. Higher harmonic number → higher pitch.

fₙ = nv/(2L) fₙ = nv/(4L), n odd Node–Node = λ/2 Node–Antinode = λ/4 f ∝ 1/L f ∝ v

Worked example

Worked Example — Panpipe Closed at One End

A panpipe is closed at one end and has a length of 0.45 m. The speed of sound is 340 m/s. (a) Calculate the fundamental frequency. (b) Calculate the frequency of the next harmonic that will resonate. (c) If the pipe were open at both ends, what would its fundamental frequency be?

1
Identify boundary conditions: Closed at one end → use fₙ = nv / (4L), with n = 1, 3, 5 … (odd only).
2
Fundamental frequency (n = 1): f₁ = (1 × 340) / (4 × 0.45) = 340 / 1.8 = 188.9 Hz
3
Next resonant harmonic: The next odd integer is n = 3. f₃ = 3 × 188.9 = 566.7 Hz (this is the 3rd harmonic, not the "2nd harmonic").
4
Open at both ends: Now use f₁ = v / (2L) = 340 / (2 × 0.45) = 340 / 0.9 = 377.8 Hz. Note this is exactly double the closed-pipe fundamental.
⚠️ Terminology trap: For a closed–open pipe, the "second resonant frequency" is the 3rd harmonic (n = 3), not the 2nd harmonic (n = 2). The 2nd harmonic cannot exist in a closed pipe. Examiners test this distinction frequently.
Common misconceptions — trap corner

Misconceptions to Avoid

MisconceptionCorrect View
"A closed pipe has no nodes"All standing waves have nodes. A closed pipe has a node at the closed end — that is the whole point of the boundary condition.
"A pipe closed at both ends doesn't work"It works fine (e.g. pressurised gas pipes). Both ends are nodes → same boundary type at each end → behaves identically to open–open: L = nλ/2, all harmonics present.
"The 2nd harmonic is always n = 2"For a closed–open pipe, the second possible resonant frequency is the 3rd harmonic (n = 3). Always specify whether you mean "2nd harmonic" or "2nd resonant frequency".
"The wave diagram shows a transverse wave"The sine curve shows displacement amplitude vs position along the pipe. The actual wave is longitudinal — air molecules vibrate parallel to the pipe axis.
💡 The helium voice explained: Your vocal tract is a closed–open pipe (vocal cords = closed end, mouth = open end). The resonant wavelengths are set by geometry (λ = 4L/n). In helium, v ≈ 970 m/s instead of 340 m/s, so f = v/λ roughly triples — chipmunk voice. The pipe hasn't changed; only the wave speed has.

Exam-style questions
Exam question · Calculate & Explain
A pipe has a length of 1.32 m and is closed at both ends. (a) Draw the displacement wave pattern for the third harmonic. (b) Calculate the wavelength of this third harmonic. (c) The speed of sound is 330 m/s. Calculate the frequency. [5 marks]
(a) Closed at both ends → node at each end (same as open–open in structure). Third harmonic (n = 3) has 3 half-wavelengths fitting inside the pipe: three "loops" with nodes at both ends and one clear antinode in each loop. ✓✓

(b) Closed–closed behaves as L = nλ/2. For n = 3: 1.32 = 3(λ/2) → λ = (2 × 1.32)/3 = 0.88 m ✓✓

(c) f = v/λ = 330/0.88 = 375 Hz ✓
Exam question · Data Analysis
A student varies the length L of a closed–open pipe and measures the fundamental frequency f. They plot f vs 1/L and obtain a straight line with gradient 82.5 m/s. (a) Explain why the graph is a straight line. (b) Use the gradient to determine the speed of sound. [3 marks]
(a) For the fundamental of a closed–open pipe, f₁ = v/(4L). Rearranging: f₁ = (v/4) × (1/L). This has the form y = mx, so f vs 1/L is a straight line through the origin with gradient v/4. ✓

(b) Gradient = v/4 = 82.5 → v = 4 × 82.5 = 330 m/s ✓✓
Exam question · Explain
A didgeridoo is approximately 1.5 m long and is closed at one end (the player's lips). Explain why the instrument can produce high-pitched sounds despite having a very low fundamental frequency. [3 marks]
The fundamental frequency is low because L is large and f₁ = v/(4L). ✓ However, higher odd harmonics (n = 3, 5, 7, 9 …) can also resonate in the pipe. ✓ Players excite these higher harmonics by adjusting their lip tension and airflow, producing frequencies that are 3×, 5×, 7× etc. the fundamental — which are perceived as high-pitched toots. ✓
