The Simple Pendulum & Mass–Spring System
Deriving the period equations, conditions for SHM, and comparing the two key systems

System 1 — Mass on a spring

Conditions: spring mass negligible, Hooke's law obeyed (spring constant k), air friction negligible, g constant, fixed support.

When displaced x from equilibrium, the restoring force is F = −kx. Applying Newton's second law: ma = −kx, so a = −(k/m)x.

Comparing with a = −ω²x gives:

ω² = k/m   ⟹   T = 2π√(m/k)

T increases with greater mass m (heavier → slower) and decreases with stiffer spring k (stiffer → faster).

The period T of a mass–spring system does NOT depend on amplitude (isochronous) or on gravitational field strength g. It works identically on Earth, the Moon, or in space.
Fig 1 — Mass on a spring. When displaced x from equilibrium, F = −kx always points back towards the mean position.

System 2 — Simple pendulum

Conditions: string mass negligible, air friction negligible, angle of swing < 5° (0.1 rad), g constant, fixed pivot, string length l constant.

The restoring force is the component of gravity along the arc: F = −mg sinθ. For small angles, sinθ ≈ θ = x/l, so F ≈ −mg(x/l) = −(mg/l)x.

This gives a = −(g/l)x, hence:

ω² = g/l   ⟹   T = 2π√(l/g)
The mass of the bob does NOT appear in the formula — it cancels in the derivation. T depends only on l and g.
Fig 2 — Simple pendulum. For small angles, mg sinθ ≈ mgθ, making the restoring force proportional to displacement.
A pendulum released from 30° does NOT undergo SHM. At large angles sinθ ≠ θ, so F is not proportional to x. SHM is only a valid approximation for θ < 5°.

Comparing the two systems

Mass–spring

T = 2π√(m/k)

T ∝ √m (heavier → longer T)
T ∝ 1/√k (stiffer → shorter T)
Independent of g
Works in any orientation

Simple pendulum

T = 2π√(l/g)

T ∝ √l (longer → longer T)
T ∝ 1/√g (stronger g → shorter T)
Independent of mass m
Requires small angle (< 5°)
Both are isochronous (T independent of A) Spring: T depends on m and k Pendulum: T depends on l and g

Worked example

A 600 g mass is attached to a spring with k = 30 N m⁻¹. Calculate the frequency of oscillation.

T = 2π√(m/k) = 2π√(0.6/30) = 2π√(0.02) = 0.889 s
f = 1/T = 1/0.889 ≈ 1.1 Hz

Exam-style questions
Exam question · Mathematical
A 0.250 kg mass is suspended from a spring of spring constant 40.0 N m⁻¹ and displaced 0.050 m from equilibrium.

(i) Show that the system undergoes SHM. [2 marks]
(ii) Calculate the period. [2 marks]
(iii) Calculate ω. [1 mark]
(i) Displaced x: net force = kx = 40x toward equilibrium. F ∝ −x ✓ and directed toward mean position ✓ — both SHM conditions met.

(ii) T = 2π√(m/k) = 2π√(0.250/40.0) = 2π × 0.0791 = 0.497 s ≈ 0.50 s ✓✓

(iii) ω = 2π/T = 2π/0.497 = 12.6 rad s⁻¹ ✓
Exam question · Reason/Explain
A student argues that a pendulum released from 30° is undergoing SHM because it oscillates. Explain why the student is incorrect. [3 marks]
At 30°, sinθ ≈ 0.500 while θ (in rad) ≈ 0.524. Since sinθ ≠ θ, the restoring force mg sinθ is NOT proportional to the arc displacement x = lθ. ✓ The condition F ∝ −x is not met, so the motion is NOT SHM. ✓ A pendulum approximates SHM only for angles below ~5° (0.1 rad), where sinθ ≈ θ to within ~0.5%. ✓
Exam question · Proportional reasoning (Hard)
A student measures the period of a pendulum on Earth as 2.00 s. They want to design a mass-spring system that has the same period.

(i) Calculate the required pendulum length. [2 marks]
(ii) If the spring constant is 50.0 N m⁻¹, calculate the mass needed for the spring system. [2 marks]
(iii) Explain what happens to each system's period if both are taken to the Moon (gMoon = 1.62 m s⁻²). [2 marks]
(i) T = 2π√(l/g) ⟹ l = gT²/(4π²) = 9.81 × 4.00/(4π²) = 0.994 m ≈ 0.99 m ✓✓

(ii) T = 2π√(m/k) ⟹ m = kT²/(4π²) = 50.0 × 4.00/(4π²) = 5.07 kg ✓✓

(iii) The pendulum period increases because T ∝ 1/√g; new T = 2.00 × √(9.81/1.62) = 4.92 s. ✓ The spring system period is unchanged — T = 2π√(m/k) does not involve g. ✓
