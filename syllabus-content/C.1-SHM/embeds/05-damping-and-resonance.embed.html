Damping & Resonance
Damped oscillations, forced oscillations, natural frequency, and the resonance phenomenon

What is damping?

Damping is the progressive loss of energy from an oscillating system due to resistive forces (friction, air resistance, viscous drag). The amplitude gradually decreases over time, and the oscillation eventually ceases.

In a damped system, total mechanical energy is no longer conserved — it is dissipated as thermal energy (heat) in the surroundings.

Damping reduces the amplitude of oscillation but does NOT significantly change the frequency (for light damping). The system still oscillates near its natural frequency.

Three regimes of damping

Underdamped

Amplitude decreases gradually over many cycles.

The system oscillates with an exponentially decaying envelope.

Example: a pendulum in air.

Critically damped

System returns to equilibrium in the shortest possible time without oscillating.

No overshoot past equilibrium.

Example: car shock absorbers.

Overdamped

System returns to equilibrium slowly without oscillating.

Takes longer than critical damping.

Example: door closer mechanism.
Underdamped: oscillates, amplitude decays Critical: fastest return, no oscillation Overdamped: slow return, no oscillation
Fig 1 — The three regimes of damping. Underdamped: amplitude decays exponentially over many cycles. Critically damped: fastest return to equilibrium without overshoot. Overdamped: slow return, no oscillation.
In IB exams, you should be able to sketch all three damping curves on the same displacement–time graph. Underdamped shows decaying sinusoidal oscillation; critically damped shows the fastest smooth return to zero; overdamped shows an even slower return.

Natural frequency

Every oscillating system has a natural frequency f₀ — the frequency at which it oscillates freely when displaced and released with no driving force and no damping.

f₀ = 1/(2π) × √(k/m)    (mass-spring)     f₀ = 1/(2π) × √(g/l)    (pendulum)

The natural frequency is determined only by the physical properties of the system (mass, spring constant, length, etc.), not by how it is driven or damped.

Forced oscillations

A forced oscillation occurs when an external periodic force is applied to a system. The system is driven at a driving frequency fd which may differ from its natural frequency f₀.

After initial transients die away, the system oscillates at the driving frequency fd, not its natural frequency. The amplitude of the steady-state oscillation depends on how close fd is to f₀.

Resonance

Resonance occurs when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency of the system:

fd = f₀   ⟹   Resonance (maximum amplitude)

At resonance, energy transfer from driver to oscillator is most efficient. The amplitude reaches a maximum, limited only by damping. In the absence of damping, the amplitude would theoretically grow without bound.

At resonance, the driving force is exactly π/2 radians (90°) out of phase with the displacement. This means the force is always in the direction of the velocity, maximizing power transfer.

The resonance curve

A graph of amplitude vs driving frequency shows a characteristic resonance peak:

FeatureLight dampingHeavy damping
Peak amplitudeTall and sharpLow and broad
Peak frequencyVery close to f₀Slightly below f₀
BandwidthNarrowWide
Q factorHighLow

As damping increases, the resonance peak becomes broader and lower, and the peak shifts slightly below f₀. With very heavy damping, resonance effectively disappears.

Light damping → sharp peak Heavy damping → broad, low peak No damping → infinite amplitude (theory)

Phase relationship in forced oscillations

ConditionPhase difference (driver vs displacement)
fd ≪ f₀≈ 0 (in phase — system follows the driver)
fd = f₀ (resonance)π/2 (90° — driver leads displacement by quarter cycle)
fd ≫ f₀≈ π (antiphase — system opposes the driver)
The phase changes most rapidly near resonance. This smooth transition from 0 to π (through π/2 at resonance) is a key feature of the IB syllabus.

Exam-style questions
Exam question · Theory / Explain
(a) Define the terms natural frequency and resonance. [2 marks]

(b) Explain why the amplitude at resonance is limited in real systems. [2 marks]
(a) Natural frequency: the frequency at which a system oscillates freely when displaced from equilibrium, with no external driving force and no damping. ✓ Resonance: the condition in which the driving frequency equals the natural frequency, resulting in maximum amplitude of oscillation. ✓

(b) In real systems, damping forces (friction, air resistance) dissipate energy. ✓ At resonance, energy input from the driver equals energy dissipated by damping — this limits the amplitude to a finite maximum. Without damping, the amplitude would increase indefinitely. ✓
Exam question · Graph sketch
Sketch on the same axes the displacement–time graphs for:
(i) an underdamped oscillation,
(ii) a critically damped system, and
(iii) an overdamped system.
All three are released from the same initial displacement. Label each curve clearly. [4 marks]
(i) Underdamped: sinusoidal oscillation with exponentially decreasing amplitude envelope. The system passes through equilibrium multiple times before coming to rest. ✓

(ii) Critically damped: the system returns to equilibrium as fast as possible without crossing the equilibrium line (no overshoot). The curve approaches x = 0 smoothly from above. ✓

(iii) Overdamped: the system returns to equilibrium without oscillating, but more slowly than the critically damped case. The curve approaches x = 0 from above but takes longer. ✓

All three start from the same initial displacement. ✓ The underdamped curve should be the only one that crosses x = 0.
Exam question · Application (Hard)
A child sits on a swing and is pushed periodically by a parent. The natural frequency of the swing is 0.40 Hz.

(i) The parent pushes with a frequency of 0.40 Hz. Describe what happens to the amplitude over several minutes. [2 marks]
(ii) The parent increases the push frequency to 0.80 Hz. Predict and explain the effect on the swing's amplitude. [2 marks]
(iii) Explain, using energy concepts, why the swing's amplitude does not grow infinitely even when pushed at f₀. [2 marks]
(i) The push frequency matches the natural frequency — this is resonance. The amplitude increases progressively as energy is transferred efficiently from parent to swing. ✓ The amplitude continues to grow until energy input per cycle equals energy lost to damping per cycle. ✓

(ii) At 0.80 Hz (2f₀), the driving frequency is far from f₀. The energy transfer is inefficient, so the swing oscillates at 0.80 Hz but with much smaller amplitude than at resonance. ✓ The system is being forced to oscillate at a non-resonant frequency. ✓

(iii) Energy is lost each cycle due to air resistance and friction at the pivot. At steady state, the energy input from each push exactly equals the energy dissipated per cycle. ✓ Since energy loss increases with amplitude (larger velocity → greater air resistance), an equilibrium amplitude is reached. ✓
