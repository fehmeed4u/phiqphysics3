Coulomb's Law
Electric charge comes in two types, always adds up, and always comes in whole multiples of one tiny unit. The force between two charges follows an inverse-square law strikingly similar to gravity — but it can push as well as pull.

🎯 Hook – hair that stands on end

Brush dry hair with a plastic brush and individual hairs move apart, standing away from each other and from the brush. Electrons transfer from the hair to the brush: the brush becomes negatively charged, the hair positively charged. The two are then attracted to each other, while hairs with the same charge repel.

Fig 1 — Brushing hair transfers electrons to the brush. The brush becomes negative, the hair positive, so they attract each other while similarly-charged hairs repel.
💡 Diagnostic question (think – then read on) — no electrons are created or destroyed when the brush charges the hair. So where did the brush's negative charge come from? It came from the hair. The hair lost exactly as many electrons as the brush gained — total charge is conserved.

⚡ Electric charge

Charge is measured in coulombs, C — a large unit, so microcoulombs and nanocoulombs are common:

$$1\,\mu\text{C} = 10^{-6}\,\text{C} \qquad 1\,\text{nC} = 10^{-9}\,\text{C}$$

Every proton carries charge \(+1.60\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}\); every electron carries \(-1.60\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}\). Any quantity of charge is a whole number of these particles, each \(\pm1.6\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}\) — charge is quantized.

Elementary charge, e $$e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}$$
🔗 Linking question — charge is quantized. Which other physical quantities are quantized? (This links to understandings in Topic E.2.)

Nuclear particles (protons, neutrons) are themselves built from quarks, whose charge is quantized in multiples of \(\pm e/3\) — but quarks cannot be observed as isolated particles, and are not required in this course.

Everyday objects have equal numbers of protons and electrons and are neutral. Electrons are the outermost, loosely-bound particles in atoms; protons sit fixed in the nucleus and cannot move. So it is always electrons that are added or removed to charge an object — protons never move.

🔌 Charging by friction and by contact

When friction occurs between two surfaces, electrons often transfer between them: one surface gains electrons (negative), the other loses them (positive). Insulating rods rubbed with cloths are the standard classroom example — insulators keep the charge where it lands, whereas free electrons in a metal would move around and neutralize it. Friction charging isn't limited to solids: it also occurs when liquids flow through pipes or air flows past fans.

Fig 2 — A classroom Van de Graaff generator uses friction to build up very high voltages. The largest, at the Boston Museum of Science, has two 4.5 m spheres on 7.6 m insulating poles and can reach seven million volts.

If a charged object physically touches another, electrons can flow between them and the charge is shared — how much depends on size, shape and conductivity. If the rod is removed, the sphere keeps whatever charge it received.

Fig 3 — Charging by contact: electrons flow from the charged rod onto the sphere and remain there once the rod is removed.
🌍 Discharging and grounding — a charged object touched by a conductor connected to the ground quickly loses its charge (this is called discharging): electrons flow to or from it while the effect on the ground itself is insignificant. Deliberately connecting an object to earth to remove its charge is grounding (earthing), keeping the object at 0 V. Charged objects also discharge through humid air. Domestic wiring is earthed with a thick copper wire to a buried metal plate or a metal water pipe.

🧲 Charging by electrostatic induction

Bring a charged object close to a neutral one without touching and it can still exert a force on the free electrons inside. A positive rod attracts electrons towards it, leaving the near side of the neutral object negative and the far side positive (a negative rod does the reverse). This charge separation without contact is electrostatic induction.

Fig 4 — A nearby positive rod attracts electrons to the near side of the sphere, leaving the far side positive.
Fig 5 — A charged comb attracts uncharged paper by induction: the paper's near side becomes oppositely charged, and attraction wins over no contact ever being made.

Induction is also the neatest way to charge an object for an experiment, because it doesn't rely on sharing charge:

Fig 6 — Charging by induction: (1) the rod separates charge on the sphere, (2) grounding lets electrons flow onto the sphere, (3) the ground connection is removed while the rod is still close, trapping the charge, (4) removing the rod leaves the sphere with an induced charge — a negative rod produces a positively charged sphere.
⚠️ Common mistake — do not confuse electrostatic induction (described here) with electromagnetic induction, a completely different effect covered in Topic D.4.

✈️ Dangers of static electricity

Large-scale electrostatic effects can be dangerous. Lightning is the obvious example. Vehicles and aircraft become charged simply by moving through air or along the ground, and a spark during refuelling could ignite the fuel-air mixture. The risk is prevented by grounding both the vehicle and the fuel supply.

Fig 7 — Aircraft refuelling: a bonding cable keeps the truck and aircraft at the same potential, and a grounding cable earths the aircraft, preventing a spark.

📐 Coulomb's law

The force between two point charges follows an inverse square law, mathematically similar to Newton's law of gravitation (Topic D.1):

Coulomb's law — separation r, charges q₁ and q₂ $$F = k\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}$$

Charged bodies that are spherical behave as point charges located at their centres, so they also obey Coulomb's law. The law was first published by Charles Augustin de Coulomb in 1783.

Fig 8 — Halving the separation between two charges quarters the force needed to explain it; doubling the separation reduces the force to a quarter. Force falls off as \(1/r^2\).

Same-sign charges (both positive or both negative) give a positive \(F\) — a repulsive force. Opposite charges give a negative \(F\) — an attractive force.

Coulomb constant $$k = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\,\text{N m}^2\text{C}^{-2}$$

Unlike the gravitational constant \(G\), \(k\) is not itself fundamental — it can be broken down further. \(\epsilon_0\) is the permittivity of free space, a fundamental constant representing the ability of a vacuum to transfer an electric force and field:

$$\epsilon_0 = 8.85\times10^{-12}\,\text{C}^2\text{N}^{-1}\text{m}^{-2}$$

🧪 Permittivity and other media

Every medium has its own permittivity, \(\epsilon\), always greater than \(\epsilon_0\) — although dry air is very close to free space. Replacing air with another medium therefore reduces the force between two given charges. The relative permittivity (sometimes called the dielectric constant) is the ratio:

Relative permittivity (no units) $$\epsilon_r = \frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon_0}$$

For example, a rubber with \(\epsilon = 4.83\times10^{-11}\,\text{C}^2\text{N}^{-1}\text{m}^{-2}\) has \(\epsilon_r = 4.83\times10^{-11}/8.85\times10^{-12} = 5.46\).

✍️ Worked example D2.1

A point charge of \(4.5\times10^{-8}\,\text{C}\) is situated in air 3.2 cm from another charge of \(-1.3\times10^{-7}\,\text{C}\). a) Determine the electrical force between them. b) If they were separated by polythene instead, calculate the approximate force.

1
Identify: two point charges in air — use Coulomb's law directly, then adjust for the medium in part (b).
2
Data: \(q_1 = 4.5\times10^{-8}\ \text{C}\), \(q_2 = -1.3\times10^{-7}\ \text{C}\), \(r = 3.2\times10^{-2}\ \text{m}\), \(k = 8.99\times10^9\ \text{N m}^2\text{C}^{-2}\).
3
Equation: $$F = k\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}$$ $$F = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(4.5\times10^{-8})(-1.3\times10^{-7})}{(3.2\times10^{-2})^2}$$
4
Answer: \(F = -5.1\times10^{-2}\ \text{N}\). The negative sign shows the force is attractive (opposite charges).
b) Polythene has \(\epsilon_r \approx 2\), so the force is divided by about 2: \(F \approx -3\times10^{-2}\ \text{N}\).

🌍 Real-world: measuring tiny forces

Coulomb's law was first verified with a torsion balance — a horizontal rod suspended by a thin fibre, twisting as one charged sphere repelled or attracted another. The force between everyday charges is often far too small to feel, which is exactly why Coulomb needed such a sensitive instrument.

✓ Examiner tip: always substitute the sign of each charge into \(F = kq_1q_2/r^2\) and let the sign of the answer tell you attraction or repulsion — don't decide the sign by inspection first and then risk contradicting your own arithmetic.

🚀 HL extension – vector form

Coulomb's law as given is a magnitude. For a full vector treatment, the force on \(q_2\) due to \(q_1\) points along the line joining them, away from \(q_1\) if the charges have the same sign and towards \(q_1\) if they are opposite — exactly the pattern in Fig D2.11's inverse-square diagram, but now carrying a direction as well as a size.

Challenge: three point charges lie on a straight line. Why is it not generally true that the net force on the middle charge is zero, even if the two outer charges are equal in magnitude? (Answer: it depends on their signs and on the middle charge's exact position — equal magnitudes at unequal distances give unequal force magnitudes, and the two forces may add rather than cancel if the signs make both forces act in the same direction.)

Exam‑style questions
Exam question · Describe
Q1. Describe the movement of electrons that causes a plastic rod to become positively charged when it is rubbed with a dry cloth. [2 marks]
✓ Friction between the rod and the cloth transfers electrons between their surfaces.
✓ Electrons move from the rod to the cloth, leaving the rod with a deficit of electrons and therefore an overall positive charge.
Exam question · Describe and explain
Q2. Describe and explain an electrostatic effect that you have seen in your home. [2 marks]
✓ Any credible everyday example, e.g. a woollen jumper crackling and clothes clinging together after being removed from a tumble dryer.
✓ Explanation in terms of friction transferring electrons between surfaces, leaving them oppositely charged and so attracted to each other.
Exam question · Determine
Q3. Two conducting spheres have charges of \(-20.0\,\mu\text{C}\) and \(+6.0\,\mu\text{C}\). If the spheres are identical and come briefly into contact, determine the charge on each sphere when they are separated. [2 marks]
✓ Total charge is conserved: \(-20.0 + 6.0 = -14.0\,\mu\text{C}\).
✓ Identical spheres share the charge equally, so each ends up with \(-7.0\,\mu\text{C}\).
Exam question · Explain
Q4. Given two conducting spheres on insulating stands (as in the earlier figures), explain how you could make one positively charged and the other negatively charged by using a negatively charged plastic rod. [3 marks]
✓ Bring the two spheres together, touching, and hold the negatively charged rod close to one of them without touching.
✓ Electrons in the spheres are repelled by the rod towards the far sphere, so the far sphere becomes negatively charged and the near sphere positively charged (electrostatic induction).
✓ Separate the two spheres while the rod is still in place, then remove the rod — each sphere keeps its induced charge.
Exam question · Explain / Outline / Suggest
Q5. a) Explain how an electrostatic effect could be dangerous when refuelling an aircraft. b) Explain how grounding can prevent the problem arising. c) Suggest how sparks could occur at a petrol (gas) station. [4 marks]
✓ a) Friction with the air as the aircraft moves can charge it; a spark discharging that charge near the fuel vapour could ignite it.
✓ b) Bonding the aircraft and fuel tanker together, and grounding both, keeps them at the same potential and lets any charge dissipate safely, so no spark can jump between them.
✓ c) Similarly, static built up on a person, a container, or from fuel flowing through a pipe or nozzle could spark near petrol vapour if it is not grounded.
Exam question · Show that
Q6. Use the value \(\epsilon_0 = 8.85\times10^{-12}\,\text{C}^2\text{N}^{-1}\text{m}^{-2}\) to show that the Coulomb constant has a value of \(8.99\times10^{9}\,\text{N m}^2\text{C}^{-2}\). [2 marks]
✓ \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} = \dfrac{1}{4\pi \times 8.85\times10^{-12}}\)
✓ \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2\text{C}^{-2}\), as required.
Exam question · Calculate
Q7. The force between two identical point charges was 5.0 N when they were separated by 7.6 cm. What were the magnitudes of the charges? [3 marks]
✓ \(F = kq^2/r^2\), so \(q^2 = Fr^2/k = \dfrac{5.0\times(0.076)^2}{8.99\times10^9}\)
✓ \(q^2 = 3.21\times10^{-12}\)
✓ \(q = 1.8\times10^{-6}\ \text{C}\) (1.8 μC).
Exam question · Determine / State
Q8. The surfaces of two insulated conducting spheres were separated by 14.0 cm. One sphere had a radius of 2.7 cm and a charge of \(3.6\times10^{-7}\) C. The other had a radius of 3.9 cm and a charge of \(-4.8\times10^{-7}\) C. a) Determine the force between the spheres in magnitude and direction. b) State any assumption you made in a). [4 marks]
✓ Centre-to-centre separation \(r = 14.0 + 2.7 + 3.9 = 20.6\ \text{cm} = 0.206\ \text{m}\).
✓ \(F = k\dfrac{q_1q_2}{r^2} = \dfrac{(8.99\times10^9)(3.6\times10^{-7})(-4.8\times10^{-7})}{(0.206)^2}\)
✓ \(F = -3.7\times10^{-2}\ \text{N}\), i.e. \(3.7\times10^{-2}\) N, attractive (opposite charges).
✓ b) Assumed each sphere behaves as a point charge located at its centre.
Exam question · Calculate
Q9. Calculate the force between two point charges of 7.4 C and 2.2 C which are separated by 1.2 m in a nonconducting liquid of relative permittivity 3.1. [3 marks]
✓ Force in vacuum: \(F_0 = k\dfrac{q_1q_2}{r^2} = \dfrac{(8.99\times10^9)(7.4)(2.2)}{(1.2)^2} = 1.02\times10^{11}\ \text{N}\).
✓ In the medium, divide by \(\epsilon_r\): \(F = F_0/\epsilon_r = 1.02\times10^{11}/3.1\).
✓ \(F \approx 3.3\times10^{10}\ \text{N}\) (repulsive, since both charges are positive).
