Electric Field and Strength
Invisible forces shape every bolt of lightning and every circuit — learn to map and measure the electric field that makes it all happen.

⚡ Hook – The invisible map

Sprinkle iron filings near a charged balloon and they shift into sweeping arcs — revealing invisible lines of influence that reach across empty space. Place a second balloon nearby and the pattern warps, stretches, and merges. You're looking at an electric field — the same field that steers electrons through wires, drives lightning to the ground, and holds every atom together. The field is everywhere; charges just make it visible.

Fig 1 — Two like charges repel: their field lines curve away from each other in the overlap region.
💡 Think first — If you placed a tiny positive test charge exactly midway between two equal positive charges, would it feel a net force? No — the two repulsive forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, so they cancel. However, this is an unstable equilibrium; any slight displacement produces a net force away from the midpoint.
Core knowledge

🧲 Electric field lines

An electric field is a region of space in which a charged particle experiences an electric force. We visualise these fields with electric field lines — imaginary curves drawn so that at every point the line is tangent to the direction of force on a positive test charge.

By convention, field lines point away from positive charges and toward negative charges. They never cross each other. Where the lines are packed close together the field is strong; where they spread apart the field is weak.

Key patterns every IB student must recognise: a radial field around an isolated point charge (lines spread outward for positive, inward for negative), a dipole field between equal and opposite charges (lines leave + and curve into −), and a uniform field between parallel plates (equally spaced, straight, parallel lines from + plate to − plate). Field lines are always perpendicular to conducting surfaces — if they weren't, the component of force along the surface would push mobile electrons until equilibrium is restored.

Fig 2 — Three fundamental electric field patterns: positive point charge, negative point charge, and electric dipole.
ℹ️ On a charged conducting sphere, all excess charge sits on the outer surface. The field outside is identical to that of a point charge at the centre; the field inside is zero.

📐 Electric field strength

Electric field strength \(E\) is defined as the force per unit positive test charge at a point. It is a vector quantity whose direction is the direction of force on a positive test charge.

Electric field strength $$E = \dfrac{F}{q}$$

The SI unit is N C⁻¹. For the special case of the uniform field between parallel metal plates separated by distance \(d\) with potential difference \(V\) across them, energy conservation gives \(W = Fd = Vq\), and rearranging yields:

Uniform field between parallel plates $$E = \dfrac{V}{d}$$

This shows that V m⁻¹ is an equivalent unit for electric field strength. The field is constant in magnitude and direction everywhere between the plates (ignoring edge effects).

The electronvolt (eV) is a convenient energy unit at atomic scales. A charge \(e\) accelerated through 1 V gains exactly 1 eV of kinetic energy — so an electron accelerated through 3000 V gains 3000 eV, without further calculation needed.

Electronvolt $$1\;\text{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\;\text{J}$$
Fig 3 — Uniform electric field between parallel plates: straight, equally spaced field lines from + to −.
✓ Examiner tip: When a question gives voltage and plate separation, jump straight to \(E = V/d\) — there's no need to know the charge. This saves time and avoids errors.

🔬 Point charge field & combining fields

Coulomb's law and the definition \(E = F/q\) combine to give the field strength at distance \(r\) from a point charge \(q\):

Field due to a point charge $$E = \dfrac{kq}{r^{2}}$$

This is an inverse-square law: double the distance and the field drops to one quarter. The field points radially outward for a positive source charge and radially inward for a negative one.

Because electric field strength is a vector, fields from separate charges are combined by vector addition. On the line joining two charges this reduces to adding or subtracting magnitudes (with careful attention to direction). At off-axis points, use the parallelogram rule or resolve into components:

Resultant of perpendicular fields $$E_{\text{R}} = \sqrt{E_{1}^{2} + E_{2}^{2}} \qquad \theta = \arctan\!\left(\dfrac{E_{2}}{E_{1}}\right)$$
Fig 4 — Combining electric fields: the parallelogram rule gives the resultant \(E_{\text{R}}\) at point P.
⚠️ When combining fields between two charges, direction matters. Between two positive charges the individual fields point in opposite directions — subtract magnitudes. Between a positive and a negative charge the fields point the same way — add them.

Worked example

✍️ Worked example – Field and energy between parallel plates

1
Identify: We need the electric field strength between parallel plates and the kinetic energy gained by an electron crossing them. This is a uniform-field problem using \(E = V/d\) and \(W = qV\).
2
Data: Plate separation \(d = 8.0\;\text{cm} = 0.080\;\text{m}\). Potential difference \(V = 15\;\text{kV} = 15\,000\;\text{V}\). Electron charge \(e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\;\text{C}\).
3
Equation: Electric field strength: \(E = \dfrac{V}{d} = \dfrac{15\,000}{0.080} = 1.875 \times 10^{5}\;\text{V m}^{-1}\). Energy gained in eV: an electron accelerated through 15 000 V gains 15 000 eV = 15 keV. Energy in joules: \(W = qV = (1.60 \times 10^{-19})(15\,000) = 2.4 \times 10^{-15}\;\text{J}\).
4
Answer: The field strength is \(1.9 \times 10^{5}\;\text{V m}^{-1}\) (2 s.f.) and the electron gains 15 keV (= \(2.4 \times 10^{-15}\;\text{J}\)) of kinetic energy. The electronvolt shortcut eliminates multiplication when the charge is \(e\).

Applications & extensions

⚡ Real-world: Electrostatic precipitators

Inside coal-fired power stations, flue gases pass between charged plates that create strong, uniform electric fields. Tiny soot and ash particles pick up charge and are driven to the collecting plates, removing up to 99% of particulate pollution before gases reach the chimney. The physics is exactly \(E = V/d\) scaled up to industrial voltages.

✓ Examiner tip: If asked to "explain" how a device works, state the field pattern first, then link force to charge using \(F = qE\) — this earns the "link" mark.

🚀 HL Extension – Millikan's oil drop experiment

By balancing gravitational force on a tiny charged oil drop against the upward electric force between horizontal parallel plates, Millikan showed that charge is quantised in multiples of \(e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\;\text{C}\). At equilibrium: \(qE = mg\), so \(q = mg/E\). Every measured charge was an integer multiple of \(e\).

Challenge: An oil drop of mass \(9.79 \times 10^{-15}\;\text{kg}\) is suspended between plates separated by 5.0 mm with a p.d. of 306 V. How many excess electrons does the drop carry? (Answer: \(q = mgd/V = 1.57 \times 10^{-18}\;\text{C}\); \(n = q/e \approx 10\) electrons.)

Trap corner

⚠️ Trap corner – common mistakes

MisconceptionCorrect view
"Field lines are the paths charges follow." Field lines show the direction of force (and therefore acceleration), not the trajectory. A moving charge can cross field lines.
"\(E = V/d\) works for any electric field." This formula applies only to the uniform field between parallel plates. For point charges, use \(E = kq/r^2\).
"Doubling the distance halves the field." The field follows an inverse-square law: doubling \(r\) reduces \(E\) to one quarter, not one half.
"The electronvolt is a unit of voltage." The electronvolt is a unit of energy (\(1\;\text{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\;\text{J}\)). It applies to any particle, not just electrons.
⚠️ Most common slip: Forgetting to convert cm to m before substituting into \(E = V/d\) or \(E = kq/r^2\). This produces answers wrong by factors of 100 or 10 000 — an instant giveaway and zero marks for the numerical answer.

Exam question · Calculate
Point charges of +26 nC and −10 nC are separated by 50 cm.
(a) Calculate the electric field strength at the midpoint between them. [3]
(b) State the direction of the resultant field at that midpoint. [1]
(c) Explain why there is no point between the charges where the field strength is zero. [1]
[5 marks]
(a) Midpoint is 0.25 m from each charge.
\(E_1 = kq_1/r^2 = (8.99 \times 10^{9})(26 \times 10^{-9}) / 0.25^2 = 3740\;\text{N C}^{-1}\) ✓
\(E_2 = kq_2/r^2 = (8.99 \times 10^{9})(10 \times 10^{-9}) / 0.25^2 = 1440\;\text{N C}^{-1}\) ✓
Both fields point in the same direction (from + toward −), so \(E_{\text{total}} = 3740 + 1440 \approx 5200\;\text{N C}^{-1}\) ✓

(b) Directed from the positive charge toward the negative charge. ✓

(c) Between opposite charges, both individual fields point in the same direction (both toward the negative charge), so they always add rather than cancel. ✓
