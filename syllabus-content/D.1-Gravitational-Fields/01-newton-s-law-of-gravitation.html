Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation
Why does the Moon stay in orbit instead of flying away? The same force that pulls an apple to the ground reaches across 384 000 km of space — and its secret is an inverse‑square law.

🍎 Hook – the apple and the Moon

In 1666, Isaac Newton watched an apple fall from a tree in his garden. He asked a revolutionary question: if gravity reaches to the top of the tallest tree, could it reach all the way to the Moon? He realised the Moon is constantly "falling" towards Earth — but because it also moves sideways, it keeps missing and stays in orbit. The same force that makes an apple drop with an acceleration of \(g \approx 9.81\ \text{m s}^{-2}\) also provides the centripetal acceleration that keeps the Moon in its circular path.

Fig 1 — Newton's insight: the force pulling an apple to the ground is the same force that keeps the Moon in orbit around Earth.
💡 Diagnostic question (think – then click) — The Moon is about 60 Earth radii away from us. If gravity follows an inverse‑square law, by what factor is the gravitational acceleration at the Moon's distance weaker than at Earth's surface? Answer: \(1/60^2 = 1/3600\), so \(a_{\text{Moon}} \approx g/3600 \approx 2.7 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m s}^{-2}\).

📐 Newton's universal law of gravitation

Every point mass attracts every other point mass with a force that is:

• Proportional to the product of the masses: \(F \propto m_1 \times m_2\)
• Inversely proportional to the square of their separation: \(F \propto 1/r^2\)
• Always attractive — it acts along the line joining the centres of the two masses.

Newton's universal law of gravitation $$F = G\,\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$$

where \(G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2\text{ kg}^{-2}\) is the universal gravitational constant. The small value of \(G\) means gravitational forces are tiny unless at least one mass is enormous (like a planet).

Fig 2 — Gravitational forces between two point masses: equal magnitude, opposite direction (Newton's third law). The force on \(m_1\) is exactly the same size as the force on \(m_2\), even if the masses are different.

🌍 When can we treat extended objects as point masses?

The formula \(F = G m_1 m_2 / r^2\) applies strictly to point masses. However, it also works perfectly for real objects under two conditions:

1. The objects are uniform spheres — then all their mass can be considered concentrated at their centres.
2. The separation is much larger than the objects' own dimensions.

For a book on Earth's surface, the distance \(r\) is measured from the book to the centre of the Earth, not the surface. The Earth's radius is \(R_{\text{E}} \approx 6.4 \times 10^6\ \text{m}\).

Fig 3 — For a uniform sphere, all mass can be treated as if it is concentrated at the centre. The distance \(r\) is measured between centres.
Linking G to g — gravitational field strength $$g = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{GM}{r^2}$$

On Earth's surface: \(g = \dfrac{G M_{\text{E}}}{R_{\text{E}}^2} \approx 9.81\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\). This is the equivalence principle: inertial mass (in \(F=ma\)) and gravitational mass (in \(F=GMm/r^2\)) are identical to within 1 part in \(10^{15}\).

✍️ Worked example – gravitational force on a 1.0 kg book

1
Identify: We need the gravitational force between a 1.0 kg book and the Earth. Since the Earth is a uniform sphere, we can treat both as point masses with all mass at their centres. The separation \(r\) is the Earth's radius.
2
Data: \(m_{\text{book}} = 1.0\ \text{kg}\), \(M_{\text{E}} = 6.0 \times 10^{24}\ \text{kg}\), \(R_{\text{E}} = 6.4 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}\), \(G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2\text{ kg}^{-2}\).
3
Equation: \(F = G\,\dfrac{m_{\text{book}}\,M_{\text{E}}}{R_{\text{E}}^2}\). Substitute: \(F = \dfrac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11}) \times 1.0 \times (6.0 \times 10^{24})}{(6.4 \times 10^6)^2}\). Denominator: \((6.4 \times 10^6)^2 = 4.096 \times 10^{13}\). Numerator: \(6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.0 \times 10^{24} = 4.002 \times 10^{14}\). Division: \(4.002 \times 10^{14} / 4.096 \times 10^{13} = 9.77\).
4
Answer: \(F \approx 9.8\ \text{N}\). This is exactly the weight of a 1.0 kg mass — confirming that \(W = mg\) is a special case of Newton's universal law.
Fig 6 — Geometry for the worked example: the distance \(r\) is measured from the book to the centre of the Earth.

🔧 Real-world: "weighing the Earth" – the Cavendish experiment

In 1798, Henry Cavendish used a torsion balance with small and large lead spheres to measure \(G\) directly for the first time. Once \(G\) was known, Earth's mass could be calculated from \(g = GM/R^2\). This was called "weighing the Earth" — one of the most precise experiments of its era.

Fig 7 — Cavendish's torsion balance: small lead masses on a suspended rod are attracted to larger stationary masses, twisting a fine wire. The tiny twist angle reveals \(G\).
✓ Examiner tip: when a question asks about "weighing the Earth", it means determining \(G\) (or \(M_{\text{E}}\)) from a direct force measurement between known masses.

🚀 HL Extension – deriving Kepler's third law

For a small mass \(m\) orbiting a large mass \(M\) in a circular orbit, gravity provides the centripetal force:

$$\frac{G M m}{r^2} = \frac{m v^2}{r} \;\Rightarrow\; v^2 = \frac{GM}{r}$$

With \(v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T}\): \(\left(\dfrac{2\pi r}{T}\right)^2 = \dfrac{GM}{r}\), giving:

$$\frac{r^3}{T^2} = \frac{GM}{4\pi^2} = \text{constant}$$

This is Kepler's third law for circular orbits. Newton showed it is a consequence of the inverse‑square law.
Challenge: Jupiter's moon Io orbits at \(r = 4.22 \times 10^8\) m with \(T = 1.53 \times 10^5\) s. Calculate Jupiter's mass. (Answer: \(M_{\text{J}} \approx 1.90 \times 10^{27}\) kg)

⚠️ Trap corner – common mistakes

MisconceptionCorrect view
"The bigger mass exerts a larger force on the smaller mass."The forces are always equal and opposite (Newton's 3rd law). \(F_{A\text{ on }B} = F_{B\text{ on }A}\).
"\(G\) and \(g\) are the same thing."\(G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2\text{ kg}^{-2}\) is a universal constant. \(g \approx 9.81\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\) is the local gravitational field strength at Earth's surface. They are related by \(g = GM/R^2\).
"Doubling the distance halves the force."Doubling \(r\) makes the force one‑quarter of its original value: \(1/2^2 = 1/4\), not \(1/2\).
"The force becomes zero at a large enough distance."The force approaches zero as \(r \to \infty\) but is never exactly zero at any finite distance.
"\(r\) is measured from the surface of the planet."\(r\) is always measured from the centre of the mass (or between centres for two extended objects).
⚠️ Most common exam slip: using the planet's radius when the question gives altitude above the surface. If a satellite is 300 km above Earth, \(r = R_{\text{E}} + 300\ \text{km} = 6.4 \times 10^6 + 3.0 \times 10^5 = 6.7 \times 10^6\) m. Always add altitude to radius!

Exam‑style questions
Exam question · State & Calculate
(a) State Newton's universal law of gravitation in words. [2 marks]
(b) The Earth (\(5.97 \times 10^{24}\) kg) and the Sun (\(1.99 \times 10^{30}\) kg) are \(1.50 \times 10^{11}\) m apart. Calculate the gravitational force exerted by the Sun on the Earth. [3 marks]
(c) Explain why the Earth can be treated as a point mass in this calculation. [1 mark]
(a) ✓ The force between two point masses is directly proportional to the product of the masses; ✓ and inversely proportional to the square of their separation.
(b) ✓ \(F = G M m / r^2\) ✓ \(= (6.67 \times 10^{-11}) \times (1.99 \times 10^{30}) \times (5.97 \times 10^{24}) / (1.50 \times 10^{11})^2\) ✓ \(= 3.52 \times 10^{22}\) N (accept \(3.5 \times 10^{22}\) N).
(c) ✓ The Earth's radius is negligible compared with the Earth–Sun distance, so all mass can be treated as concentrated at the centre.

Exam question · Explain & Compare
A student says: "The gravitational force on a 10 kg mass is ten times greater than the force on a 1 kg mass at the same location, because gravity pulls harder on bigger masses."
(a) Explain whether the student is correct about the magnitude of the force. [2 marks]
(b) Using the equivalence principle, explain why all objects fall with the same acceleration \(g\) near Earth's surface despite having different masses. [2 marks]
(a) ✓ The student is correct about force magnitude: \(F = G M m / r^2\), so doubling the mass doubles the force. ✓ However, the force on Earth from the mass is exactly the same size (Newton's 3rd law) — the student's phrasing "pulls harder" may imply a one‑way force, which is incorrect.
(b) ✓ By the equivalence principle, inertial mass (\(m\) in \(F = ma\)) and gravitational mass (\(m\) in \(F = G M m / r^2\)) are identical. ✓ So \(a = F/m = (G M m / r^2)/m = G M / r^2 = g\), which is independent of the object's mass. All objects experience the same gravitational acceleration.
