Gravitational Potential
Gravitational potential energy belongs to a particular mass in a particular place. Gravitational potential belongs to the place itself — energy per kilogram, ready for any mass you put there.

🛰️ Hook – three satellites, one number

Three satellites orbit the Earth at the same height, but with different masses: 5000 kg, 2000 kg and 3700 kg. Because they have different masses, they have different gravitational potential energies: -2.90×10¹¹ J, -1.16×10¹¹ J and -2.15×10¹¹ J. Divide each by its own mass and something remarkable happens — every satellite gives the same answer, -5.8×10⁷ J kg⁻¹. That number does not belong to any one satellite. It belongs to the orbit.

Fig 1 — Three satellites, same orbit, different \(E_{\text{p}}\), same \(V_{\text{g}} = -5.8\times10^{7}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\).
💡 Diagnostic question — explain how it is possible for a body to have negative potential energy. Gravitational PE is defined to be zero at infinity. Gravity is attractive, so as a mass is brought from infinity to any finite distance, the gravitational force does positive work on it — energy is released, not supplied. Since it started at zero, its PE at every finite distance must be less than zero, i.e. negative.
🔗 Top tip — the same idea shows up for electrons in atoms and nucleons in nuclei: an attractive force binds them into a confined system, so their energy is also given a negative value, zero being reserved for a free particle at infinite separation.

🧮 From potential energy to potential

For a small mass \(m\) in the field of a much larger mass \(M\), gravitational potential energy is \(E_{\text{p}} = -GMm/r\). Dividing by \(m\) removes the mass of the object being placed in the field, leaving a property of the location alone:

Gravitational potential energy $$E_{\text{p}} = -G\frac{Mm}{r}$$ Gravitational potential $$V_{\text{g}} = \frac{E_{\text{p}}}{m} = -G\frac{M}{r}$$

Gravitational potential at a point is the work done per unit mass (1 kg) in bringing a small test mass from infinity to that point. It is a scalar quantity — it has no direction. Like potential energy, its zero is at infinity, so every value of \(V_{\text{g}}\) is negative (or zero). The SI unit is J kg⁻¹.

Fig 2 — \(E_{\text{p}}\) against \(r\) for 1 kg near Earth. The curve is what you get before dividing by the mass placed in the field.
🛍️ Common mistake — a shop sells many sizes of rice at many prices; the useful figure isn't the price of each packet, but the price per kilogram. Potential energy is the "price of the packet" (depends on the mass you chose); potential is the "price per kg" — the more generally useful number, and the one that lets you compare satellites of different mass in the same orbit.

🎯 Equipotential surfaces

An equipotential surface (or line) connects places that have the same potential. Around a spherical mass \(M\) they are concentric circles (spheres, in 3-D), drawn at equal numerical intervals of potential — which means they must get further apart with distance, since increasing separation of equipotentials indicates a weakening field. Every equipotential line forms a closed loop. A mass moved anywhere along one equipotential, by any path, requires zero net energy input.

Fig 3 — Equipotentials around a spherical mass. Wider spacing = weaker field. The innermost line is most negative.
Fig 4 — A contour map. Lines join points of equal altitude — the same idea as an equipotential line, applied to height instead of \(V_{\text{g}}\).

Contour lines on a map join places of equal height above sea level, which in a uniform field are effectively equipotential lines. Where contours crowd together the ground is steepest, and anything free to move — water in a river — moves perpendicular to them, exactly as a mass moves perpendicular to gravitational equipotentials.

📐 Field lines meet equipotentials at right angles

Equipotential lines and gravitational field lines describe the same field two ways. Field lines are always perpendicular to equipotential lines, and point from higher potential to lower potential. This holds for a radial field around a point mass and equally for the uniform field near a flat surface.

Fig 5 — (a) radial field: field lines point inward, equipotentials are circles. (b) uniform field: field lines point straight down, equipotentials are horizontal.

Because gravitational potential is a scalar (unlike field strength, a vector), the potential from two or more masses at any point is found by simple addition — no components needed.

🌍🌙 Adding potentials: the Earth–Moon system

The scalar nature of potential is most useful with two large masses. Along the line joining the Earth and the Moon, each body contributes its own \(-GM/r\) potential; adding them point by point gives a combined curve that dips towards each body and rises to a local maximum (least negative value) at a point P between them.

Fig 6 — Potentials in the Earth–Moon system. The blue curve is the simple sum of the two individual \(-GM/r\) curves.
🔎 Significance of point P — P is where the combined potential has a turning point (a local maximum, i.e. least negative). Because \(g = -\Delta V_{\text{g}}/\Delta r\), a turning point in \(V_{\text{g}}\) means the gradient is momentarily zero there: P is the point where the Earth's and the Moon's pulls exactly cancel, the neutral point of the combined field.

⚡ Potential difference and the work equation

Gravitational potential difference, \(\Delta V_{\text{g}}\), is the work done per unit mass moving between two points in a field:

$$\Delta V_{\text{g}} = \frac{W}{m}$$ $$W = m\Delta V_{\text{g}}$$

Moving to a greater (less negative) potential — away from a planet — needs work done on the mass, so \(W\) is positive. Moving to a lower (more negative) potential — falling towards a planet — the mass does the work itself, so \(W\) is negative, and gravitational PE converts to kinetic energy. This mirrors electric potential difference from Topic B.5, \(V = W/q\), which will return in Topic D.2.

Fig 7 — Moving to a higher orbit needs work done on the satellite; moving to a lower orbit, the satellite does the work. The result does not depend on the path taken.

✍️ Worked example 1 – potential energy on Earth's surface (D1.10)

Confirm that the gravitational potential energy of 1.0 kg on the Earth's surface is -6.3×10⁷ J. Mass of Earth = 6.0×10²⁴ kg, radius of Earth = 6.4×10⁶ m.

1
Identify: use \(E_{\text{p}} = -GMm/r\) with \(r\) = Earth's radius, since the mass sits on the surface.
2
Data: \(G = 6.67\times10^{-11}\), \(M = 6.0\times10^{24}\ \text{kg}\), \(m = 1.0\ \text{kg}\), \(r = 6.4\times10^{6}\ \text{m}\).
3
Equation: $$E_{\text{p}} = -G\frac{Mm}{r}$$ $$= -\frac{(6.67\times10^{-11})(6.0\times10^{24})(1.0)}{6.4\times10^{6}}$$
4
Answer: \(E_{\text{p}} = -6.3\times10^{7}\ \text{J}\), as given.

✍️ Worked example 2 – reading Fig 1's orbit (D1.11)

Using Fig 1: (a) a 4250 kg satellite is placed in the same orbit — calculate its gravitational potential energy. (b) Determine the height of the orbit above Earth's surface. (Mass of Earth = 6.0×10²⁴ kg, radius of Earth = 6.4×10⁶ m.)

1
Identify: (a) gravitational potential energy = potential × mass. (b) rearrange \(V_{\text{g}} = -GM/r\) for \(r\), then subtract Earth's radius.
2
Data: \(V_{\text{g}} = -5.8\times10^{7}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\), \(m = 4250\ \text{kg}\).
3
Equation: $$E_{\text{p}} = V_{\text{g}}m = -(5.8\times10^{7})(4250)$$ $$-5.8\times10^{7} = -\frac{(6.67\times10^{-11})(6.0\times10^{24})}{r}$$
4
Answer: (a) \(E_{\text{p}} = -2.5\times10^{11}\ \text{J}\). (b) \(r = 6.9\times10^{6}\ \text{m}\), so height \(= (6.9\times10^{6}) - (6.4\times10^{6}) = 5\times10^{5}\ \text{m}\) (500 km).

✍️ Worked example 3 – changing orbit (D1.12)

A satellite of mass 850 kg is in an orbit 7.9×10⁶ m from Earth's centre. (a) Calculate the gravitational potential in this orbit. (b) It is moved to an orbit where \(V_{\text{g}} = -5.40\times10^{7}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\); state whether this is a higher or lower orbit. (c) Calculate the work done in this change of orbit.

1
Identify: (a) \(V_{\text{g}} = -GM/r\). (b) compare magnitudes: more negative = lower orbit. (c) \(W = m\Delta V_{\text{g}}\).
2
Data: \(m = 850\ \text{kg}\), \(r = 7.9\times10^{6}\ \text{m}\), new \(V_{\text{g}} = -5.40\times10^{7}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\).
3
Equation: $$V = -G\frac{M}{r} = -\frac{(6.67\times10^{-11})(6.0\times10^{24})}{7.9\times10^{6}}$$ $$V = -5.1\times10^{7}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}$$
4
Answer: (a) \(V = -5.1\times10^{7}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\) (calculator shows \(-5.0658\times10^{7}\)). (b) the new potential is more negative, so it is a lower orbit. (c) $$W = m\Delta V_{\text{g}} = (850)[(-5.40\times10^{7})-(-5.07\times10^{7})]$$ $$W = -2.8\times10^{9}\ \text{J}$$ — the satellite loses this much gravitational PE moving to the lower orbit, and the answer does not depend on the path taken.

✍️ Worked example 4 – field strength from a graph (D1.13)

Using Fig 8, determine the magnitude of the gravitational field strength at the marked point on the curve.

1
Identify: \(g\) is the magnitude of the gradient of the tangent to the \(V_{\text{g}}\)-\(r\) graph at that point.
2
Data: tangent rises \(\Delta V = 3.8\times10^{8}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\) over \(\Delta r = 4.2\times10^{7}\ \text{m}\).
3
Equation: $$g = \left|\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta r}\right| = \frac{3.8\times10^{8}}{4.2\times10^{7}}$$
4
Answer: \(g = 9.0\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\).

🛰️ Real-world: budgeting a satellite's orbit change

Every orbit raise or lower a satellite performs costs (or releases) a known amount of energy per kilogram, \(\Delta V_{\text{g}}\) — engineers use exactly the calculation in Worked example 3 to budget the fuel needed for a manoeuvre, since the work done does not depend on the path flown between the two orbits.

✓ Examiner tip: never write "the potential increases" when you mean the potential energy increases (or vice versa) — state clearly which one you mean and whether you are dividing by, or already including, the mass.

🚀 HL Extension – equipotentials around two masses

Around two equal masses, individual near-circular equipotentials merge, further out, into a single closed curve enclosing both — because potential is scalar, the combined value at any point is just the sum of each mass's own \(-GM/r\).

Fig 10 — Equipotentials around two equal masses, M and M.

Challenge: sketch the field lines for Fig 10. Field lines are drawn perpendicular to every equipotential, pointing from higher to lower potential — so from outside inward toward each mass, converging radially close to each M, with a saddle point on the line joining them where the two inward pulls momentarily cancel (the two-mass equivalent of point P in Fig 6).

⚠️ Trap corner – common mistakes

MisconceptionCorrect view
Potential and potential energy are the same thingPotential is energy per unit mass (\(V_{\text{g}} = E_{\text{p}}/m\)) — a property of the location, not of any one object placed there
Moving to a "higher" potential means it becomes positivePotential stays negative everywhere except infinity (where it is zero); "higher" means less negative, closer to zero
Field lines run parallel to equipotential linesThey are always perpendicular to equipotentials, pointing from higher to lower potential
Potential is a vector, like field strengthPotential is a scalar — combine two sources by simple addition, never by components
⚠️ Most common slip: mixing up the sign of work done. Moving away from a planet (to a less negative potential) needs positive work done on the mass; falling towards it (more negative potential) means the mass does positive work, releasing kinetic energy.
Vg = -GM/r Vg = Ep/m ΔVg = W/m g = -ΔVg/Δr potential is scalar zero at infinity equipotentials ⟂ field lines same orbit → same Vg

Q27. A satellite of mass 200 kg was raised from the Earth's surface to a height of 320 km. (a) Determine the change in its gravitational potential energy. (b) What value would the (incorrect) use of \(\Delta E_{\text{p}} = mg\Delta h\) produce? Mass of Earth = 6.0×10²⁴ kg, radius of Earth = 6.4×10⁶ m. [4 marks]
✓ (a) \(E_{\text{p1}} = -GMm/R = -1.25\times10^{10}\ \text{J}\) at \(r=6.4\times10^{6}\) m.
✓ \(E_{\text{p2}} = -GMm/(R+h) = -1.19\times10^{10}\ \text{J}\) at \(r=6.72\times10^{6}\) m.
✓ \(\Delta E_{\text{p}} = E_{\text{p2}}-E_{\text{p1}} = +6.0\times10^{8}\ \text{J}\).
✓ (b) \(\Delta E_{\text{p}} = mg\Delta h = 200\times9.8\times(3.2\times10^{5}) = 6.3\times10^{8}\ \text{J}\) — close because \(h \ll R\), but strictly incorrect since \(g\) is not constant over 320 km.

✓ (a) \(E_{\text{p}} = -G(M_{\text{Earth}}M_{\text{Moon}})/r = -7.7\times10^{28}\ \text{J}\).
✓ (b) The Sun's gravitational pull on the Earth and Moon is far larger in absolute terms than their mutual pull.
✓ But the question asks only for the PE of the Earth–Moon pair — the Sun is a separate pairwise interaction with each body and does not enter this particular calculation.
✓ Because Earth and Moon are at almost the same distance from the Sun, its effect on their relative motion (which is what matters for their mutual orbit) nearly cancels, so ignoring it here is reasonable.

Q37. Explain why contour lines on a geographic map can be considered to be equipotential lines. [3 marks]
✓ Near the Earth's surface the gravitational field is uniform, so \(E_{\text{p}} = mgh\), and gravitational potential per unit mass is \(gh\).
✓ Points of equal altitude therefore have equal gravitational potential.
✓ A contour line joins points of equal altitude, so it also joins points of equal potential — the definition of an equipotential line.
