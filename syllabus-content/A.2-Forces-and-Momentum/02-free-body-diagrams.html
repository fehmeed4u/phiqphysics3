Free Body Diagrams
One object, every force acting on it, nothing else. Draw it correctly and the rest of the mechanics question follows almost mechanically.

🎯 Hook – Draw less, understand more

Even the simplest of force diagrams can get confusing if all the forces are shown. So we do something that looks like cheating: draw only one object, and show only the forces acting on that one object. Everything else — the table, the string, the Earth — disappears. What is left is a free-body diagram, and it contains everything you need.

Fig 1 — Four standard cases. The object has a solid outline; every arrow is a force acting on that object. Note the Moon has only one force on it.
💡 Diagnostic question (think – then click) — A box is pulled along the ground at constant speed. How many forces are on the free-body diagram, and what is the resultant? Four — weight, normal force, pulling force and friction. The resultant is zero, because the velocity is constant.

📐 Point particles and the rules of the diagram

Diagrams are often simplified further by representing the object as a small square or circle and treating it as a point particle. A point particle is an idealised representation of an object whatever its actual size and shape: it has no dimensions and occupies no space. Typically the point is located at the centre of mass.

The payoff is that all forces then act through the same point, so the analysis can ignore any rotational effects the forces might cause. Sometimes it is preferred to draw the object with its real shape — but that can cause confusion about exactly where each force acts.

RuleWhy it matters
One object onlyForces on the table, the string or the Earth belong on their diagrams, not this one
Only forces acting on the objectThe force the box exerts on the ground never appears on the box's diagram
Arrow length shows magnitudeEqual forces must be drawn equal; a zero resultant should look like a zero resultant
Label every arrow with the force's name"Force" earns no marks — weight, tension, normal contact force, friction, drag do
No velocity or acceleration arrowsA free-body diagram shows forces only; adding \(v\) or \(a\) arrows loses marks
⚠️ There is no such thing as a "force of motion". If a question's diagram shows an object coasting, the only horizontal force is resistive — nothing pushes it along.

➕ Resultants and components

A free-body diagram can be analysed to find the resultant force on a system — the vector sum of the forces acting, sometimes called the unbalanced or net force. For two forces, the resultant is represented in size and direction by the diagonal of the parallelogram (or rectangle) which has the two original force vectors as adjacent sides.

Fig 2 — Adding two forces: the resultant is the diagonal of the parallelogram built on them. For perpendicular forces this is a rectangle, so Pythagoras gives the magnitude.

The opposite process is just as useful. A single force \(F\) can be considered equivalent to two smaller forces at right angles to each other, called its components. This is resolving a force, and it is used whenever the original force does not act in a direction convenient for analysis. Because the two components are perpendicular, their effects can be considered separately.

Perpendicular components of a force $$F\cos\theta \qquad F\sin\theta$$
Fig 3 — Resolving \(F\) into perpendicular components. \(\cos\theta\) goes with the side adjacent to the angle — identify the angle before writing anything down.
💡 On an inclined plane — a flat surface at an angle to the horizontal — resolve along and perpendicular to the slope rather than horizontally and vertically. The motion is along the slope, so those axes turn a two-dimensional problem into two one-dimensional ones.

✍️ Worked example – an object on the point of slipping

An object of mass 12.7 kg is stationary on a slope inclined at 35° to the horizontal, and just begins to slide at that angle. Taking \(g = 9.81\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\), find the components of the weight down and into the slope, the frictional and normal forces, and the coefficient of static friction.

1
Identify: the object is in equilibrium, so the resultant contact force from the slope must be equal and opposite to the weight \(F_{\text{g}}\). That contact force is the combination of \(F_{\text{N}}\) perpendicular to the slope and \(F_{\text{f}}\) up the slope.
2
Data: \(m = 12.7\ \text{kg}\), \(\theta = 35^\circ\), \(g = 9.81\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\). Resolve along and perpendicular to the slope, so \(\sin 35^\circ = 0.574\) and \(\cos 35^\circ = 0.819\).
3
Equation: down the slope, \(mg\sin 35^\circ = 12.7 \times 9.81 \times 0.574 = 71.5\ \text{N}\). Into the slope, \(mg\cos 35^\circ = 12.7 \times 9.81 \times 0.819 = 102\ \text{N}\).
4
Answer: friction is 71.5 N up the slope and the normal force is 102 N — each equal and opposite to the corresponding component. Then \(F_{\text{f}} = \mu_{\text{s}}F_{\text{N}}\) gives \(\mu_{\text{s}} = 71.5/102 = 0.70\), which is exactly \(\tan 35^\circ\).
Fig 6 — Geometry for the worked example. The angle between the weight and the perpendicular to the slope equals the slope angle — that is why \(\cos\theta\) goes with the normal component.
On the point of slipping $$\mu_{\text{s}} = \frac{F_{\text{f}}}{F_{\text{N}}} = \frac{mg\sin\theta}{mg\cos\theta} = \tan\theta$$

🔧 Real-world: ramps and wheelchair gradients

An inclined plane is a simple device that reduces the force needed to raise a load: pushing a load up a ramp needs only \(mg\sin\theta\) rather than \(mg\). Accessibility standards cap ramp gradients near 1 in 12 — about 4.8° — which keeps that component below a tenth of the weight.

✓ Examiner tip: "draw a free-body diagram" is worth marks on its own. Draw it even when the question does not ask, then read the physics off it.

🚀 HL Extension – the swinging pendulum

For a pendulum of mass 158 g at 20° to the vertical, resolving along the string gives \(T = mg\cos 20^\circ = 0.158 \times 9.81 \times 0.940 = 1.5\ \text{N}\). The other component, \(mg\sin\theta\), is unbalanced and acts as the restoring force along the arc.
Challenge: where in the swing is the tension greatest? (Answer: at the lowest point — \(\cos\theta\) is largest there, and the string must also supply the centripetal force)

⚠️ Trap corner – common mistakes

MisconceptionCorrect view
Both members of a Newton's third law pair go on the diagramOnly forces acting on the chosen object. Its reaction on the ground belongs on the ground's diagram.
The normal force always equals the weightOnly on a horizontal surface with no vertical acceleration. On a slope \(F_{\text{N}} = mg\cos\theta\), which is smaller.
A moving object needs a forward force on its diagramConstant velocity means zero resultant. A coasting object has only resistive horizontal forces.
Weight and its components both belong on the diagramDraw one or the other. Showing all three double-counts the weight.
Resolve horizontally and vertically on a slopeResolve along and perpendicular to the slope — that is the direction the motion can happen in.

Exam‑style questions
Exam question · Draw
Draw fully labelled free-body diagrams for each of the following: (a) a car moving horizontally with constant velocity [2] (b) a boat decelerating after its engine has been switched off [2] (c) a car accelerating up a hill. [3] [7 marks]
✓ (a) Weight down and normal contact force up, equal in length; driving force forward and drag plus friction backward, equal in length ✓ resultant zero in both directions.
✓ (b) Weight down balanced by upthrust; a single horizontal arrow — water resistance — pointing backwards, against the motion ✓ no forward force at all.
✓ (c) Weight vertically down; normal contact force perpendicular to the hill surface ✓ driving force up the slope, longer than the sum of the resistive force and the down-slope component of weight ✓ so there is a net force up the slope.
Exam question · Calculate
A wooden block of mass 2.7 kg rests on a slope inclined at 22° to the horizontal. (a) Calculate the components of its weight down the slope and perpendicular to the slope. [3] (b) State the frictional force and normal contact force acting on the block. [2] (c) If the angle is increased the block begins to slide when \(\theta = 26^\circ\). Calculate the coefficient of friction and state whether it is static or kinetic. [3] [8 marks]
✓ (a) \(mg = 2.7 \times 9.81 = 26.5\ \text{N}\) ✓ down slope \(= 26.5\sin 22^\circ = 9.9\ \text{N}\) ✓ into slope \(= 26.5\cos 22^\circ = 24.6\ \text{N}\)
✓ (b) Friction \(= 9.9\ \text{N}\) up the slope ✓ normal force \(= 24.6\ \text{N}\) perpendicular to and away from the surface — each equal and opposite to the corresponding component.
✓ (c) \(\mu = \tan 26^\circ\) ✓ \(= 0.49\) ✓ this is the static coefficient, because it is measured at the instant sliding is about to begin.
Exam question · Show that
A pendulum bob of mass 158 g hangs on a string. (a) Draw a free-body diagram for the bob when the string makes an angle of 20° to the vertical. [2] (b) By resolving the weight, show that the tension in the string at this position is 1.5 N. [3] (c) State the effect of the other component of the weight on the pendulum. [1] (d) Show that a mass on an inclined plane just begins to slip when \(\tan\theta\) equals the coefficient of static friction. [3] [9 marks]
✓ (a) Two forces only: weight \(mg\) vertically downwards from the bob ✓ and tension \(T\) along the string towards the pivot.
✓ (b) Resolve the weight along the string: component \(= mg\cos 20^\circ\) ✓ \(= 0.158 \times 9.81 \times 0.940\) ✓ \(= 1.46 \approx 1.5\ \text{N}\), and the tension balances it at the extreme of the swing.
✓ (c) The perpendicular component \(mg\sin\theta\) is unbalanced and accelerates the bob along the arc back towards the centre — it is the restoring force.
✓ (d) At the point of slipping, friction is at its maximum: \(F_{\text{f}} = \mu_{\text{s}}F_{\text{N}}\) ✓ and in equilibrium \(F_{\text{f}} = mg\sin\theta\), \(F_{\text{N}} = mg\cos\theta\) ✓ so \(\mu_{\text{s}} = \dfrac{mg\sin\theta}{mg\cos\theta} = \tan\theta\) — the mass and \(g\) cancel.
