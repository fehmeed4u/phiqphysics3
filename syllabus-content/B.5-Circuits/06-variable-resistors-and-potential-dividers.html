Variable Resistors and Potential Divider Circuits
A rheostat only ever limits current and p.d. — it can never take a component's p.d. all the way to zero. A potentiometer can. This lesson shows why, then builds up to potential dividers using LDRs and thermistors as sensors.

🎛️ Hook – two variable resistors that do very different jobs

Resistors can have variable resistance. Variable resistors come in a wide variety of shapes and sizes. In Fig 1, a is small and has a high resistance; b is much larger because it is designed to carry bigger currents, although it has a lower resistance — it is often called a rheostat.

Fig 1 — Two different variable resistors. (a) is small with a high resistance; (b) is a rheostat — larger, lower resistance, carries bigger currents.
💡 Diagnostic question (think – then read on) — a rheostat is in series with a lamp across a battery. As you increase the rheostat's resistance, what happens to the current, and to the p.d. across the rheostat and across the lamp? The overall circuit resistance rises, so the current falls. The p.d. across the rheostat rises while the p.d. across the lamp falls — but only ever down to a minimum set by the ratio of the two resistances, never to zero.

📏 The rheostat: a two-terminal limit

Many variable resistances have three terminals — one at each end of the resistance track, and a third, movable sliding contact between them. When only two of the three terminals are used (one end plus the slider), the device behaves as a simple series resistor of adjustable size: a rheostat.

Series circuit$$I = \frac{V}{R_{\text{total}}}$$

Worked example B5.7. A battery supplies a constant 12 V (negligible internal resistance). A fixed component has resistance 24 Ω; a rheostat varies from 0 to 48 Ω, in series with the component. Find the current and the p.d. across each part at three rheostat settings.

1
Identify: series circuit, so use \(I = V/R_{\text{total}}\), then \(V = IR\) for each part.
2
Data: \(V = 12\ \text{V}\), \(R_{\text{comp}} = 24\ \Omega\), \(R_{\text{v}} = 0, 24, 48\ \Omega\).
3
Equation:
\(I = 12/(24+0)\)
\(I = 12/(24+24)\)
\(I = 12/(24+48)\)
4
Answer: \(R_{\text{v}}=0\): \(I=0.50\ \text{A}\), \(V_{\text{v}}=0\ \text{V}\), \(V_{\text{comp}}=12\ \text{V}\).
\(R_{\text{v}}=24\,\Omega\): \(I=0.25\ \text{A}\), \(V_{\text{v}}=6.0\ \text{V}\), \(V_{\text{comp}}=6.0\ \text{V}\).
\(R_{\text{v}}=48\,\Omega\): \(I=0.17\ \text{A}\), \(V_{\text{v}}=8.0\ \text{V}\), \(V_{\text{comp}}=4.0\ \text{V}\).
⚠️ It is not possible to reduce the component's p.d. below 4.0 V in this arrangement, however far the rheostat is turned up. Using the same variable resistor connected as a potentiometer instead, the p.d. across the component can be varied all the way from 0 to 12 V — see below.
Fig 2 — The circuit for Worked example B5.7: battery, rheostat and fixed component in series.

🔀 The potentiometer: the full range

A potentiometer is the name given to a three-terminal variable resistor when its sliding contact is used to produce a varying p.d., rather than just a varying series resistance.

Connected as in Fig 3, the potentiometer provides a p.d., \(V_{\text{out}}\), that varies continuously from zero to the full p.d. of the battery, \(V_{\text{in}}\). Maximum voltage is obtained with the sliding contact at the top (as drawn); the voltage is zero with the contact at the bottom, when both connections to the other circuit come from the same point.

Fig 3 — A variable resistor used as a potentiometer. The sliding contact splits the track into \(R_1\) (top) and \(R_2\) (bottom); \(V_{\text{out}}\) is taken across \(R_2\).

A potentiometer provides the best way of varying the p.d. across a component in order to investigate its \(I\)–\(V\) characteristics (Fig 4). When a potentiometer feeds another circuit, \(V_{\text{out}}\) cannot be assumed correct without considering the resistance of that circuit — generally the circuit's resistance should be much higher than the potentiometer's, unlike in the worked example below.

Fig 4 — A circuit for investigating \(I\)–\(V\) characteristics: the potentiometer's slider feeds the component through an ammeter, with a voltmeter across the component.

Worked example B5.8. Same values as before: 12 V supply, component 24 Ω, potentiometer 0 to 48 Ω. Find the p.d. across the component with the slider at the top, at the bottom, and at the mid-point.

1
Identify: at the mid-point the bottom half of the potentiometer (24 Ω) is in parallel with the 24 Ω component — this is the loading case the general rule above warns about.
2
Data: \(V_{\text{in}} = 12\ \text{V}\), each half of the potentiometer \(= 24\ \Omega\), component \(= 24\ \Omega\).
3
Equation (mid-point): bottom half ∥ component \(= 12\ \Omega\), in series with the top half (24 Ω), total \(= 36\ \Omega\).
\(V_{\text{bottom}} = (12/36)\times 12\)
4
Answer: top: connected across the full supply, 12 V. Bottom: not connected across the supply, 0 V. Mid-point: top half gets \((24/36)\times12=8.0\) V, so the bottom half — and the component — get 4.0 V.

🌡️ Sensors: LDRs and thermistors

Fig 5 shows an LDR (light-dependent resistor) and a thermistor, both semi-conducting components used as electrical sensors in circuits controlling lighting and heating.

Fig 5 — LDR (left) and thermistor (right), for sensing light intensity and temperature.
📖 Potential-dividing circuit — two resistors in series with a constant p.d. across them. When one resistance changes, the p.d. across each resistor changes, and this can be used to control another part of the circuit.
General potential-divider ratio$$V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{s}} \times \frac{R_{\text{out}}}{R_{\text{total}}}$$

More light energy releases more charge carriers in an LDR, so its resistance falls sharply as light intensity rises — from about \(10^{6}\,\Omega\) in the dark to about \(10^{2}\,\Omega\) in bright sunlight (10,000× less; note the resistance scale is logarithmic). Thermal energy releases more charge carriers in a thermistor, so its resistance falls as it gets hotter (this type is called NTC — a second type, PTC, has resistance that rises with temperature instead).

✍️ Worked example B5.9 – an LDR light-switch

Consider the circuit of Fig 8: a 6.0 V supply across a variable resistor (\(R_{\text{v}}\)) in series with an LDR, \(V_{\text{out}}\) taken across the LDR. The light comes on if \(V_{\text{out}} > 5.0\) V. With \(R_{\text{v}} = 50\,\text{k}\Omega\), find \(V_{\text{out}}\) in bright sunlight and in a dark room. Take LDR resistances from Fig 6.

1
Identify: assuming the same current in both resistors, \(V_{\text{LDR}} = V_{\text{s}} \times (R_{\text{LDR}}/R_{\text{total}})\).
2
Data: \(V_{\text{s}} = 6.0\ \text{V}\), \(R_{\text{v}} = 50\ \text{k}\Omega\); sunlight \(R_{\text{LDR}} \approx 100\,\Omega\); dark \(R_{\text{LDR}} \approx 1.0\times10^{6}\,\Omega\).
3
Equation:
sunlight: \(V_{\text{LDR}} = 6.0\times\dfrac{100}{100+50000}\)
dark: \(V_{\text{LDR}} = 6.0\times\dfrac{1.0\times10^{6}}{1.0\times10^{6}+50000}\)
4
Answer: sunlight: \(V_{\text{LDR}} = 0.12\ \text{V}\) — too low, light stays off. Dark: \(V_{\text{LDR}} = 5.7\ \text{V}\) — above 5.0 V, light switches on. The switching threshold can be tuned by choosing \(R_{\text{v}}\); in practice the input resistance of the lighting circuit also has to be considered.
Fig 8 — An LDR in a potential-dividing circuit; \(V_{\text{out}}\) is the input to a light-control circuit.

🌍 Real-world: from rough comparison to a calibrated sensor

An LDR in a potential-dividing circuit can compare light intensities at different times or places, but only roughly. If more accuracy is needed, the output must be calibrated against a reliable reference meter, which usually displays intensity in lux (not needed in this course). The same applies to a thermistor calibrated against a thermometer to make a working thermostat.

✓ Examiner tip: when a question gives you a threshold voltage for switching (as in B5.9), always compare your calculated \(V_{\text{out}}\) to that threshold and state explicitly whether the device turns on or off — a bare number earns no mark for the conclusion.

🚀 Extension – swapping the sensor's position

In Fig 8 the LDR is the bottom resistor, so \(V_{\text{out}}\) rises as it gets darker (its resistance rises). Swap the LDR and the fixed resistor's positions, and \(V_{\text{out}}\) instead rises as it gets brighter — useful for a circuit that must switch on in daylight rather than darkness. The same trick works for a thermistor: put it on top to make \(V_{\text{out}}\) fall as temperature rises (a heating thermostat), or on the bottom to make \(V_{\text{out}}\) rise as temperature rises (a cooling/fridge thermostat).

Challenge: a refrigerator thermostat needs \(V_{\text{out}}\) to rise as the inside gets warmer, triggering the compressor. Where should the (NTC) thermistor go — top or bottom of the divider? Answer: on top. As temperature rises, thermistor resistance falls, so a smaller fraction of the divider's p.d. drops across it and a larger fraction drops across the fixed bottom resistor — so \(V_{\text{out}}\), taken across that bottom resistor, rises.

⚠️ Trap corner – common mistakes

MisconceptionCorrect view
A rheostat in series can reduce a component's p.d. to zeroIt can only reduce it to a minimum set by the ratio of the two resistances (4.0 V in Worked example B5.7) — never to zero. Only a potentiometer connection gives the full 0→\(V_{\text{in}}\) range
A potentiometer's \(V_{\text{out}}\) can always be read off directly from the ratio of its two halvesOnly if the circuit it feeds has much higher resistance than the potentiometer. If it doesn't (as in Worked example B5.8), the fed circuit's resistance is in parallel with part of the potentiometer and changes the ratio
LDR resistance increases with light intensityIt decreases — more light releases more charge carriers, lowering resistance
Thermistor resistance always decreases with temperatureTrue only for the common NTC type. A PTC thermistor's resistance rises with temperature
The resistance axis on an LDR graph behaves like a normal linear scaleIt is logarithmic — equal divisions represent ×10 changes in resistance, not equal additions
⚠️ Most common slip: assuming the current is the same through every resistor in a potential-divider sketch even when another component is connected in parallel with one of them (as in B5.8's mid-point case) — check for a parallel branch before applying \(V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{s}} \times R/R_{\text{total}}\) directly.
I = V/R rheostat: 2-terminal, limited range potentiometer: 3-terminal, 0→Vin V_out = Vs × R_out/R_total watch for parallel loading LDR: light↑ → R↓ (log scale) NTC thermistor: T↑ → R↓ PTC thermistor: T↑ → R↑

Exam‑style questions
Exam question · Calculate / Discuss / Suggest
Q34. A 12.0 V battery is connected across two 100 Ω resistors, X and Y, in series (Fig 9). Point A is to the left of X; point B is between X and Y. (a) Calculate the p.d. between A and B. A student wants to connect a lamp rated 2.5 A, 6.0 V. (b) Calculate the working resistance of the lamp, and discuss whether it will work normally connected between A and B, in parallel with X. (c) A second student thinks the lamp will work if X is removed (lamp still connected between A and B). Calculate the new voltage across the lamp, and state whether it will work normally. (d) Suggest how the lamp could be made to work normally using the same 12 V battery. [8 marks]
Fig 9 — Circuit for Q34.
✓ (a) X and Y are equal (100 Ω each) in series, so the 12.0 V splits equally: \(V_{\text{AB}} = 6.0\ \text{V}\).
✓ (b) \(R_{\text{lamp}} = V/I = 6.0/2.5 = 2.4\ \Omega\).
✓ Lamp ∥ X: \((100\times2.4)/102.4 = 2.34\ \Omega\), in series with Y (100 Ω) — total 102.34 Ω.
✓ \(V_{\text{lamp}} = 12.0\times2.34/102.34 \approx 0.27\ \text{V}\) — far below the 6.0 V needed, so the lamp will glow very dimly, not normally.
✓ (c) With X removed, lamp (2.4 Ω) is in series with Y (100 Ω): \(I = 12.0/102.4 = 0.117\ \text{A}\), \(V_{\text{lamp}} = 0.117\times2.4 \approx 0.28\ \text{V}\) — still far short of 6.0 V, so it still will not work normally.
✓ (d) The lamp needs a resistor placed so most of the 12.0 V is dropped elsewhere at the lamp's rated current — e.g. a series "dropper" resistor of \((12.0-6.0)/2.5 = 2.4\ \Omega\), or a properly sized potential divider designed around the lamp's 2.4 Ω working resistance.
Exam question · Calculate / State / Estimate
Q35. In Fig 10, the variable resistor can be changed continuously from 1 kΩ to 10 kΩ, in series with a fixed R = 5 kΩ, across a 6.0 V supply. (a) Calculate the maximum and minimum \(V_{\text{out}}\) across R. (b) State (without calculation) how your answer to (a) changes if \(V_{\text{out}}\) is also connected across another 5 kΩ. (c) Estimate the percentage change in the thermistor's resistance (Fig 7) between 0 °C and 60 °C. [6 marks]
Fig 10 — Circuit for Q35.
✓ (a) Max \(V_{\text{out}}\) when the variable resistor is smallest (1 kΩ): \(V_{\text{out}} = 6.0\times5/(1+5) = 5.0\ \text{V}\).
✓ Min \(V_{\text{out}}\) when the variable resistor is largest (10 kΩ): \(V_{\text{out}} = 6.0\times5/(10+5) = 2.0\ \text{V}\).
✓ (b) Adding a parallel 5 kΩ load lowers the effective output resistance, so both the maximum and minimum \(V_{\text{out}}\) decrease.
✓ (c) From Fig 7: about 33,000 Ω at 0 °C falling to about 5,000 Ω at 60 °C.
✓ Percentage change \(\approx (33000-5000)/33000\times100 \approx 85\%\) decrease.

Exam question · Describe
Q37. Describe a laboratory experiment that could be used to obtain results similar to those in Fig 7 (thermistor resistance vs temperature). Include a fully annotated diagram. [6 marks]
✓ Apparatus: thermistor and a thermometer both immersed in a beaker of water on a stirred heater/hot plate, thermistor connected to a multimeter (ohmmeter) via leads kept out of the water.
✓ Method: heat gradually, stir continuously for a uniform temperature, and at each interval (e.g. every 5 °C) pause, let the reading settle, and record temperature and resistance.
✓ Take readings over a wide range (e.g. 0 °C, using an ice bath, up to 60 °C or beyond) and repeat for reliability.
✓ Diagram should show: beaker of water, thermometer bulb near the thermistor bead, thermistor leads to the ohmmeter, stirrer, and heat source — all labelled.
✓ Plot resistance (y) against temperature (x) to obtain a curve like Fig 7.
✓ Safety: use an electrically insulated thermistor probe or keep leads/meter away from the water.
