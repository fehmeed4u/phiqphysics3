EMF and Internal Resistance
Every real source of electrical energy — battery, solar cell, dynamo — has resistance of its own. This lesson shows why the voltage you measure across a battery is never quite the emf once current flows, and how to work with the difference.

🔆 Hook – the street light that never plugs in

A solar-powered street light has no mains cable at all. During the day its panels convert sunlight directly into electrical energy; that energy is stored, then released at night to power the lamps. Whether the source is a solar panel, a chemical battery, or a bicycle dynamo, the same quantity — emf — describes how much energy it delivers per unit charge.

Fig 1 — Solar cells collect energy during the day to power the lamps at night: a stand-alone solar lighting system.
💡 Diagnostic question — why can a solar cell, a dynamo, and a car battery all be described using the same quantity, emf? Because emf is defined purely by energy transferred per coulomb of charge — it says nothing about the mechanism (chemical, photovoltaic, kinetic) that produced that energy. Any source of electrical energy has an emf.

🔋 Sources of electrical energy

To run electrical or electronic devices, we need a source of electrical energy. The main options:

  • Most homes are supplied with a pd from mains electricity generated at power stations.
  • A home-based electrical generator converts kinetic energy into electrical energy, often from burning a fuel.
  • Batteries (chemical or electric cells) use chemical reactions to provide a pd. Single-use or rechargeable.
  • Solar cells (photovoltaic cells) use radiant energy from the Sun to produce a pd.
  • Wind generators use the kinetic energy of moving air to produce a pd.
  • A dynamo — a generator that produces direct current — on a bicycle transfers kinetic energy to electrical energy for the lamp.

Each source has advantages and disadvantages, assessed by: convenience of use, power available, pd available, whether it contributes to pollution/global warming, whether it is renewable, whether the supply is continuous, ac or dc, mobile or fixed, its internal resistance (see below), and cost.

⚡ Defining emf

The electromotive force (emf) of a battery, or any other source of electrical energy, is the total energy transferred in the source per unit charge passing through it.

Electromotive force · SI unit volt, V (J C⁻¹) $$\varepsilon = \frac{\text{energy transferred}}{\text{charge}}$$

Emf is given the symbol \(\varepsilon\). The name can confuse — emf is not actually a force, which is why it is almost always just called "emf". A battery with an emf of 12 V transfers a total of 12 J to every coulomb of charge that flows through it. But some of that energy is transferred within the source itself — that's the subject of the rest of this lesson.

🧲 Internal resistance and lost volts

Cells, batteries and other sources of electrical energy are not perfect conductors. The materials they are made from have resistance — the internal resistance of the source, symbol \(r\), typically less than 1 Ω.

Fig 2 — A cell in a simple circuit. The emf ε and internal resistance r are drawn separately to aid understanding, but in practice they are combined — there is no accessible point between them.

The voltmeter across the terminals is assumed ideal. Switch open, no current (\(I=0\)): there is no pd across the internal resistance (\(V_r = Ir = 0\)), so the voltmeter shows the true emf. Switch closed, current \(I\) flows: a pd \(V_r = Ir\) appears across the internal resistance, so the voltmeter reading falls.

📖 Lost volts — the voltage drop below the emf that occurs when a source delivers current to a circuit; lost volts (\(Ir\)) increase with larger currents. Terminal potential difference, V — the pd across the battery terminals when it is supplying current (less than the emf).

Worked numerical example: a battery has emf 9.0 V and internal resistance 0.4 Ω. Switch open — voltmeter reads 9.0 V. A current of 2.5 A flows: \(V_r = 2.5 \times 0.4 = 1.0\ \text{V}\), so the voltmeter reading falls from 9.0 V to \((9.0-1.0) = 8.0\) V.

Total energy transferred per coulomb = energy transferred to the circuit per coulomb + energy transferred inside the cell per coulomb. In symbols:

$$\varepsilon = V + Ir$$

The same current flows through both resistances, so using \(V=IR\):

$$\varepsilon = IR + Ir$$ $$\varepsilon = I(R+r)$$

An ideal voltmeter across a battery only shows the emf if no current is flowing. At all other times the reading is less, by an amount depending on the current at that moment.

🔬 Investigating emf and internal resistance

A student set up a circuit to investigate the emf and internal resistance of an AA battery: the battery (with its internal resistance) in series with an ammeter and a variable resistor, and a voltmeter connected across the battery terminals.

Fig 5 — Investigating internal resistance: the AA battery (dashed box) in series with an ammeter and variable resistor R; a voltmeter reads the terminal pd.

Varying the resistance gave these readings:

I / AV / V
1.01.0
0.61.2
0.41.3
0.21.4

The student concluded the battery had an emf of 1.5 V and internal resistance 0.5 Ω. She reasoned this by comparing pairs of readings: each 0.2 A drop in current corresponds to a 0.1 V rise in terminal pd, so the gradient magnitude — and hence r — is \(0.1/0.2 = 0.5\ \Omega\); extrapolating the straight-line trend to \(I=0\) gives \(V = \varepsilon \approx 1.5\) V.

Her teacher agreed the conclusions were acceptable, but said the investigation was not accurate enough, and suggested improvements — including plotting an appropriate graph (V against I) to extract ε and r more precisely from the intercept and gradient of a best-fit line, rather than from just two readings.

✍️ Worked example – finding current and terminal pd

A battery with an emf of 1.5 V and internal resistance 0.82 Ω is connected in a circuit with a 5.6 Ω fixed resistor. a Calculate the current in the circuit. b If an ideal voltmeter is connected across the terminals of the battery when the current is flowing, what reading will it show?

1
Identify: a single loop, so use \(\varepsilon = I(R+r)\), then \(V=IR\) for the terminal reading.
2
Data: \(\varepsilon = 1.5\) V, \(R = 5.6\ \Omega\), \(r = 0.82\ \Omega\).
3
Equation (a):
\(I = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{R+r} = \dfrac{1.5}{5.6+0.80}\)
4
Answer: \(I = 0.23\) A.
(b) \(V = IR = 0.23 \times 5.6 = 1.3\) V.
Fig 6 — Geometry for the worked example: emf 1.5 V, internal resistance r, and external resistor R = 5.6 Ω in series.

🌍 Real-world: choosing a battery

The emf and internal resistance of a source are its defining features, along with physical size and mass, which affect how much energy it can store. 1.5 V is the most common single-cell emf.

A 1400 mAh battery can supply 1.4 A for one hour: \(\text{Energy} = VIt = 3.7 \times 1.4 \times 3600 = 1.9\times10^{4}\) J (≈20 kJ). An e-bike battery — about 3 kg, 50 Ah (9 MJ), emf 50 V, internal resistance 0.1 Ω — is enough for roughly 30 km on mostly level ground.

Fig 7 — An e-bike battery supplies energy to assist pedalling — a practical application of battery emf and capacity.
✓ Examiner tip: when comparing sources, weigh emf, internal resistance, and capacity together — a high emf with a high internal resistance can deliver less usable current than a lower-emf source with very low r (e.g. a car battery).

🚀 HL extension – combining cells

Cells of the same emf connected in series give a greater overall emf, but also a greater total internal resistance. Connected in parallel, the overall emf stays the same, but the total internal resistance is reduced.

n identical cells, each ε₀, r₀ $$\varepsilon_{\text{series}} = n\varepsilon_0,\ r_{\text{series}} = nr_0$$ $$\varepsilon_{\text{parallel}} = \varepsilon_0,\ r_{\text{parallel}} = \frac{r_0}{n}$$

Challenge: three identical cells, each emf 2.0 V and \(r=0.15\ \Omega\), are connected in parallel and then supply a 2.0 Ω external resistor. Find the current.

Answer: parallel combination gives \(\varepsilon=2.0\) V, \(r=0.15/3=0.05\ \Omega\). \(I=\varepsilon/(R+r)=2.0/2.05=0.98\) A.

⚠️ Trap corner – common mistakes

MisconceptionCorrect view
A voltmeter across a battery always reads its emfOnly true when no current flows. With current, the voltmeter reads the terminal pd, \(V=\varepsilon - Ir\)
Internal resistance can always be ignoredOnly when \(r \ll R\). In high-current or low-resistance circuits it has a significant effect — check before assuming "negligible internal resistance"
Cells in series and in parallel behave the same waySeries: emf and total internal resistance both add. Parallel: emf stays the same, total internal resistance falls
A short circuit just means "a strong current"It is an unwanted low-resistance path; the very small effective R can drive a dangerously large current unless a fuse or breaker limits it
⚠️ Most common slip: using the quoted emf as the terminal pd in a calculation where current is clearly flowing. Always check first whether the circuit is open (\(I=0\), voltmeter = emf) or closed (\(I\neq0\), voltmeter = terminal pd = ε − Ir).
ε = V + Ir ε = I(R + r) lost volts = Ir terminal pd falls under load r typically < 1 Ω I = 0 → V = ε series cells: ε, r both add parallel cells: ε same, r falls

Exam‑style questions
Exam question · Determine
Q29. Three cells, each of emf 1.5 V and internal resistance 0.20 Ω, were connected together. Determine their combined emf and internal resistance if they were connected: a in series b in parallel. [4 marks]
✓ (a) Series: emfs add, \(\varepsilon = 3 \times 1.5 = 4.5\) V.
✓ Internal resistances add, \(r = 3 \times 0.20 = 0.60\ \Omega\).
✓ (b) Parallel: combined emf stays \(1.5\) V (same as one cell).
✓ Combined \(r = 0.20/3 = 0.067\ \Omega\).
Exam question · Calculate
Q30. A very high-resistance voltmeter shows a voltage of 12.5 V when connected across the terminals of a battery not supplying a current. When the battery is connected to a lamp, a current of 2.5 A flows and the voltmeter reading falls to 11.8 V. a State the emf of the battery. b Calculate the internal resistance. c Calculate the resistance of the lamp. [5 marks]
✓ (a) With no current the voltmeter reads the true emf: \(\varepsilon = 12.5\) V.
✓ (b) \(\varepsilon = V + Ir \Rightarrow 12.5 = 11.8 + 2.5r\)
✓ \(r = 0.7/2.5 = 0.28\ \Omega\).
✓ (c) \(R = V/I = 11.8/2.5 = 4.72\ \Omega\).
Exam question · Calculate / Predict
Q31. When a battery of emf 4.5 V and internal resistance 1.1 Ω was connected to a resistor, the current was 0.68 A. a What was the value of the resistor? b If the resistor was replaced with another of twice the value, predict the new current. c State an assumption made in answering b. [5 marks]
✓ (a) \(\varepsilon = I(R+r) \Rightarrow 4.5 = 0.68(R+1.1)\)
✓ \(R = 5.5\ \Omega\).
✓ (b) New \(R = 11.0\ \Omega\): \(I = \varepsilon/(R+r) = 4.5/12.1 = 0.37\) A.
✓ (c) Assumed the internal resistance \(r\) stays constant (unaffected by the current drawn).
Exam question · Calculate / Suggest
Q32. If a connecting wire is connected by mistake across a battery or power supply, it is an example of a short circuit. a Calculate the current that flows through a battery of emf 12.0 V and internal resistance 0.25 Ω if it is accidentally "shorted". b Suggest what will happen to the battery. [3 marks]
✓ (a) With external resistance ≈ 0, \(I = \varepsilon/r = 12.0/0.25 = 48\) A.
✓ (b) Such a large current will generate a lot of heat inside the battery very quickly.
✓ The battery may overheat, be permanently damaged, or (depending on the chemistry) vent or catch fire, unless protected by a fuse or breaker.
Exam question · Outline
Q33. A 1.50 V cell with an internal resistance of 0.100 Ω is connected to a resistance of 500 Ω. Outline why it would be reasonable to assume that the cell has "negligible internal resistance". Include a calculation. [3 marks]
✓ \(I = \varepsilon/(R+r) = 1.50/500.1 = 3.0\times10^{-3}\) A.
✓ Lost volts \(= Ir = 3.0\times10^{-3}\times0.100 \approx 3\times10^{-4}\) V.
✓ This is about 0.02% of the emf — utterly insignificant compared with \(R \gg r\), so \(r\) can safely be ignored.
