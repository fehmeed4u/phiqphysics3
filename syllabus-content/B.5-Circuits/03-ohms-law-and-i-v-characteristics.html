Ohm's Law and I-V Characteristics
A single graph tells you everything about how a component behaves — whether its resistance is fixed, and if not, exactly how it changes.

🎯 Hook – the lamp that won't obey a simple rule

Connect a filament lamp to a variable power supply and slowly turn up the voltage. Double the voltage, and the current does not double — it goes up by less. Do the same with a resistor and the current doubles exactly. The lamp and the resistor are following completely different rules, and only one of them is Ohm's law.

Fig 1 — As the p.d. across a filament lamp rises, its filament heats up and glows brighter — and its resistance changes as it does so.
💡 Diagnostic question (think – then read on) — why does doubling the voltage across a filament lamp not double the current? The filament heats up as current flows, and its resistance increases with temperature. A higher resistance at the higher voltage means the current rises by less than a simple proportional (V = IR with constant R) relationship would predict.

⚡ The complete statement of Ohm's law

The complete and precise statement of Ohm's law is:

📖 The current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, provided that its temperature remains constant.
Ohm's law $$V = IR$$

Where \(V\) is the potential difference in volts (V), \(I\) is the current in amperes (A), and \(R\) is the resistance in ohms (\(\Omega\)).

The condition constant temperature is the single most frequently forgotten element in exam answers. Ohm's law is not a universal rule for every component — it only applies when the physical conditions, especially temperature, do not change.

📊 Measuring an I-V characteristic

The best way to check whether a component obeys Ohm's law is to plot its I-V characteristic — a graph of current (\(y\)-axis) against potential difference (\(x\)-axis) — using an ammeter in series with the component and a voltmeter connected in parallel across it.

Fig 2 — Standard I-V circuit: the ammeter reads the current through the component, the voltmeter reads the p.d. across it, at a range of supply settings.

The shape of the resulting graph is a "lie detector" for ohmic behaviour:

✓ Ohmic conductor — strictly obeys Ohm's law. Graph: a straight line through the origin. The ratio \(V/I\) is constant, so resistance is constant, independent of voltage or current. Examples: a fixed resistor, metal wires at constant temperature.
⚠️ Non-ohmic conductor — does not obey Ohm's law; its resistance changes with the p.d. or current. Graph: any shape that is not a straight line through the origin. Examples: filament lamps, diodes, thermistors.

🔍 Diode and thermistor in detail

A diode is designed to allow current to flow in only one direction. In forward bias it has very low resistance once the applied voltage exceeds a small threshold voltage (typically around 0.6 V for silicon diodes). In reverse bias it has extremely high resistance, allowing almost no current to flow (only a negligible leakage current).

Fig 5 — Diode: negligible current in reverse bias, then a sharp rise in forward bias past the ~0.6 V threshold.

A thermistor (NTC – negative temperature coefficient) behaves oppositely to a metal filament: as current flows through it, its temperature rises, but its resistance decreases as temperature increases. This lets even more current flow, so the graph's slope increases as \(V\) rises.

Fig 6 — NTC thermistor: the curve gets steeper (not flatter) as p.d. rises, showing resistance falling as the device warms up.

✍️ Worked example – resistance from two points on a filament lamp's I-V graph

A filament lamp's I-V graph gives the following two data points: at \(V_1 = 1.0\) V, \(I_1 = 0.50\) A; at \(V_2 = 4.0\) V, \(I_2 = 1.0\) A. Show that the lamp's resistance increases between these two points, and state whether the lamp is ohmic.

1
Identify: resistance at any point on a non-ohmic graph must be found from \(R = V/I\) at that point — never from the graph's gradient.
2
Data: point 1: \(V_1 = 1.0\) V, \(I_1 = 0.50\) A. Point 2: \(V_2 = 4.0\) V, \(I_2 = 1.0\) A.
3
Equation:
\(R_1 = V_1/I_1 = 1.0/0.50\)
\(R_2 = V_2/I_2 = 4.0/1.0\)
4
Answer: \(R_1 = 2.0\ \Omega\), \(R_2 = 4.0\ \Omega\). Resistance doubles between the two points, so the lamp is not ohmic — its resistance is not constant. A true ohmic conductor would give the same \(R\) at every point.
Fig 7 — The two marked points sit on the same curve, but the line from the origin to each point has a different slope — proof the resistance is not constant.

🌍 Real-world: diodes as one-way valves

Because a diode's resistance is enormous in reverse bias and tiny in forward bias, diodes act like an electrical one-way valve. This is exactly why they're used in rectifier circuits, which convert alternating current into direct current, and in LEDs, which only light up when connected the correct way round.

✓ Examiner tip: never calculate resistance from the gradient of a non-ohmic I-V graph — that gradient changes at every point and is not \(R\). Always read off a \((V, I)\) pair and use \(R = V/I\) directly.

🚀 HL Extension – NTC vs PTC thermistors

The thermistor discussed above is an NTC (negative temperature coefficient) device: resistance falls as temperature rises. A PTC (positive temperature coefficient) thermistor does the opposite — its resistance rises with temperature, the same direction as a metal filament.

Challenge: sketch, in words, how a PTC thermistor's I-V graph would differ in shape from the NTC graph in Fig 6. (Answer: a PTC thermistor's resistance increases as it heats up, so its I-V curve would flatten as \(V\) increases — the slope decreasing rather than increasing, similar in shape to the filament lamp curve in Fig 4, not the NTC curve in Fig 6.)

Exam‑style questions
Exam question · State
Q1. State Ohm's law, including the condition under which it holds. [2 marks]
✓ The current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it.
✓ Provided that its temperature remains constant.
Exam question · Calculate
Q2. A component has a p.d. of 6.0 V across it when a current of 0.40 A flows through it. Calculate its resistance at this point. [2 marks]
✓ \(R = V/I = 6.0/0.40\)
✓ \(R = 15\ \Omega\).
Exam question · Explain
Q3. A student measures the resistance of a filament lamp at two different currents and finds two different values. Explain whether this means the student made an experimental error. [3 marks]
✓ No, this is expected — a filament lamp is non-ohmic, so its resistance is not constant.
✓ As current increases, the filament heats up, ion vibrations increase, collisions with electrons increase, and resistance increases.
✓ Resistance should be found from \(R = V/I\) at each point separately, not assumed to be a single fixed value.
Exam question · Sketch and explain
Q4. Sketch, in words, the I-V characteristic of a semiconductor diode, and explain the physical reason for its shape in reverse bias. [3 marks]
✓ Almost flat at zero current for negative (reverse bias) p.d., then rising very sharply for positive (forward bias) p.d. past a threshold of about 0.6 V.
✓ In reverse bias the diode has an extremely high resistance, so only a negligible leakage current flows.
✓ In forward bias, past the threshold, the diode's resistance becomes very low, so current rises steeply.
Exam question · Determine
Q5. A thermistor has a p.d. of 2.0 V across it when the current is 0.020 A, and a p.d. of 5.0 V across it when the current is 0.080 A. Determine the resistance at each point, and state whether the thermistor is NTC or PTC. [4 marks]
✓ \(R_1 = 2.0/0.020 = 100\ \Omega\).
✓ \(R_2 = 5.0/0.080 = 62.5\ \Omega\).
✓ Resistance has decreased as the p.d. (and current, and temperature) increased.
✓ This is NTC (negative temperature coefficient) behaviour.
