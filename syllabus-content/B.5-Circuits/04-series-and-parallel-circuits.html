Series and Parallel Circuits
Two rules for how current and voltage share themselves out — one for components strung one after another, one for components wired side by side. Almost every circuit you'll meet is built from these two patterns.

🎄 Hook – the string of lights that all go dark together

Old-style decorative light strings are wired in series: if one bulb blows, the current path is broken and every bulb on the string goes dark. Modern strings are wired in parallel: each bulb sits on its own branch across the same two wires, so one failed bulb only kills itself — the rest stay lit. The wiring pattern, not the bulbs, decides what happens when one component fails.

Fig 1 — One failed bulb in series takes out the whole string; in parallel, the other branches don't even notice.
💡 Diagnostic question — why does removing one bulb from a parallel string not stop current flowing to the others? Each bulb is connected directly across the same two supply wires, on its own separate path. Removing one branch doesn't break the paths belonging to the others — current can still complete a full loop through every remaining bulb.

➡️ Series circuits: one path for the current

Series connection: two or more components connected so that there is only one path for the current, which is the same through all of them. Because charge is conserved, the charge per second (current) flowing into each component must equal the current flowing out of it and into the next — there's nowhere else for it to go.

Current in series $$I = I_1 = I_2 = \ldots$$

Each component uses up some of the available potential difference driving the current round the circuit, so the separate p.d.s must add up to the p.d. across the whole combination:

Potential difference in series $$V = V_1 + V_2 + \ldots$$

Substituting \(V=IR\) for each resistor into \(V=V_1+V_2+\ldots\) gives \(IR_{\text{s}} = IR_1 + IR_2 + IR_3\). The current \(I\) is the same throughout, so it cancels, leaving the resistance of a single resistor \(R_{\text{s}}\) that would behave exactly like the whole combination:

Total resistance in series $$R_{\text{s}} = R_1 + R_2 + \ldots$$
Fig 2 — Resistors in series (Fig B5.27). Current is the same everywhere on the loop; each voltmeter reads a share of the total p.d.

⑂ Parallel circuits: many paths, same p.d.

Parallel connection: two or more components connected between the same two points, so they all have the same potential difference across them.

Potential difference in parallel $$V = V_1 = V_2 = \ldots$$

Conservation of charge means the current splits at the junction and recombines afterwards, so the total current supplied equals the sum of the branch currents:

Current in parallel $$I = I_1 + I_2 + \ldots$$

Applying \(I=V/R\) to every branch, \(V/R_{\text{p}} = V/R_1 + V/R_2 + V/R_3\). Every term shares the same \(V\), so it cancels, leaving the equation for the single resistor \(R_{\text{p}}\) equivalent to the whole combination:

Total resistance in parallel $$\frac{1}{R_{\text{p}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \ldots$$
Fig 3 — Resistors in parallel (Fig B5.28). All three share the same p.d.; the supply current is the sum of the branch currents.

All the sockets and appliances in a house are wired in parallel: each one gets the full supply voltage regardless of what else is switched on, and each can be switched independently without affecting the others.

⚖️ Kirchhoff's laws — the two rules made formal

Kirchhoff's first law (current/junction law): at any junction, the total current entering equals the total current leaving. This is just conservation of charge applied at a point — it's what forces \(I=I_1+I_2+\ldots\) in a parallel combination.

Kirchhoff's second law (voltage/loop law): around any closed loop, the sum of the potential differences is zero. This is conservation of energy — it's what forces \(V=V_1+V_2+\ldots\) in a series combination.

🔗 Nature of Science — conservation of charge and conservation of energy aren't special rules for circuits; they're two of the most fundamental principles in all of science, also used to explain ion movement across cell membranes and energy transfers in natural systems generally.

🔬 Tool 3: how many significant figures does "5000 Ω" have?

Without more context, you can't tell. If 5000 and 8000 are exact mathematical values, every figure is significant. If they're measurements, the number of significant figures depends on the uncertainty in the measurement — which isn't visible from the digits alone. Scientific notation removes the ambiguity: \(5.00\times10^3\ \Omega\) is unmistakably three significant figures, while \(5\times10^3\ \Omega\) is one.

For the numerical data in this course, the rule is simple: unless told otherwise, assume every digit given is significant.

✍️ Worked example 1 – 5000 Ω and 8000 Ω resistors

A 5000 Ω resistor and an 8000 Ω resistor are connected first in series, then in parallel, with a 4.5 V battery. Find, for each arrangement: the combined resistance; the current through each resistor; the p.d. across the 5000 Ω resistor.

1
Identify: series first — same current through both, resistances add. Then parallel — same p.d. across both, reciprocals add.
2
Data: \(R_1=5000\ \Omega\), \(R_2=8000\ \Omega\), \(V=4.5\) V.
3
Equation — series:
\(R_{\text{s}} = 5000+8000 = 13000\ \Omega\)
\(I = V/R_{\text{s}} = 4.5/13000\)
Equation — parallel:
\(1/R_{\text{p}} = 1/5000+1/8000 = 13/40000\)
\(R_{\text{p}} = 40000/13\)
4
Answer — series: \(R_{\text{s}}=13\,000\ \Omega\); \(I=3.5\times10^{-4}\) A through both resistors; \(V(5000\ \Omega)=IR=1.7\) V.
Answer — parallel: \(R_{\text{p}}=3100\ \Omega\); both resistors have the full 4.5 V across them; \(I(5000\ \Omega)=4.5/5000=9.0\times10^{-4}\) A; \(I(8000\ \Omega)=4.5/8000=5.6\times10^{-4}\) A.

✍️ Worked example 2 – five lamps (B5.5)

Lamps A and B are in series in the main circuit. Between them the circuit splits into two parallel branches: lamps C and D in series with each other, and lamp E alone. All five lamps are identical. (a) Compare the brightness of all five lamps. (b) If each lamp has a constant resistance of 2.0 Ω, find the total resistance of the circuit.

1
Identify: A and B carry the full circuit current (series). That current splits between the C–D branch and the E branch (parallel), so A and B must be brighter than C, D and E.
2
Data (b): each lamp \(R=2.0\ \Omega\). C and D are in series with each other: \(R_{\text{CD}} = 2.0+2.0 = 4.0\ \Omega\). This is in parallel with E.
3
Equation (b):
\(1/R_{\text{CD‖E}} = 1/4.0 + 1/2.0\)
\(R_{\text{CD‖E}} = 4/3 = 1.3\ \Omega\)
Total: \(R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{A}}+R_{\text{B}}+R_{\text{CD‖E}}\)
4
Answer (a): A and B are equally bright (same current, in series). C and D are equally bright as each other, but dimmer than A and B. E is brighter than C or D, because more current flows through the single lamp E than through either of the two lamps sharing the C–D branch.
Answer (b): \(R_{\text{total}} = 2.0+2.0+1.3 = 5.3\ \Omega\).
Fig 4 — Five-lamp circuit (Fig B5.29). A and B share the full current; it then splits between the C–D branch and lamp E.

🏠 Real-world: why your house is wired in parallel

If household appliances were wired in series, switching off one lamp would cut the current to every other appliance in the house, and every device would have to share the mains voltage between them. Parallel wiring gives every socket the full supply voltage and lets each device be switched on or off without touching any of the others.

✓ Examiner tip: if asked why household circuits use parallel wiring, name both reasons — full supply voltage to each device, and independent switching — a one-reason answer usually drops a mark.

🚀 HL extension – solving combination circuits

Most real circuits are neither purely series nor purely parallel — they're built from both. The strategy is always the same: find a parallel (or series) section that reduces to a single equivalent resistor, replace it with that one resistor, and repeat until the whole network is one simple series chain.

Challenge: in the five-lamp circuit above, why can't you just add all five resistances together to get the total? Answer: R = R1+R2+… only applies to resistors genuinely in series — C, D and E are not all in series with each other, since C+D and E form two separate parallel paths. You must collapse that parallel section to one equivalent resistor first, then add it to A and B in series.

Exam question · Calculate
Q24. Calculate the four possible total resistances that can be made by combining three 10 Ω resistors. [3 marks]
✓ All three in series: \(10+10+10 = 30\ \Omega\).
✓ All three in parallel: \(1/R = 3/10\), so \(R = 3.3\ \Omega\).
✓ Two in parallel, that combination in series with the third: \((10\times10)/20 + 10 = 5+10 = 15\ \Omega\).
✓ Two in series, that combination in parallel with the third: two in series \(=20\ \Omega\); \((10\times20)/30 = 6.7\ \Omega\).

Q25. Figure B5.30 shows a simple circuit (12 V battery, a 20 Ω resistor and a 10 Ω resistor in series) in which the ammeter and voltmeter have been connected in the wrong positions — the ammeter in parallel with the 10 Ω resistor, and the voltmeter in series with it. (a) Predict the readings you would expect on the meters. Explain your answer. (b) When the meters are swapped into their correct positions, what readings would you expect? [4 marks]
Fig 7 — Fig B5.30: ammeter and voltmeter connected in the wrong positions.
✓ (a) An (almost ideal) ammeter has near-zero resistance, so placing it in parallel with the 10 Ω resistor short-circuits that resistor. Virtually all the current takes the ammeter's zero-resistance path, so the ammeter reads close to \(12/20 = 0.6\) A — the current for the 20 Ω resistor alone.
✓ The voltmeter, in series with the (now short-circuited) 10 Ω resistor, has a very high resistance, so almost no current flows down that branch — it reads close to 0 V.

Q26. (a) Calculate the current that flows through a 12.0 Ω resistor connected to a p.d. of 9.10 V. (b) An ideal ammeter would display this current accurately, but what value will an ammeter of resistance 0.31 Ω record? (c) Determine the percentage error when using the ammeter in this way. [4 marks]
✓ (a) \(I = V/R = 9.10/12.0 = 0.758\) A.
✓ (b) The ammeter's own resistance adds to the circuit: \(I = 9.10/(12.0+0.31) = 9.10/12.31 = 0.739\) A.
✓ (c) Percentage error \(= \dfrac{0.739-0.758}{0.758}\times100 = -2.5\%\) — the ammeter reads about 2.5% low.

Q27. Figure B5.31 shows a 12 V circuit with resistors A–F. The total current from the battery is 6.0 A, flowing through resistor F. It splits: 1.0 A flows through resistor C, which then splits into branches through A and B (with 0.5 A through B); a middle branch through resistor D carries current I2; a bottom branch through resistor E carries 3.0 A; the currents recombine to give I3. (a) Determine the currents I1, I2 and I3. (b) If C = 5.0 Ω and E = 2.0 Ω, determine values for the four unknown resistances A, B, D and F. [6 marks]
Fig 8 — Fig B5.31: three parallel branches (C→A/B, D, E) between resistor F and the recombination point.
✓ (a) The three branches (C→A/B, D, E) are in parallel, so their currents sum to the total 6.0 A: \(1.0+I_2+3.0 = 6.0\), giving \(I_2 = 2.0\) A. Since A and B split the 1.0 A equally (B = 0.5 A), \(I_1 = 0.5\) A. All branch currents recombine before F, so \(I_3 = 6.0\) A.
✓ (b) All three parallel branches share the same p.d., equal to the p.d. across E: \(V = I_{\text{E}}R_{\text{E}} = 3.0\times2.0 = 6.0\) V.
✓ Branch C+A‖B: \(V_{\text{C}} = 1.0\times5.0 = 5.0\) V, leaving \(6.0-5.0=1.0\) V across A‖B, so \(R_{\text{A}} = R_{\text{B}} = 1.0/0.5 = 2.0\ \Omega\).
✓ Branch D: \(R_{\text{D}} = 6.0/2.0 = 3.0\ \Omega\).
✓ F carries the full 6.0 A and drops the remaining \(12-6.0=6.0\) V, so \(R_{\text{F}} = 6.0/6.0 = 1.0\ \Omega\).
