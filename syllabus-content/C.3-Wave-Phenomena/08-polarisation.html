Polarisation
How restricting the plane of oscillation proves light is a transverse wave — and the mathematics of Malus’s Law.
Core concepts — what is polarisation?

Why Only Transverse Waves Can Be Polarised

Polarisation is the restriction of wave oscillations to a single plane. This is only possible for transverse waves, where oscillations occur perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. Because transverse oscillations can point in many different perpendicular directions, a filter can select just one plane and block the rest.

Longitudinal waves (such as sound) oscillate parallel to their direction of travel. There is only one axis of vibration — along the propagation direction — so there is no additional plane to select or filter. This is why sound cannot be polarised, and why polarisation is direct evidence that light is a transverse wave.

Fig 1 — Transverse waves oscillate perpendicular to propagation (multiple planes available for filtering). Longitudinal waves oscillate parallel to propagation (no plane to filter).
💡 Polarisation is one of the key pieces of evidence that light is a transverse electromagnetic wave. If light were longitudinal, polarising filters would have no effect at all.

Malus’s Law — the master equation

Building Malus’s Law Step by Step

Step 1 — The polariser: When unpolarised light of intensity I₀ hits a polariser, the filter transmits only the component aligned with its axis. Since unpolarised light has oscillations spread equally across all planes, exactly half the intensity passes through:

I₁ = I₀ / 2

This factor of ½ is a fixed result — it applies every time unpolarised light meets a polariser, regardless of the filter’s orientation.

Step 2 — The analyser: The polarised light (intensity I₁, amplitude E₀) now meets a second filter — the analyser — whose transmission axis is rotated by angle θ from the polariser’s axis.

Step 3 — Vector resolution: Only the component of the electric field parallel to the analyser’s axis passes through. This component has amplitude E₀ cos θ. Since intensity is proportional to amplitude squared, the transmitted intensity is:

I = I₁ cos²θ

Combining both steps for an unpolarised source:

I = (I₀ / 2) cos²θ

where I = transmitted intensity (W m−²), I₀ = incident unpolarised intensity (W m−²), and θ = angle between the transmission axes of the polariser and analyser.

Fig 4 — Malus’s Law setup: unpolarised light I₀ passes through a polariser (emerging as I₁ = I₀/2), then through an analyser rotated at angle θ. The final transmitted intensity is I = I₁ cos²θ.
⚠️ The most common exam trap: If the question states the intensity of unpolarised light, you must include the factor of ½ before applying cos²θ. If the question gives intensity after the polariser (already polarised), use I = I₁ cos²θ directly. Forgetting the ½ is the single most dropped mark on polarisation questions.
✓ Exam strategy: Always ask yourself — “Is this light unpolarised or already polarised?” Write down I₁ = I₀/2 as a separate line before applying Malus’s Law. This earns you a method mark even if you make an arithmetic slip later.
I₁ = I₀ / 2 I = I₁ cos²θ I = (I₀/2) cos²θ Intensity ∝ Amplitude²
Graphical analysis

Graph 1: Transmitted Intensity vs Analyser Angle (I vs θ)

Plotting the transmitted intensity I against the analyser angle θ produces a cos²θ curve. The intensity reaches maximum values at θ = 0°, 180°, and 360° (axes aligned or anti-aligned) and falls to zero at θ = 90° and 270° (crossed polarisers).

Notice the curve is not linear: at θ = 45°, the intensity is 50% of maximum (since cos²45° = 0.5), not 50% of the way to zero. This non-linearity is a hallmark of the cos² relationship.

Fig 5 — I vs θ graph: a cos²θ curve with maxima at 0° and 180°, and zeros at 90° and 270°.
Fig 6 — Linearised graph: plotting I vs cos²θ gives a straight line through the origin with gradient equal to I₁, confirming direct proportionality.

Graph 2: Linearised Graph (I vs cos²θ)

To verify Malus’s Law experimentally, plot I on the y-axis against cos²θ on the x-axis. If the law holds, the result is a straight line through the origin. The gradient of this line equals I₁ (the intensity after the polariser). This linearisation technique is a very common exam question — you may be asked to determine the gradient and explain its physical significance.

💡 If an examiner asks you to “estimate the angle at which the intensity is 25% of maximum,” use cos²θ = 0.25, so cos θ = 0.5, giving θ = 60°. Likewise, 50% of maximum occurs at θ = 45° (since cos²45° = 0.5).
Polarisation by reflection

Glare and Polaroid Sunglasses

When unpolarised light reflects off a non-metallic surface such as water, glass, or a road, the reflected light becomes partially plane-polarised in the horizontal plane. This is the source of glare.

Polaroid sunglasses exploit this by having a vertical transmission axis. Since the glare is predominantly horizontally polarised, the vertical filter blocks most of it, reducing glare intensity dramatically.

At a specific angle of incidence called Brewster’s angle (θp = arctan n, where n is the refractive index), the reflected light is 100% horizontally polarised. At all other angles, the reflected light is only partially polarised.

Fig 7 — Polarisation by reflection: unpolarised light strikes a non-metallic surface. The reflected ray is partially polarised in the horizontal plane. At Brewster’s angle, the reflected ray is 100% horizontally polarised.
⚠️ Misconception alert: Reflected light is not always fully polarised — it is only partially polarised at most angles. Complete polarisation of the reflected ray occurs only at Brewster’s angle.
Extension — the three-filter paradox

Adding a Filter Can Increase Transmission

Consider two crossed polarisers (axes at 90°): no light passes through. Now insert a third filter between them at angle θ to the first polariser. Remarkably, light now passes through the system.

The middle filter rotates the plane of polarisation. The polarised light emerging from the first filter has a component along the middle filter’s axis (I₁ cos²θ). This re-polarised light then has a component along the final filter’s axis [cos²(90° − θ)]. The resulting intensity is:

Ifinal = (I₀ / 2) · cos²θ · cos²(90° − θ)

Maximum transmission occurs when the middle filter is at θ = 45°, giving Ifinal = I₀/8. This paradox beautifully illustrates that polarisation is about vector components, not simple “blocking.”

Fig 8 — The three-filter paradox: crossed polarisers block all light, but inserting a middle filter at 45° allows light through. The middle filter creates a new polarisation component that can pass the final filter.
Worked example

Worked Example — Unpolarised Light Through Two Filters

Unpolarised sunlight of intensity 950 W m⁻² passes through a vertical polariser. It then passes through an analyser rotated 30° from vertical. Calculate the final transmitted intensity.

1
Identify the known values: I₀ = 950 W m⁻² (unpolarised), θ = 30° (angle between transmission axes).
2
Apply the polariser rule: The light is unpolarised, so the polariser halves the intensity.
I₁ = I₀ / 2 = 950 / 2 = 475 W m⁻².
Do not apply cos²θ yet — that is only used at the analyser.
3
Apply Malus’s Law at the analyser:
I = I₁ cos²θ = 475 × cos²(30°).
cos 30° = √3/2 ≈ 0.866, so cos²30° = 0.75.
4
Calculate and state answer with units:
I = 475 × 0.75 = 356 W m⁻² (3 s.f.).
Common misconceptions — trap corner

Misconceptions to Avoid

❌ Misconception✅ Correct Physics
A polariser creates polarised lightA polariser filters existing light — it removes the component perpendicular to the transmission axis. It does not create new light.
Any wave can be polarisedOnly transverse waves can be polarised. Longitudinal waves (e.g. sound) cannot, because oscillations occur along the propagation direction only.
I = I₀ cos²θ (missing the ½)This is only correct if I₀ is already polarised. If I₀ is unpolarised, you must write I = (I₀/2) cos²θ.
Reflected light is always fully polarisedReflected light is only partially polarised at most angles. Full polarisation occurs only at Brewster’s angle (θp = arctan n).
Real-world application — LCD screens

How LCD Screens Use Polarisation

A liquid crystal display works by sandwiching liquid crystal material between two crossed polarisers. A backlight shines through the first (vertical) polariser, producing plane-polarised light. The liquid crystal layer can twist the plane of polarisation by a controllable amount when a voltage is applied across it.

When no voltage is applied, the liquid crystals twist the polarisation by 90°, allowing light to pass through the second (horizontal) polariser — the pixel appears bright. When voltage is applied, the crystals straighten, the plane is not rotated, and the crossed polariser blocks the light — the pixel appears dark. By varying the voltage, intermediate brightness levels are achieved.

Exam-style questions
Exam question · Explain
Explain, with reference to the nature of waves, why sound cannot be polarised but light can. [4 marks]
✓ Polarisation requires oscillations to occur in multiple planes perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer.
✓ Light is a transverse wave — its electric field oscillates perpendicular to the direction of propagation, so oscillations exist in many perpendicular planes.
✓ Sound is a longitudinal wave — its oscillations (compressions and rarefactions) occur parallel to the direction of propagation.
✓ Since sound oscillates in only one direction (along propagation), there is no perpendicular plane for a filter to select — hence sound cannot be polarised.

Exam question · Calculate
Unpolarised light of intensity 800 W m⁻² is incident on a system of three polarising filters. The first filter has a vertical transmission axis. The second filter has its axis at 30° to vertical. The third filter has its axis at 90° to vertical (horizontal). Calculate the final transmitted intensity. [4 marks]
✓ After first polariser: I₁ = I₀ / 2 = 800 / 2 = 400 W m⁻².
✓ After second filter (30° to first): I₂ = I₁ cos²(30°) = 400 × (√3/2)² = 400 × 0.75 = 300 W m⁻².
✓ Angle between second and third filters = 90° − 30° = 60°. After third filter: I₃ = I₂ cos²(60°) = 300 × (0.5)² = 300 × 0.25 = 75 W m⁻².
✓ Final transmitted intensity = 75 W m⁻².
