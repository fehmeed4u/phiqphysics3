Young's Double-Slit Experiment
How two slits, one wavelength, and a screen reveal the wave nature of light — deriving and applying s = λD / d

Why Should You Care?

Have you ever noticed the rainbow colours on a soap bubble or a thin oil film on a puddle? Those colours come from interference — the same wave behaviour that Thomas Young used in 1801 to prove that light is a wave. When two sets of waves overlap, they can reinforce or cancel each other, creating striking patterns of bright and dark bands called fringes.

Fig 1 — Thin-film interference in everyday life. The same principle — superposition of waves with different path lengths — underlies Young's experiment.

⚠️ Coherence trap: Two separate light bulbs are not coherent — their phase difference changes randomly billions of times per second, so no stable fringe pattern forms. You need a single source split into two beams (or a laser, which is inherently coherent).
How the experiment works

The Setup

Monochromatic light (a single wavelength) passes through a narrow single slit, which acts as a point-like coherent source. This light then hits a barrier with two narrow slits separated by a small distance d. Each slit diffracts the incoming wave, producing two overlapping sets of circular wavefronts that travel to a distant screen.

Fig 2 — Overview of Young's double-slit experiment. Light diffracts through both slits; where the two sets of waves overlap on the screen, constructive and destructive interference produce alternating bright and dark fringes.

Why Fringes Form

At any point on the screen, two waves arrive — one from each slit. Because the slits are at slightly different distances from that point, the waves have travelled different path lengths.

Where the waves arrive in phase (crest meets crest), they add up — constructive interference → bright fringe.

Where they arrive out of phase (crest meets trough), they cancel — destructive interference → dark fringe.

The path difference determines which type of interference occurs at each point on the screen.

Constructive Interference

Path difference = whole number of wavelengths

Δ = nλ   (n = 0, 1, 2, …)

Waves arrive in phase → amplitudes add → bright fringe.

Destructive Interference

Path difference = half-integer number of wavelengths

Δ = (n + ½)λ

Waves arrive in antiphase → amplitudes cancel → dark fringe.

💡 Intensity at bright fringes: When two equal waves interfere constructively, the resultant amplitude is doubled. Since intensity ∝ amplitude², the bright fringe intensity is 4× that of a single slit — not 2×. This is a common exam trap.
Fig 3 — Coherent versus incoherent sources. Only coherent waves (constant phase relationship) produce a stable interference pattern.
Deriving the fringe spacing formula

Geometry of the Double Slit

Consider two rays leaving the slits and meeting at the n-th bright fringe on the screen. The screen is at distance D from the slits, and the fringe is at distance sn from the central maximum.

The path difference between the two rays is:

path difference = d sin θ

For a bright fringe (constructive interference), this must equal a whole number of wavelengths:

d sin θ = nλ   (n = 0, ±1, ±2, …)

When the screen is far away (D ≫ d), the angle θ is small, so we can use the small-angle approximation:

sin θ ≈ tan θ = sn / D

Substituting into the constructive condition:

d · (sn / D) = nλ   →   sn = nλD / d

The spacing between adjacent bright fringes (fringe n and fringe n + 1) is:

s = λD / d

This is constant — the fringes are equally spaced, independent of the order n.

Fig 4 — Geometry of the double slit. The path difference between the two rays is d sin θ. For small angles, sin θ ≈ sn / D, which leads directly to the fringe spacing formula.

All Key Formulas

Constructive:   d sin θ = nλ   (n = 0, 1, 2, …)
Destructive:   d sin θ = (n + ½)λ
Fringe spacing:   s = λD / d
Small-angle approx:   sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ θ (rad)
s ∝ λ s ∝ D s ∝ 1/d
✓ Exam strategy — proportionality reasoning: Many IB questions ask "what happens to s if you double D and halve d?" You don't need to recalculate — just use the proportionalities: s doubles (from D) and doubles again (from 1/d) → s increases by a factor of 4.
⚠️ Unit conversion trap: Slit separation d is often given in mm, screen distance D in m, and wavelength λ in nm. Convert everything to metres before substituting into s = λD / d. Remember: 1 mm = 10⁻³ m, 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m.
Graphs you must know
Fig 5 — Intensity vs. position for double-slit interference. The peaks are equally spaced (spacing = s). For negligible slit width, all maxima have the same height. Read s directly as the distance between adjacent peaks.

s vs. λ (straight line)

Plotting fringe spacing against wavelength with fixed d and D gives a straight line through the origin.

Gradient = D / d

Use this to find D/d from experimental data by plotting s against λ for several different colours.

s vs. 1/d (straight line)

Plotting fringe spacing against the reciprocal of slit separation (with fixed λ and D) gives a straight line through the origin.

Gradient = λD

Use this linearisation to determine λ from a range of slit-separation measurements.

sin θn vs. fringe order n

Plotting sin θn (where θn = arctan(sn / D)) against fringe order n yields a straight line through the origin with gradient λ / d. This linearisation avoids measuring small fringe spacings directly and is useful in high-precision experiments.

HL extension — finite slit width

Real Slits Have Width

In a real experiment, each slit has a finite width a. This produces a single-slit diffraction envelope that modulates the double-slit fringes. Some bright fringes may be "missing" where a double-slit maximum coincides with a single-slit minimum.

The condition for missing orders occurs when the ratio d / a is an integer — the m-th double-slit maximum vanishes at the k-th single-slit minimum, where m / k = d / a.

Fig 6 — Ideal vs. real double-slit pattern. The dashed curve is the single-slit diffraction envelope (from slit width a). Where the envelope reaches zero, double-slit fringes vanish — these are "missing orders".
Worked example

Worked Example — Sodium Light Double-Slit Calculation

In a Young's double-slit experiment, light of wavelength 589 nm (sodium) passes through slits separated by 0.25 mm. The screen is 2.80 m away. Calculate the fringe spacing.

1
List the known values (convert to SI):
λ = 589 nm = 589 × 10⁻⁹ m
d = 0.25 mm = 0.25 × 10⁻³ m = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ m
D = 2.80 m
2
Select the equation:
s = λD / d
3
Substitute and solve:
s = (589 × 10⁻⁹ × 2.80) / (2.5 × 10⁻⁴)
s = (1.649 × 10⁻⁶) / (2.5 × 10⁻⁴)
s = 6.60 × 10⁻³ m
4
State the answer with units:
s = 6.60 mm

Follow-Up — Proportionality Reasoning

(b) If the experiment is repeated with blue light (λ = 450 nm), the fringe spacing decreases because s ∝ λ. Blue light has a shorter wavelength than sodium light, so the fringes become closer together.

(c) If the slit separation is doubled to 0.50 mm (same λ, same D), then since s ∝ 1/d, doubling d halves s. New fringe spacing = 6.60 / 2 = 3.30 mm.

💡 Examiner's insight: Part (b) is a "state and explain" question — you must give the direction of change AND link it to the formula or proportionality for full marks.
Common misconceptions

Trap Corner — Don't Fall For These

MisconceptionCorrect View
Two flashlights can produce interferenceThey are incoherent — the phase difference changes randomly. You need one source split into two beams.
Bright fringe intensity is 2× a single slitAmplitude doubles → intensity = amplitude² → bright fringe intensity is 4× a single slit.
Dark fringes always have zero intensityPerfect cancellation requires equal amplitudes from both slits. In practice, minima may not reach exactly zero.
s = λD / d works for any angleIt relies on the small-angle approximation (θ < ~10°). For large angles, use d sin θ = nλ directly.
Increasing D only affects brightnessIncreasing D does spread the light (dimmer), but it also increases fringe spacing (s ∝ D). These are separate effects.

Exam-style questions
Exam question · Calculate and explain
A student uses a green laser (λ = 532 nm) with double slits of separation d = 0.30 mm. The screen is 2.50 m away.

(a) Calculate the fringe spacing s. [2 marks]
(b)(i) The green laser is replaced with a red laser (λ = 650 nm). State whether the fringes become wider or narrower. [1 mark]
(b)(ii) Calculate the new fringe spacing. [2 marks]
(c) Explain why the fringes in (b)(ii) are still equally spaced. [1 mark] [6 marks]
(a) s = λD / d = (532 × 10⁻⁹ × 2.50) / (0.30 × 10⁻³) ✓
s = 4.43 × 10⁻³ m = 4.43 mm ✓

(b)(i) Wider ✓ (since λ increases and s ∝ λ)

(b)(ii) s = (650 × 10⁻⁹ × 2.50) / (0.30 × 10⁻³) ✓
s = 5.42 × 10⁻³ m = 5.42 mm ✓

(c) The formula s = λD / d gives a constant spacing that does not depend on the fringe order n ✓
Exam question · Analyse data
An intensity-vs-position graph for a double-slit experiment (D = 1.80 m) shows that the distance between the first minima on either side of the central maximum is 8.4 mm.

(a) Determine the fringe spacing s. [1 mark]
(b) If the slit separation d = 0.12 mm, calculate the wavelength of the light used. [2 marks]
(c) Sketch on the same axes the pattern expected if d were increased to 0.24 mm, with the same light and D. [2 marks] [5 marks]
(a) The distance between the first minima on either side = 2s, so s = 8.4 / 2 = 4.2 mm ✓

(b) Rearranging: λ = sd / D = (4.2 × 10⁻³ × 0.12 × 10⁻³) / 1.80 ✓
λ = 2.8 × 10⁻⁷ m = 280 nm (ultraviolet) ✓

(c) Doubling d halves the fringe spacing (s ∝ 1/d) → fringes are twice as close together ✓. Same central-peak height (assuming negligible slit width); pattern has more fringes visible in the same width of screen ✓.
Exam question · Explain
A student observes a double-slit interference pattern using white light instead of monochromatic light.

(a) Describe what the student sees at the central maximum. [1 mark]
(b) Describe and explain what the student sees away from the central maximum. [3 marks] [4 marks]
(a) The central maximum is white/bright ✓ (all wavelengths have zero path difference there and interfere constructively).

(b) Away from centre, the student sees coloured fringes ✓. Different wavelengths have different fringe spacings (s ∝ λ), so the bright fringes for each colour occur at slightly different positions ✓. The red fringes are spaced further apart than blue fringes, causing the colours to spread out. After a few orders the colours overlap and the pattern becomes washed out / white ✓.
