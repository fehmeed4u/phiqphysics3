Multiple Slits & Diffraction Gratings
From two slits to thousands — understanding how increasing N produces sharp, bright interference maxima and the power of the grating equation d sin θ = nλ.

🌈 Why Does a CD Spray Rainbows?

Hold a CD or DVD under a white light and tilt it. You see vivid rainbow colours fanning out across the surface. It is not a prism — there is no refraction through a material. The tightly spaced tracks on the disc (spacing ≈ 1.6 µm) act as a reflection diffraction grating. Each colour in white light constructively interferes at a different angle, splitting the light into a spectrum. By the end of this lesson you will be able to calculate exactly which colours appear at which angles.

Fig 1 — A CD acts as a reflection diffraction grating. The track spacing is about 1.6 µm, comparable to visible wavelengths, so each colour diffracts at a different angle producing the rainbow pattern.
From double slit to N slits

What Happens When You Add More Slits?

In double-slit interference you observe broad, evenly spaced bright fringes. Now imagine adding a third slit, then a fourth, then fifty. The key insight is that the positions of the bright fringes do not change — they are controlled only by the slit spacing d and the wavelength λ. What changes dramatically is the sharpness and brightness of each fringe.

As the number of slits N increases, the primary maxima become much narrower and much brighter (intensity scales as N²). The broad humps of the double-slit pattern are replaced by tall, needle-like peaks. Between them, small secondary maxima (N − 2 of them) appear but become negligibly faint for large N.

Fig 2 — As the number of slits N increases, the primary maxima stay at the same angular positions but become dramatically narrower and more intense. Secondary maxima between them shrink to insignificance.
💡 Key idea: More slits do not move the peaks — they sharpen them. The grating equation d sin θ = nλ is identical for 2 slits or 2000 slits. The slit spacing d has not changed; only the number of sources contributing has increased.

Primary vs Secondary Maxima

Primary (principal) maxima are the very bright, narrow fringes whose positions satisfy d sin θ = nλ. Their peak intensity is proportional to N², where N is the number of slits.

Secondary maxima are the tiny bumps that appear between adjacent primary maxima. There are exactly N − 2 secondary maxima between each pair of primary maxima. For a diffraction grating with hundreds of slits, these secondary maxima are negligible.

Primary maxima: N² intensity Secondary maxima: N − 2 between peaks Peak positions: unchanged by N
The diffraction grating

What Is a Diffraction Grating?

A diffraction grating is simply a very large number of equally spaced parallel slits — typically hundreds or thousands per millimetre. Because N is so large, the primary maxima are extremely sharp and bright, making the grating an incredibly precise tool for measuring wavelengths.

Gratings are specified by their line density, for example "600 lines per mm". The slit spacing (also called the grating constant) is d = 1 / (lines per metre). Converting correctly from lines/mm to lines/m is critical.

d = 1 / (lines per metre)
⚠️ Unit trap: If a grating has 500 lines/mm, you must convert to lines per metre before computing d. That means d = 1 / (500 × 10³) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ m. Writing d = 1/500 = 0.002 mm gives the wrong power of ten and will cost you marks.

Deriving the Grating Equation

Consider parallel monochromatic light hitting a grating at normal incidence. Each slit acts as a coherent source. For a primary maximum to form, the waves from every pair of adjacent slits must arrive in phase at the screen. This requires the path difference between adjacent slits to be a whole number of wavelengths.

From the geometry, the path difference between adjacent slits is d sin θ, where θ is the angle from the central axis. Setting this equal to nλ gives the grating equation:

d sin θ = nλ

Here d is the slit spacing, θ is the diffraction angle, n is the order number (0, 1, 2, …), and λ is the wavelength.

Fig 3 — Parallel rays arrive at a grating. The path difference between adjacent slits is d sin θ. For constructive interference this must equal nλ.
Key relationships & proportionalities

Reading the Grating Equation

From d sin θ = nλ, three important proportionalities follow (each holding the other variables constant):

sin θ ∝ n — Higher orders appear at larger angles. The spacing between orders is equal in sin θ, not in θ itself.

sin θ ∝ λ — Longer wavelengths (red) diffract to larger angles than shorter wavelengths (blue). This is why gratings spread white light into a spectrum with red on the outside.

sin θ ∝ 1/d — More lines per mm means a smaller d, which pushes maxima to wider angles. A finer grating spreads the spectrum more.

Maximum Observable Order

Since sin θ cannot exceed 1, there is a highest observable order for any grating and wavelength combination. Setting sin θ = 1 in the grating equation gives:

n_max = floor(d / λ)

You must round down to the nearest integer because sin θ = 1 corresponds to θ = 90°, which is the physical limit. Any order for which nλ/d would exceed 1 simply does not appear.

✓ Exam tip: When asked "determine the highest order visible", calculate d/λ and take the integer part. Always show the inequality sin θ ≤ 1 as justification — examiners award a mark for stating this condition explicitly.

Missing Orders

Each slit has a finite width b, so the overall pattern is the multi-slit interference pattern multiplied by the single-slit diffraction envelope. A primary maximum goes missing when it coincides with a minimum of the single-slit pattern.

Single-slit minima occur at b sin θ = mλ. Grating maxima occur at d sin θ = nλ. Dividing these two conditions:

n / m = d / b

So if d/b = 3, the orders n = 3, 6, 9, … will be missing. If d/b = 2, then n = 2, 4, 6, … are missing.

⚠️ Misconception: "If 2λ/d < 1 then the n = 2 order must be visible." Not necessarily! The single-slit diffraction envelope can suppress it. The grating equation is necessary but not sufficient — the single-slit pattern is the "boss of brightness."
Graphical analysis

The sin θ vs n Graph — Key Linearisation

Rearranging d sin θ = nλ gives sin θ = (λ/d) × n. Plotting sin θ on the y-axis against order number n on the x-axis gives a straight line through the origin with gradient = λ/d. This is the most common data-processing graph in grating questions.

If d is known, the gradient directly gives the wavelength: λ = gradient × d. The y-intercept should be zero; a non-zero intercept suggests systematic error in angle measurement.

Fig 6 — Plotting sin θ against n produces a straight line through the origin. The gradient equals λ/d, allowing precise determination of wavelength.

Applications

Spectroscopy — Identifying Elements

Diffraction gratings are the heart of the spectrometer, one of the most important instruments in science. When light from a hot gas passes through a grating, each element's unique emission wavelengths appear as discrete bright lines at specific angles. By measuring θ and applying d sin θ = nλ, the wavelengths — and therefore the elements — can be identified precisely.

This is how astronomers determine the chemical composition of distant stars, and how chemists analyse unknown samples. The sharper the lines (higher N), the more precisely two close wavelengths can be distinguished.

Fig 7 — Discrete emission lines from a gas discharge lamp viewed through a grating. Each line corresponds to a specific wavelength and can be measured precisely using d sin θ = nλ.
Trap corner — common misconceptions

❌ "More slits move the peaks"

The peak positions depend only on d and λ. Increasing N sharpens and brightens the peaks but does not change where they occur.

❌ "Small-angle approximation works"

Gratings produce large angles (30°, 50°, even 70°). The approximation sin θ ≈ θ fails badly. Always use sin θ exactly.

❌ "n = 0 shows a rainbow"

At n = 0 the path difference is zero for all wavelengths, so all colours overlap. The central maximum is always white.

❌ "More lines/mm = narrower spread"

It is the opposite. More lines/mm means a smaller d, which gives larger sin θ values and a wider angular spread of the spectrum.

Worked examples

Worked Example 1 — Finding Angles and Angular Separation

A diffraction grating has 500 lines per mm. Light of wavelength 550 nm is incident normally. (a) Calculate the angle of the first-order maximum. (b) Calculate the angle of the second-order maximum. (c) Determine the angular separation between the first and second orders.

1
Find the slit spacing d: d = 1 / (500 × 10³) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ m
2
First order (n = 1): sin θ₁ = nλ / d = (1 × 550 × 10⁻⁹) / (2.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.275 → θ₁ = sin⁻¹(0.275) = 16.0°
3
Second order (n = 2): sin θ₂ = (2 × 550 × 10⁻⁹) / (2.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.55 → θ₂ = sin⁻¹(0.55) = 33.4°
4
Angular separation: Δθ = 33.4° − 16.0° = 17.4°

Worked Example 2 — Highest Order and Missing Orders

Using the same grating (d = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ m) and wavelength (550 nm): (a) What is the highest observable order? (b) If each slit has width b = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ m, which order is missing?

1
Highest order: n_max = floor(d / λ) = floor(2.0 × 10⁻⁶ / 550 × 10⁻⁹) = floor(3.63) = 3
2
Missing order condition: n/m = d/b. Calculate d/b = (2.0 × 10⁻⁶) / (1.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 2. So the missing orders are n = 2, 4, 6, …
3
Conclusion: Since n_max = 3, the only missing order within the observable range is n = 2 (when m = 1). The n = 2 peak is absent from the pattern.
💡 Examiner note: When explaining missing orders, always state both conditions — the single-slit minimum (b sin θ = mλ) and the grating maximum (d sin θ = nλ) — then divide them to obtain d/b = n/m. This logical chain earns full marks.
d sin θ = nλ d = 1/(lines per m) n_max = floor(d/λ) Missing order: d/b = n/m Gradient of sin θ vs n = λ/d I_max ∝ N²

Exam-style questions
Exam question · Calculate & Determine
A diffraction grating has 300 lines per mm. Monochromatic light is normally incident on the grating. The first-order maximum is observed at θ = 10.2°.
(a) Calculate the slit spacing d in metres. [1]
(b) Calculate the wavelength of the light. [2]
(c) Determine the highest order maximum that can be observed. [2] [5 marks]
(a) d = 1 / (300 × 10³) = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m ✓

(b) λ = d sin θ / n = (3.33 × 10⁻⁶ × sin 10.2°) / 1 ✓
λ = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.1771 = 5.90 × 10⁻⁷ m = 590 nm ✓ (1 mark substitution, 1 mark answer)

(c) n_max = floor(d / λ) = floor(3.33 × 10⁻⁶ / 5.90 × 10⁻⁷) = floor(5.64) ✓
n_max = 5 ✓
Exam question · Explain & Calculate
A diffraction grating with 500 lines per mm is used to observe the spectrum of white light (400 nm – 700 nm).
(a) Calculate the angular position of the first-order maximum for red light (λ = 700 nm). [2]
(b) Calculate the angular position of the first-order maximum for violet light (λ = 400 nm). [1]
(c) Hence determine the angular width of the first-order visible spectrum. [1]
(d) Explain why the central maximum (n = 0) is white rather than a spectrum. [2] [6 marks]
(a) d = 1 / (500 × 10³) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ m.
sin θ = nλ/d = (1 × 700 × 10⁻⁹) / (2.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.350 ✓
θ_red = sin⁻¹(0.350) = 20.5° ✓

(b) sin θ = (1 × 400 × 10⁻⁹) / (2.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.200
θ_violet = sin⁻¹(0.200) = 11.5° ✓

(c) Angular width = 20.5° − 11.5° = 9.0° ✓

(d) At n = 0, the path difference between adjacent slits is zero for all wavelengths ✓. Therefore all colours constructively interfere at the same position (θ = 0°) and combine to give white light ✓.
Exam question · Explain
A student increases the number of slits N in a multi-slit experiment from 2 to 50, while keeping d and λ unchanged. Explain the effect on (a) the positions of the primary maxima and (b) the appearance of the primary maxima. [4 marks]
(a) The positions of the primary maxima do not change ✓, because the grating equation d sin θ = nλ depends only on d and λ, not on N ✓.

(b) The primary maxima become much narrower/sharper ✓ and much brighter (intensity increases as N², so from 4 units to 2500 units) ✓.
