Einstein's Postulates
Two simple statements — the laws of physics are the same in every inertial frame, and light always travels at c — force space and time to bend. This lesson works through the Lorentz transformations, clock synchronisation, the relativity of simultaneity, and how velocities add when nothing is allowed to exceed c.

🎯 Hook – the experiment that found nothing

In 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley built an instrument sensitive enough to detect tiny differences in the speed of light beams sent in different directions, expecting the Earth's motion through a hypothetical "luminiferous aether" to show up as a shift. It found nothing at all — the speed of light came back identical in every direction, no matter how the experiment was turned. Repeated tests since have never found a difference either.

Fig 1 — Albert Einstein in 1905, the year he published special relativity.
💡 Diagnostic question (think – then read on) — if there's no aether and no experiment can detect an observer's own absolute motion through space, what does that tell you about the idea of a single "correct" reference frame? There isn't one. Every inertial frame's measurements are equally valid — none is privileged as the "truly stationary" one.

📖 The two postulates

Two problems faced classical physics at the end of the nineteenth century: Maxwell's electromagnetism contradicted classical mechanics in places, and no experiment could show light travelling at different speeds depending on direction relative to the (rotating, orbiting) Earth. Einstein resolved both with special relativity in 1905, built on two postulates. It is "special" because it is restricted to inertial frames of reference.

📖 First postulate — the laws of physics are identical (invariant) in all inertial reference frames. No experiment can reveal an observer's absolute velocity through the Universe; the Universe has no unique stationary frame.
📖 Second postulate — the speed of light in a vacuum, \(c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\), is the same constant in all inertial reference frames, regardless of the motion of the source or the observer.

The effects of special relativity only become significant at speeds close to \(c\) — nothing in daily life reveals them. For the constancy of \(c\) to hold when observers move relative to each other, space and time themselves must behave differently than everyday experience suggests: since speed = distance/time, and observers in relative motion can measure different distances between the same two events, they must also measure different time intervals. Time is not an invariant quantity. Space and time are linked as a single entity — spacetime.

📐 The Lorentz transformation equations

Newtonian physics uses Galilean transformations to convert coordinates between frames. Relativity needs the Lorentz transformation instead. If an event has coordinates \((x,t)\) in frame S, and frame S′ moves at velocity \(v\) relative to S along the \(x\)-axis (origins coincident at \(t=0\)), the same event has coordinates \((x',t')\) in S′:

Lorentz transformation $$x' = \gamma(x - vt)$$ $$t' = \gamma\left(t - \frac{vx}{c^2}\right)$$
Lorentz factor $$\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\dfrac{v^2}{c^2}}}$$

\(\gamma\) is dimensionless and always \(\geq 1\). For everyday speeds \(v \ll c\), \(\gamma \approx 1\) and relativistic effects vanish; as \(v \to c\), \(\gamma\) grows without limit. For differences between two events — \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta t\) — the same equations apply to the intervals directly:

Interval form $$\Delta x' = \gamma(\Delta x - v\Delta t)$$ $$\Delta t' = \gamma\left(\Delta t - \frac{v\Delta x}{c^2}\right)$$

🌌 Astronomical units: the light-year

Astronomical distances are huge, so astronomers use the light-year (ly) — the distance light travels in one year:

$$1\ \text{ly} = (3.00\times10^{8})\times 365\times24\times3600 = 9.46\times10^{15}\ \text{m}$$

(Exactly defined as 9 460 730 472 580 800 m.) The nearest star beyond the Sun, Alpha Proxima, is \(4.02\times10^{16}\) m away — or 4.25 ly. Working in light-years lets \(c\) cancel neatly in relativity calculations, which is why it appears throughout the worked examples below.

⏱️ Clock synchronisation and the relativity of simultaneity

Comparing event times at different locations in one frame requires synchronized clocks. The simplest method: flash a light exactly midway between two clocks and start both when the flash arrives. If the clocks are unequal distances \(L_1\), \(L_2\) from the source, set them to \(L_1/c\) and \(L_2/c\) before starting, so both correctly reach the same reading when their flash arrives.

Fig 3 — A flash from the centre of a train carriage (Event 0) reaches both ends and reflects back (Events 1, 2) to the origin (Event 3).

An observer on the train (frame S) sees the pulses sent out, arriving at each end, and returning — all simultaneously. An observer on the ground (frame S′) watching the same train moving past sees something different: the pulse travelling backward against the carriage's motion reaches its end (Event 1) before the forward-travelling pulse reaches its end (Event 2), because it has less distance to cover at the same speed \(c\). The return pulses, however, are still seen arriving back together.

✓ Rule: if two events happen at the same place and the same time, they are simultaneous for every observer in every frame. If they happen at different places, simultaneity is frame-dependent — one observer's "at the same time" can be another observer's "one after the other".

➕ Relativistic velocity addition

Galilean relativity simply subtracts velocities: \(u' = u - v\). That fails once speeds approach \(c\), because it could predict speeds above \(c\). The Lorentz transformations lead instead to the relativistic velocity addition equation:

Velocity addition $$u' = \frac{u-v}{1-\dfrac{uv}{c^2}}$$

\(u\) is an object's velocity measured in frame S; \(u'\) is the same object's velocity as measured in frame S′, which moves at velocity \(v\) relative to S. However large \(u\) and \(v\) are, \(u'\) always comes out below \(c\). When \(u\) and \(v\) are both small compared with \(c\), the equation reduces to the familiar Galilean form.

✍️ Worked example 1 – transforming an event's coordinates

(a) Calculate the Lorentz factor for a relative speed of \(1.50\times10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) (0.50c). (b) An event in reference frame S occurs at \(x=5000\) m and \(t=2.0\) s. Calculate where and when the same event occurs as observed from a rocket (frame S′) with relative velocity 0.50c. (c) State the assumption needed for part (b).

1
Identify: use \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}\), then the Lorentz transformation \(x'=\gamma(x-vt)\), \(t'=\gamma(t-vx/c^2)\).
2
Data: \(v=1.50\times10^{8}\) m s⁻¹, \(x=5000\) m, \(t=2.0\) s, \(c=3.00\times10^{8}\) m s⁻¹.
3
Equation:
\(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-(1.50\times10^8)^2/(3.00\times10^8)^2}\)
\(= 1.15\)
\(x' = 1.15\times(5000 - 1.50\times10^8\times2.0)\)
\(t' = 1.15\times(2.0 - 0.50\times3.00\times10^8\times5000/(3.00\times10^8)^2)\)
4
Answer: \(x' \approx -3.4\times10^{8}\ \text{m}\), \(t' = 2.3\ \text{s}\).
(c) It was assumed the two frames' origins coincided at \(t=0\) s.

✍️ Worked example 2 – using light-years to simplify the algebra

According to an Earth-based observer (frame S) a rocket reaches a point 20 ly away after 30 years: \((x,t)=(20\ \text{ly},\,30\ \text{y})\). Frame S′ moves at 0.50c relative to S, origins coincident at \(t=0\). Determine the rocket's coordinates according to an observer in S′.

1
Identify: \(\gamma=1.15\) for \(v=0.50c\) (as in Worked example 1). Using light-years and years lets \(c\) cancel.
2
Data: \(x=20\) ly, \(t=30\) y, \(v=0.50c\), \(\gamma=1.15\).
3
Equation:
\(x' = 1.15\times[20 - (0.50c\times30\,\text{y})]\)
\(= 1.15\times(20-15)\ \text{ly}\)
\(t' = 1.15\times(30 - 0.50c\times20\,\text{ly}/c^2)\)
\(= 1.15\times(30-10)\ \text{y}\)
4
Answer: \(x' = 5.8\ \text{ly}\), \(t' = 23\ \text{y}\). So in S′ the rocket has travelled only 5.8 ly in 23 years — a speed of just 0.25c.

✍️ Worked example 3 – an event in SI units

One observer records an event at \(x=250\) m and \(t=1.7\times10^{-6}\) s. Determine the coordinates of this event as recorded by a second observer travelling at 0.75c relative to the first, frames coincident at \(t=0\).

1
Identify: Lorentz transformation with \(v=0.75c\).
2
Data: \(x=250\) m, \(t=1.7\times10^{-6}\) s, \(v=0.75c\).
\(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.75^2} = 1.51\)
3
Equation:
\(x' = 1.51\times(250 - [0.75\times3.00\times10^8\times1.7\times10^{-6}])\)
\(t' = 1.51\times(1.7\times10^{-6} - 0.75\times3.00\times10^8\times250/(3.00\times10^8)^2)\)
4
Answer: \(x' = -200\ \text{m}\), \(t' = 1.6\times10^{-6}\ \text{s}\).

✍️ Worked example 4 – a spacecraft passing a space-station

A spacecraft travelling at 0.6c passes a space-station; clocks in both locations are set to zero as it passes. A short time later the spacecraft passes a point 3000 m from the station's origin, at station-frame time \(t=1.67\times10^{-5}\) s. Determine what time a clock on the spacecraft records for this event.

1
Identify: \(t' = \gamma(t-vx/c^2)\), with \(x\), \(t\) given in the station's frame.
2
Data: \(v=0.6c\), \(x=3000\) m, \(t=1.67\times10^{-5}\) s.
\(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.6^2}=1.25\)
3
Equation:
\(t' = 1.25\times(1.67\times10^{-5} - 0.6\times3.00\times10^8\times3000/(3.00\times10^8)^2)\)
4
Answer: \(t' = 1.34\times10^{-5}\ \text{s}\).

✍️ Worked example 5 – velocity addition, the symmetric case

Two particles are seen from an external reference frame travelling towards each other, each at 0.60c (Fig 6). An observer riding with one particle measures the speed of the other. What do they record?

1
Identify: \(u' = (u-v)/(1-uv/c^2)\), with \(u=-0.60c\) (the other particle, external frame) and \(v=+0.60c\) (the observer's own frame, relative to the external frame).
2
Data: \(u=-0.60c\), \(v=0.60c\).
3
Equation:
\(u' = \dfrac{-0.60c-0.60c}{1-\dfrac{(-0.60c)(0.60c)}{c^2}} = \dfrac{-1.2c}{1.36}\)
4
Answer: \(u' = -0.88c\) — magnitude 0.88c. Note that simple Galilean addition would (wrongly) give 1.2c. It is easy to lose a negative sign in this calculation; \(u\) and \(v\) are vectors.

✍️ Worked example 6 – velocity addition, the asymmetric case

Two rockets travel the same direction through empty space, seen from an external frame S: the first at 0.75c, a second (behind it) at 0.95c. Calculate what an observer riding the first rocket measures for the second rocket's approach speed.

1
Identify: \(u'=(u-v)/(1-uv/c^2)\) with \(u=0.95c\) (second rocket, frame S) and \(v=0.75c\) (first rocket's own frame, relative to S).
2
Data: \(u=0.95c\), \(v=0.75c\).
3
Equation:
\(u' = \dfrac{0.95c-0.75c}{1-\dfrac{0.95c\times0.75c}{c^2}} = \dfrac{0.20c}{0.2875}\)
4
Answer: \(u' = 0.69c\).

🌍 Real-world: measuring the cosmos in light-years

Every distance you'll meet in astronomy — to the nearest star, across the Milky Way, to another galaxy — is quoted in light-years precisely because using c-based units keeps the Lorentz factor's algebra clean: a "vx/c²" term collapses into ordinary years once x is in light-years. Astronomers also use the parsec for the same reason, covered in Topic E.5.

✓ Examiner tip: if a question gives you distances in light-years and times in years, don't convert to metres and seconds first — leave them as they are and let \(c\) cancel. Converting needlessly is the most common way marks are lost through arithmetic slips in this topic.

🚀 HL Extension – can two light beams have a "relative speed" of 2c?

Two light beams travelling directly towards each other are measured by a lab observer to close the gap between them at 2c. This is not forbidden — a closing speed between two separately-measured objects is not the speed of any single object, so it can exceed c.

Challenge: what happens if you try to find the closing speed "as measured by a photon" using the velocity addition equation? Substituting \(u=v=c\) gives \(u'=(c-(-c))/(1-c\cdot(-c)/c^2) = 2c/2 = c\) — light is still measured at exactly c. But the question is a trick: a photon cannot have a valid rest frame at all, since \(\gamma\to\infty\) at \(v=c\), so "as measured by a photon" is not physically meaningful.

A star-explosion event occurs at a point 20 light-years from Earth; according to the Earth-based observer, the rocket passes the Earth 20 years before the light from the explosion arrives. Determine the \(x'\) and \(t'\) coordinates of the explosion event for the observer in the rocket's frame. [5 marks]

✓ t′ = 1.15 × (0 − 0.50c × 20 ly/c²) = 1.15 × (−10 y) = −11.5 y.
✓ So the rocket's observer records the explosion happening 11.5 years before the rocket passed Earth.

Q5. From Earth, the Milky Way is measured to be 100 000 light-years in diameter. Calculate the diameter of the Milky Way for an observer in a distant galaxy moving at 0.2c away from Earth, in the same plane as the measured diameter. [3 marks]
✓ γ = 1/√(1 − 0.2²) = 1.021.
✓ The 100 000 ly figure is the proper length (measured at rest relative to the galaxy); a moving observer measures a shorter length, L = L₀/γ.
✓ L = 100 000/1.021 ≈ 9.8 × 10⁴ ly.

Q6. According to Earth-based astronomers a star near the centre of the Milky Way exploded 800 years before a second star, 2000 ly beyond it, also exploded. Determine how much later the second explosion is according to a rocket travelling towards the explosions at 0.2c. [4 marks]
✓ γ = 1/√(1 − 0.2²) = 1.021. Take Δx = 2000 ly, Δt = 800 y, v = +0.2c (rocket moving toward both explosions, same direction as the spatial separation).
✓ vΔx/c² = 0.2 × 2000 ly/c = 0.2 × 2000 y = 400 y (using ly/c = 1 year).
✓ Δt′ = γ(Δt − vΔx/c²) = 1.021 × (800 − 400) = 1.021 × 400.
✓ Δt′ ≈ 408 y.

Q7. In a laboratory an electron travels at 0.9c. According to the laboratory observer, at \(t \approx 9.6\times10^{-9}\) s it is at \(x=2.6\) m along a vacuum tube. Calculate the Lorentz factor and use it to find the time and position of the electron according to an observer in the electron's own frame. [4 marks]

Q9. Rachel rides in a rocket carrying a light clock — two mirrors M1, M2 separated by distance \(d\), reflecting a light pulse between them. Mateo watches the rocket fly past at speed \(v\). (a) Sketch or describe Rachel's view of the light path compared with Mateo's. (b) According to Mateo, how far does the rocket move sideways while the pulse travels from M1 to M2, in a time \(\Delta t\)? (c) Using a (incorrect) Galilean transformation, what would you naively expect for the speed of the light pulse according to Mateo? (d) Using Newtonian geometry, how far does the light have to travel between M1 and M2 according to Mateo? (e) Using the correct postulate instead, express the distance the light travels according to Mateo in terms of \(c\) and \(\Delta t\). (f) If Rachel measures a time \(\Delta t'\) for the same trip, use Pythagoras to derive an expression for \(\Delta t\) in terms of \(\Delta t'\), \(v\) and \(c\). (g) Explain, in terms of the constancy of \(c\), why the two observers must disagree about the time taken. [8 marks]
✓ (a) Rachel (moving with the clock) sees the light go straight up and down, path length \(d\). Mateo (watching it move past) sees the light trace a diagonal zig-zag, since the rocket carries the mirrors sideways while the light is in flight.
✓ (b) The rocket moves \(v\Delta t\) sideways in time \(\Delta t\).
✓ (c) Adding the vertical speed \(c\) and horizontal speed \(v\) as vectors would (wrongly) suggest a speed of \(\sqrt{c^2+v^2}\) — different from \(c\), contradicting postulate 2.
✓ (d) By Pythagoras, the diagonal distance is \(\sqrt{d^2+(v\Delta t)^2}\).
✓ (e) Since \(c\) must be the same for every observer, the distance travelled according to Mateo is simply \(c\Delta t\).
✓ (f) \(d=c\Delta t'\) (Rachel's straight path). Equating: \((c\Delta t)^2 = (c\Delta t')^2+(v\Delta t)^2\), so \(\Delta t^2(c^2-v^2)=c^2\Delta t'^2\), giving \(\Delta t = \gamma\Delta t'\) (this general result is developed fully as time dilation in Lesson 04).
✓ (g) Both observers must measure the same speed \(c\) for light, but Mateo sees it travel a longer (diagonal) path than Rachel's straight vertical path; since speed = distance/time is fixed at \(c\) for both, the longer path must take the longer time — so Mateo records the larger interval.

Q17. In a space race two spaceships pass a mark, measured by race officials to be travelling in the same direction at 0.6c and 0.7c respectively. According to the faster spaceship, calculate how fast the other ship is moving. [3 marks]
✓ \(u=0.6c\) (slower ship, lab frame), \(v=0.7c\) (faster ship's own frame).
✓ \(u' = (0.6c-0.7c)/(1-0.6\times0.7) = -0.1c/0.58\).
✓ \(u' \approx -0.17c\) — the slower ship appears to fall behind (recede) at 0.17c.
