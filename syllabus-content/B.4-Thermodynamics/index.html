First Law of Thermodynamics
Heat engines built the modern world by turning a flow of thermal energy into useful mechanical work. This lesson gives you the accounting system behind every one of them — conservation of energy applied to a gas.

🚂 Hook – the machine that ended muscle power

Burning fuel to heat water into steam, and using that steam to make something move, had been understood for a very long time — but it took until the early eighteenth century for the first commercial steam engines to appear. About 100 years later, in 1825, George Stephenson built the engine Locomotion for the first public steam railway. About 200 years on, our world runs on devices that get useful mechanical work from a flow of thermal energy — heat engines in cars, boats, trains, planes, factories and power stations.

Fig 1 — Locomotion, George Stephenson's engine for the first public steam railway, opened in the UK in 1825.
💡 Diagnostic question (think – then read on) — every heat engine needs a continuous fuel supply to keep running. Why can't a single burst of thermal energy keep an engine going forever? An engine converts a flow of thermal energy into work as it happens — once the fuel stops supplying that flow, there is no more thermal energy arriving to do further work, whatever energy the system already has stored as internal energy.

🌍 Heat engines and the birth of thermodynamics

We are surrounded by devices that get useful mechanical work from a flow of thermal energy transferred from a burning fuel — usually a fossil fuel. It is difficult to overstate the importance these devices have had on modern life. We are also very aware of the problems they bring: limited fossil-fuel resources, inefficient devices, pollution and global warming.

Fig 2 — Heat engines everywhere: cars, planes, power stations. All of them transfer thermal energy from a fuel to do useful mechanical work.

This branch of physics is thermodynamics. It grew out of a need to understand heat engines, but it now underpins a much wider understanding of internal energy, heat, temperature, work and pressure, and how they connect to the microscopic behaviour of particles.

🔗 Linking question: what paradigm shifts enabling changes to human society, such as harnessing the power of steam, can be attributed to advancements in physics understanding?

📦 Systems, surroundings and reservoirs

A system is simply the thing we are studying — in this topic, a gas. A closed system allows energy to be transferred in or out as heat or work, but no mass can cross its boundary. Compare this with an isolated system, in which neither mass nor energy can be transferred in or out — the kind of system used when discussing conservation of momentum.

The surroundings are everything else — the gas container and the rest of the Universe. If part of the surroundings is deliberately designed to encourage thermal energy to flow into or out of it, we call it a (thermal) reservoir: a part of the surroundings kept at approximately constant temperature.

Fig 3 — Gas in a cylinder with a movable piston. The piston is gas-tight (no gas can pass) and slides without friction.

A thermodynamic system can be as complex as a rocket engine, planet Earth or a human body — but here we develop understanding using the behaviour of gases in heat engines.

⚙️ Work done when a gas expands: W = PΔV

First consider a gas expanding with no change in pressure — an isobaric change (\(\Delta P = 0\)). If the gas in the cylinder is given thermal energy, its pressure momentarily exceeds the pressure of the surroundings, so it pushes the piston outward, keeping the two pressures equal as it expands.

Fig 4 — Gas expanding in a cylinder. The piston moves outward by \(\Delta s\) while the pressure \(P\) stays the same.

Work done by the gas in pushing back the surrounding air (assuming no friction):

$$\text{work done by gas} = \text{force} \times \text{distance moved}$$
$$= (PA)\Delta s$$

because force = pressure × area. Since the change of volume \(\Delta V = A\Delta s\):

Work done when a gas changes volume at constant pressure $$W = P\Delta V$$

If work is done on the gas to reduce its volume, \(\Delta V\) and \(W\) both take negative values.

Fig 5 — Expansion of a gas from 0.020 m³ to 0.050 m³ at a constant pressure of 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa. The shaded rectangle is the work done by the gas.

For this example:

$$W = P\Delta V = (1.0 \times 10^{5})(0.050 - 0.020)$$
$$= 3.0 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J}$$
📐 The work done, \(P\Delta V\), is numerically equal to the area under a PV diagram — true for every thermodynamic process, whatever the shape of the graph. This is one reason PV diagrams are so widely used.

⚖️ First law of thermodynamics: Q = ΔU + W

If an amount of thermal energy, \(+Q\), is transferred into a system, the system may gain internal energy, \(+\Delta U\), and/or expand and do work on the surroundings, \(+W\). Conservation of energy connects these three quantities:

First law of thermodynamics · thermal energy supplied to a gas $$Q = \Delta U + W$$

This equation covers every possibility of expanding or compressing a gas, and/or supplying or removing thermal energy from it.

⚠️ Common mistake – sign conventions. Thermal energy transferred into the gas is \(+Q\), removed is \(-Q\). An increase in internal energy is \(+\Delta U\), a decrease is \(-\Delta U\). Work done by the gas during expansion is \(+W\), work done on the gas during compression is \(-W\).

✏️ Top tip! When applying the first law, show each energy in brackets with its sign, e.g. \((+120)\), \((-100)\) — it makes errors much easier to catch.
1
Worked example B4.2 – Identify: 80 J of work was done by a gas when 120 J of thermal energy was transferred to it. Find the change in internal energy.
2
Data: \(Q = (+120)\) J (into gas), \(W = (+80)\) J (by gas, expansion).
3
Equation: \(Q = \Delta U + W\)
\((+120) = \Delta U + (+80)\)
4
Answer: \(\Delta U = (+40)\) J. The positive sign shows the internal energy increased.
1
Worked example B4.3 – Identify: 150 J of work was done when a gas was compressed. At the same time its internal energy increased by 50 J. Find the thermal energy transferred.
2
Data: \(\Delta U = (+50)\) J, \(W = (-150)\) J (compression, so negative).
3
Equation: \(Q = \Delta U + W\)
\(Q = (+50) + (-150)\)
4
Answer: \(Q = (-100)\) J. The negative sign shows the transfer was out of the gas.

🌡️ Internal energy of an ideal monatomic gas

The internal energy of an ideal monatomic gas can be calculated from:

Internal energy · ideal monatomic gas $$U = \tfrac{3}{2}Nk_{\text{B}}T = \tfrac{3}{2}nRT$$

so changes in internal energy can be calculated from:

Change in internal energy $$\Delta U = \tfrac{3}{2}Nk_{\text{B}}\Delta T = \tfrac{3}{2}nR\Delta T$$
1
Worked example B4.4 – Identify: using worked example B4.3, if the gas contained \(3.4 \times 10^{23}\) particles, find the temperature rise. Assume an ideal monatomic gas.
2
Data: \(\Delta U = 50\) J, \(N = 3.4 \times 10^{23}\), \(k_{\text{B}} = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J K}^{-1}\).
3
Equation: \(\Delta U = \tfrac{3}{2}Nk_{\text{B}}\Delta T\)
\(50 = 1.5 \times (3.4\times10^{23}) \times (1.38\times10^{-23}) \times \Delta T\)
4
Answer: \(\Delta T = 7.1\ \text{K}\).

📈 Reading work done off a PV diagram

Straight sloped lineV / m³ · P / PaStraight line, P and V both changingArea = rectangle + triangle under the line; add the two areas

Work done when a gas changes pressure and/or volume can always be determined from the area under a PV diagram, whatever its shape.

Fig 6 — Straight-line PV graph. The shaded area splits into a rectangle plus a triangle.
Fig 7 — Curved PV graph. The shaded area under the curve must be estimated.

Determine the work done in the two changes of state shown in Figs 6 and 7.

2
Data: \(P\) from \(3.9\) to \(2.1 \times 10^{5}\) Pa, \(V\) from \(0.017\) to \(0.038\) m³.
3

\(\left[\tfrac12(3.9-2.1)\times10^{5}\times(0.038-0.017)\right]\)
\(+\left[(0.038-0.017)\times2.1\times10^{5}\right]\)

📉 For Fig 7, the graph is curved, so the area must be estimated (see the strip method below). In this case the gas expands, so the work is done by the gas.

🧮 Estimating the area under a curved PV graph

When the PV graph is curved, the exact area cannot be read from a simple shape. Divide it into narrow strips of equal width and treat each strip as a thin rectangle or trapezium; the total area is the sum of the strips.

Fig 8 — Estimating the area under a curved graph using equal-width strips.
✓ Examiner tip: an equal-area rectangle drawn by eye over the whole curve is quicker than strips and is accurate enough for most exam questions — its height is the average pressure \(P_{\text{av}}\).

🌍 Real-world: the cost of a heat-engine world

Every heat engine that powers a car, plane, ship or power station needs a continuous transfer of thermal energy from a burning fuel — almost always a fossil fuel. That dependence is why heat engines sit at the centre of the climate and resource problems discussed in Topic B.2: fossil fuels are finite, real engines are never perfectly efficient, and burning fuel produces pollution and greenhouse gases.

✓ Examiner tip: "thermodynamics" questions about efficiency or emissions expect you to link the physics (\(Q = \Delta U + W\), energy always conserved) to the practical limits of real engines — not just recite the equation.

🚀 HL extension – why "monatomic" matters

\(\Delta U = \tfrac{3}{2}Nk_{\text{B}}\Delta T\) only holds for an ideal monatomic gas, where all the internal energy is translational kinetic energy of single atoms. A diatomic or polyatomic molecule can also rotate and vibrate, storing extra internal energy at the same temperature rise — so its internal energy changes by more than \(\tfrac{3}{2}Nk_{\text{B}}\Delta T\) for the same \(\Delta T\).

Challenge: for the same \(\Delta T\) and the same number of particles, would a diatomic gas need more or less thermal energy than a monatomic gas to produce that rise, at constant volume? (Answer: more — some of the energy supplied goes into rotational and vibrational modes as well as translational kinetic energy, so \(\Delta U\), and hence \(Q\), is larger for the same \(\Delta T\).)

Q1. The volume of a gas expanded from 43 cm³ to 51 cm³ while its pressure remained constant at 1.1 × 10⁵ Pa. (a) Calculate the work done. (b) State whether work was done on the gas, or by the gas. [2 marks]
✓ (a) \(W = P\Delta V = (1.1\times10^{5})(51-43)\times10^{-6} = 0.88\ \text{J}\).
✓ (b) The volume increased, so the work was done by the gas.
Exam question · Calculate
Q2. 0.84 J of work was done on a gas of volume 7.6 × 10⁻⁵ m³. If the pressure was constant at 1.4 × 10⁵ Pa, calculate the new volume. [3 marks]
✓ Work done on the gas, so \(W = -0.84\) J: \(-0.84 = 1.4\times10^{5} \times \Delta V\).
✓ \(\Delta V = -6.0\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3\) (the gas is compressed).
✓ New volume \(= 7.6\times10^{-5} - 6.0\times10^{-6} = 7.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^3\).

Q3. Fig 9 shows how the volume of a gas changed as the pressure on it was increased, from 600 kPa at 300 cm³ to 200 kPa at 900 cm³. Show that the work done during the expansion was approximately 230 J. [3 marks]
Fig 9 — PV graph for a gas.
✓ Estimate the area under the curve using an equal-area rectangle of height roughly 380 kPa (the average pressure).
✓ Width of the rectangle \(= (900-300) = 600\ \text{cm}^3 = 6.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3\).
✓ \(W \approx (3.8\times10^{5})(6.0\times10^{-4}) \approx 230\ \text{J}\), as required.
Exam question · Calculate
Q4. 200 J of work was done when a gas expanded at constant temperature. How much thermal energy was transferred? [2 marks]
✓ Constant temperature means \(\Delta U = 0\) (for an ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature).
✓ \(Q = \Delta U + W = 0 + (+200) = +200\ \text{J}\) transferred into the gas.
Exam question · Calculate
Q5. 70 J of work was done while a gas was compressed. At the same time 30 J of thermal energy was transferred out of the gas. Calculate the change in internal energy. [2 marks]
✓ \(Q = (-30)\) J, \(W = (-70)\) J (compression).
✓ \(Q = \Delta U + W \Rightarrow (-30) = \Delta U + (-70)\)
✓ \(\Delta U = (+40)\ \text{J}\); the internal energy increased.

Q6. 50 J of thermal energy was removed from a gas and its internal energy decreased by 10 J. (a) How much work was done? (b) Was the work done on the gas, or by the gas? [3 marks]

✓ (b) The negative sign shows the work was done on the gas.
Exam question · Calculate
Q7. An ideal monatomic gas containing 3.4 × 10²⁴ particles was heated from 297 K to 348 K. (a) What was the change in internal energy of the gas? (b) If, in this time, 5600 J of thermal energy was supplied to the gas, what was the amount of work done by, or on, the gas? [4 marks]
✓ (a) \(\Delta U = \tfrac32Nk_{\text{B}}\Delta T = 1.5\times(3.4\times10^{24})\times(1.38\times10^{-23})\times(348-297)\)
✓ \(\Delta U = 3.6\times10^{3}\ \text{J}\).
✓ (b) \(Q = \Delta U + W \Rightarrow 5600 = 3600 + W\)
✓ \(W = +2.0\times10^{3}\ \text{J}\), done by the gas (it expanded).
Exam question · Calculate
Q8. The internal energy of 3.2 moles of some helium gas, at an initial temperature of 302 K, rose by 540 J. (a) What was the final temperature of the gas? (b) If, at the same time, the gas expanded from a volume of 220 cm³ to 380 cm³ at constant pressure of 2.3 × 10⁵ Pa, how much work was done? (c) How much thermal energy was transferred? [6 marks]
✓ (a) \(\Delta U = \tfrac32nR\Delta T \Rightarrow 540 = 1.5\times3.2\times8.31\times\Delta T\)
✓ \(\Delta T = 13.5\ \text{K}\), so final temperature \(= 302+13.5 = 316\ \text{K}\).
✓ (b) \(W = P\Delta V = (2.3\times10^{5})(380-220)\times10^{-6} = 37\ \text{J}\).
✓ (c) \(Q = \Delta U + W = 540 + 37\)
✓ \(Q = 577\ \text{J}\) transferred into the gas.
Exam question · Calculate
Q9. During an investigation of how the volume of a fixed mass of air changed with temperature at constant atmospheric pressure (1.0 × 10⁵ Pa), the volume increased from 2.3 cm³ to 3.1 cm³. (a) Calculate how much work was done. (b) Was the work done on the gas, or by the gas? (c) The expansion happened when 0.150 J of thermal energy was transferred into the system. (a) Represent this process on a PV diagram. (b) Indicate the work done during this process on your drawing. (c) Calculate the work done. (d) Determine the change in internal energy of the gas. Exam question · Describe / Estimate / Determine
Q11. Fig 10 shows work done to change the state of a gas from point B (15 cm³, 3.7 × 10⁵ Pa) to point A (42 cm³, 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa). (a) Describe the process. (b) Did the process obey Boyle's law? (c) What has happened to the temperature of the gas? Explain your answer. (d) Estimate the work done during the process. (e) During the process, 1.9 J of thermal energy flowed out of the gas. Determine the change in its internal energy. [7 marks]
Fig 10 — PV graph, from point B to point A.
✓ (a) The gas expands from B to A, and its pressure falls at the same time.
✓ (b) \(P_{\text{B}}V_{\text{B}} = 3.7\times10^{5}\times15 = 5.6\times10^{6}\); \(P_{\text{A}}V_{\text{A}} = 1.0\times10^{5}\times42 = 4.2\times10^{6}\) — not equal, so Boyle's law (constant temperature) was not obeyed.
✓ (c) \(PV\) fell, and \(PV \propto T\) for a fixed amount of gas, so the temperature decreased.
✓ (d) Estimate the area under the curve between B and A, e.g. using an equal-area rectangle of height roughly \(2.0\times10^{5}\) Pa over a width of \((42-15)\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3\): \(W \approx 5.4\ \text{J}\), done by the gas.
✓ (e) \(Q = \Delta U + W \Rightarrow (-1.9) = \Delta U + 5.4\)
✓ \(\Delta U \approx -7.3\ \text{J}\); the internal energy decreased, consistent with the fall in temperature.
Exam question · Calculate
Q12. 1.74 mol of an ideal monatomic gas expanded and during the process its temperature changed. The container was well insulated and no thermal energy was able to flow into, or out of, the gas. (a) If the internal energy of the gas decreased by 29.7 J, calculate the temperature change of the gas. (b) Determine how much work was done by the gas during this expansion. [4 marks]
✓ (a) \(\Delta U = \tfrac32nR\Delta T \Rightarrow (-29.7) = 1.5\times1.74\times8.31\times\Delta T\)
✓ \(\Delta T = -1.37\ \text{K}\); the gas cooled.
✓ (b) No thermal energy flows in or out, so \(Q = 0\): \(0 = \Delta U + W \Rightarrow W = -\Delta U\)
✓ \(W = +29.7\ \text{J}\), done by the gas as it expands.
Exam question · Explain
Q13. Explain why a gas gets hotter when it is compressed rapidly. [3 marks]
✓ Compressing the gas means work is done on it, so \(W\) is negative.
✓ Compression happens too quickly for thermal energy to be transferred to or from the surroundings, so \(Q \approx 0\).
✓ From \(Q = \Delta U + W\), \(\Delta U = -W\), which is positive — the internal energy, and hence the temperature, rises.
