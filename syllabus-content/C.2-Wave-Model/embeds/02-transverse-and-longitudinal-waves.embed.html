Transverse & Longitudinal Waves
Wave types, particle motion, sound waves, compressions & rarefactions, and mechanical vs electromagnetic waves
Syllabus content
Transverse and longitudinal travelling waves. The nature of sound waves. The differences between mechanical waves and electromagnetic waves.
1. Transverse Waves
Definition — Transverse Wave
A wave is transverse if the displacement of the particles of the medium is at right angles (perpendicular) to the direction of energy transfer.

In a transverse wave on a rope, the rope segments oscillate up and down while the wave pattern propagates horizontally. The highest points are crests and the lowest are troughs. Each particle performs SHM with the same frequency as the wave but is slightly out of phase with its neighbour.

A transverse wave: particles oscillate perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer.

Examples: waves on strings, ropes, and water surfaces; all electromagnetic waves; seismic S-waves.

Determining particle motion from a snapshot

This is a key exam skill. Given the wave direction and a displacement–distance graph, you can find which way any particle will move next:

  1. Draw the wave profile (solid line) at the current instant.
  2. Draw a copy of the wave (dashed line) shifted slightly in the direction of propagation.
  3. Each particle moves vertically from its position on the solid curve towards its position on the dashed curve.
Shift the wave slightly in its travel direction to see where each particle moves next.
Each particle moves vertically toward the shifted (dashed) profile.
Exam tip — Velocity at crests and equilibrium
At a crest or trough: the particle has zero velocity (momentarily at rest) but maximum acceleration (directed towards equilibrium). At the equilibrium position: the particle has maximum speed but zero acceleration. This follows directly from SHM.
2. Longitudinal Waves
Definition — Longitudinal Wave
A wave is longitudinal if the displacement of the particles is parallel to the direction of energy transfer. Longitudinal waves consist of alternating compressions and rarefactions.

Imagine pushing and pulling one end of a slinky spring along its length. Coils crowd together (compression) and then spread apart (rarefaction). These regions propagate along the spring.

Longitudinal wave on a slinky: compressions (coils bunched together) and rarefactions (coils spread apart).
CompressionA region where particles are closer together than at equilibrium → higher density and pressure.
RarefactionA region where particles are further apart than at equilibrium → lower density and pressure.

Displacement–distance graphs for longitudinal waves

We represent longitudinal waves using the same sinusoidal graph style. The key difference: the displacement axis represents particle displacement along the wave direction. Positive displacement = motion in the direction of wave travel; negative = opposite.

A longitudinal wave drawn as a displacement–distance graph. Compressions and rarefactions sit at the zero-crossings.

Locating compressions and rarefactions on the graph

This is one of the most commonly tested and most commonly wrong topics:

  • A compression occurs where displacement is zero and the gradient is negative (for a wave moving right). Particles on the left have moved right and particles on the right have moved left — they've crowded together.
  • A rarefaction occurs where displacement is zero and the gradient is positive. Particles on both sides have moved away from this point.
Exam tip — Critical error to avoid
Compressions and rarefactions are NOT at the peaks and troughs of the displacement graph. They occur at the zero-crossing points. This is the single most common error with longitudinal wave graphs. At the peaks/troughs, the displacement is maximum but the density is approximately normal (equilibrium separation).

Worked Example 1 — Longitudinal wave analysis

A longitudinal wave travels to the right. A displacement–distance graph shows that at point P (at x = 20 cm), the displacement is zero and the graph is transitioning from positive to negative. Is P a compression or rarefaction?

Solution
At P, the gradient is negative. Particles to the left of P have positive displacement (moved right, towards P). Particles to the right of P have negative displacement (moved left, towards P). Particles on both sides have moved towards P → higher density → P is a compression.
3. Sound Waves

Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave. It is produced when a vibrating surface (loudspeaker, vocal cord, tuning fork) creates alternating compressions and rarefactions in the surrounding air.

A loudspeaker creates compressions and rarefactions of air pressure — a longitudinal sound wave.

Key facts about sound

  • Medium required: Sound cannot travel through a vacuum.
  • Typical speed in air: ~340 m s⁻¹ at room temperature (increases slightly with temperature).
  • Speed in different media: Generally fastest in solids, slowest in gases — because particles are closer and inter-particle forces are stronger in solids.
  • Audible range: approximately 20 Hz – 20 kHz.
  • Pitch: determined by frequency (higher f → higher pitch).
  • Loudness: determined by amplitude (greater A → louder).

To give a sense of scale: at the threshold of hearing (1000 Hz), the pressure variation at the eardrum exceeds atmospheric by just 20 μPa (atmospheric pressure is ~10⁵ Pa). The corresponding molecular displacement is about 10⁻¹¹ m — a tenth of a hydrogen atom's diameter!

Critical thinking — Why can't transverse waves travel through gases?
Transverse mechanical waves require a restoring force perpendicular to the direction of propagation. This requires the medium to sustain shear stress. Gases (and bulk liquids) have no fixed molecular structure and cannot resist shear — their molecules simply slide past each other. Hence only longitudinal (compression) waves can propagate. This explains why seismic S-waves (transverse) cannot pass through Earth's liquid outer core, while P-waves (longitudinal) can — giving evidence for the core's liquid state.
Critical thinking — Water surface waves: truly transverse?
We often call water surface waves “transverse,” but this is a simplification. In deep water, individual water particles actually move in circles (or ellipses in shallow water) — combining both vertical and horizontal motion. The surface appears to move transversely, but the underlying particle motion is more complex. This is a good example of how models simplify reality.

Q4 · Explain[3]
State and explain two differences between an electromagnetic wave and a sound wave.
EM waves need no medium (can travel through vacuum) while sound requires a medium. ✓ EM waves are transverse (oscillating E and B fields) while sound is longitudinal (compressions and rarefactions). ✓ EM waves travel at \(c = 3.0\times10^8\) m s⁻¹ in vacuum, far faster than sound (~340 m s⁻¹ in air). ✓
Q5 · Explain[3]
Explain why seismic S-waves (transverse) cannot travel through Earth's liquid outer core, but P-waves (longitudinal) can. What does this tell us about the state of the outer core?
Transverse waves need a medium that can sustain shear stress. Liquids cannot resist shear, so S-waves are not transmitted through the liquid outer core. ✓ Longitudinal P-waves rely on compression, which liquids can transmit, so they pass through. ✓ The absence of S-waves through the core is evidence that the outer core is liquid. ✓
