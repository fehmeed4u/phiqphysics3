Intensity and Amplitude of Waves
From energy concepts to the I ∝ A² relationship — build intuition, read graphs, and conquer exam‑style questions.

Why does turning up the volume feel so much louder?

A tiny ripple carries almost no energy; a tsunami can destroy coastlines. A whisper is barely audible; a rock concert can shake your chest. The difference is not just in how high the wave is (its amplitude), but in how much energy it delivers per second per square metre — its intensity.

Fig 1 — Everyday extremes: from a tiny ripple to a giant wave. The amplitude increase means far greater energy.

1. Amplitude — a quick recap

Amplitude (A) is the maximum displacement of a particle from its equilibrium position. For a sound wave it can be measured as pressure variation; for an electromagnetic wave it’s the maximum electric field strength.

Fig 2 — A sinusoidal wave. The amplitude A is the distance from the rest position to a crest (or trough).

2. What is intensity?

Intensity (I) is the power transmitted per unit area, measured in watts per square metre (W m⁻²).

I = P / A

Imagine a light bulb radiating energy. The same total power is spread over an ever‑larger spherical surface as you move away. That’s why a star looks dim from Earth — not because it emits less power, but because the intensity is tiny when the light reaches us.

Fig 3 — For a point source, power P is spread over a sphere of area 4πr², so I = P / (4πr²). Intensity drops with the inverse‑square of distance.

3. Energy in a wave and the I ∝ A² law

The energy carried by a wave is proportional to the square of its amplitude. For a sinusoidal mechanical wave, the intensity can be derived as:

I = 2π² ρ v f² A²

where ρ is the density of the medium, v the wave speed, f the frequency. For a given medium and frequency, intensity is directly proportional to amplitude squared:

I ∝ A²
💡 Examiner insight: You’ll rarely need the full constant. Most IB questions expect you to use the proportionality I₁/I₂ = (A₁/A₂)² — this works for any wave type (sound, water, light) when only amplitude changes.
⚠️ Common mistake: “If intensity doubles, amplitude doubles.” No — because of the square root: √2 ≈ 1.41. Doubling I only multiplies amplitude by about 1.4.
✓ Exam tip: Always write I₁/I₂ = (A₁/A₂)² before plugging numbers. It avoids confusion and shows the examiner you understand the proportionality.

4. Reading the relationship from graphs

The square‑law is best understood by looking at different graphs.

Fig 4 — I vs A: a parabolic curve through the origin, because I ∝ A².
Fig 5 — log I vs log A: a straight line of gradient exactly 2, confirming the square relationship.

To make the relationship linear, plot I vs A² — a straight line through the origin. The slope gives the constant 2π²ρv f².

I vs A² → straight line log I vs log A → gradient = 2

5. Inverse‑square law for a point source

When waves spread spherically from a source, amplitude also decreases with distance. Because I ∝ 1/r² and I ∝ A², it follows that A ∝ 1/r.

Fig 6 — Intensity vs distance from a point source: the rapid fall‑off follows 1/r². Amplitude would fall as 1/r.
💡 Extension (HL): The fact that A ∝ 1/r for a spherical wave comes directly from energy conservation: power through any spherical shell is constant, so 4πr₁² I₁ = 4πr₂² I₂ ⇒ I ∝ 1/r² ⇒ A ∝ 1/r.

Worked Example — Comparing two waves

Fig 7 — Wave P (top) and Wave Q (bottom). Both have the same frequency and travel in the same medium.

Wave P has amplitude X₀, Wave Q has amplitude 2X₀. They travel in the same medium with identical frequency. Find the ratio of their intensities, I_Q / I_P.

1
State the known values: A_Q = 2 X₀, A_P = X₀. Frequency and medium are identical, so the constant 2π²ρv f² cancels.
2
Write the proportional equation: I ∝ A² ⇒ I_Q / I_P = (A_Q / A_P)².
3
Substitute: I_Q / I_P = (2 X₀ / X₀)² = 2² = 4.
4
State the answer: Wave Q has 4 times the intensity of Wave P. Doubling amplitude quadruples intensity.

Exam‑style questions
Exam question · Calculate
A wave has an amplitude of 0.5 mm and an intensity of 2.0 W m⁻². Calculate its intensity when the amplitude is increased to 1.5 mm, assuming the frequency and medium remain the same. [2 marks]
Model answer:
✓ I₁/I₂ = (A₁/A₂)²
✓ I₂ = I₁ × (A₂/A₁)² = 2.0 × (1.5/0.5)² = 2.0 × 9 = 18 W m⁻² (accept 2 s.f.)
Exam question · Explain
Explain why a graph of intensity against amplitude is not a straight line. Use the relationship I ∝ A² in your answer. [2 marks]
Model answer:
✓ Because I ∝ A², the relationship is quadratic, so a graph of I vs A produces a parabola, not a straight line.
✓ To obtain a straight‑line graph, one must plot I against A² (or log I vs log A).
Exam question · Apply
A point source emits sound with a power of 50 W. Calculate the intensity at a distance of 2.0 m from the source. [2 marks]
Model answer:
✓ I = P / (4πr²)
✓ I = 50 / (4π × 2.0²) = 50 / (16π) ≈ 1.0 W m⁻² (2 s.f.)
