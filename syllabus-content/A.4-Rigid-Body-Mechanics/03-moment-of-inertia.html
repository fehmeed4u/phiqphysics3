Moment of Inertia
Mass tells you how hard it is to speed up a moving object. Moment of inertia tells you how hard it is to speed up a spinning one — and it depends on more than just how much mass there is.

🚪 Hook – the door that won't budge

Push a door near its hinge and it barely moves; push the same door with the same force near the handle, far from the hinge, and it swings open easily. The mass of the door hasn't changed. What has changed is how far the mass is from the axis of rotation — and that is the whole idea behind this lesson.

Fig 1 — A point mass m rotating at a distance r from its axis. Its resistance to a change of rotational motion is I = mr².
💡 Diagnostic question (think – then read on) — two identical solid discs have the same mass and radius. One spins about an axis through its centre; the other spins about an axis through its edge. Which is harder to speed up? The one spinning about its edge — more of its mass sits at a larger average distance from that axis, so it has a larger moment of inertia, even though the mass and shape are identical.

🌀 From inertia to moment of inertia

In Topic A.2 we saw that when a resultant force is applied to an object, the result is a linear acceleration, the magnitude of which depends on the mass of the object. Resistance to a change of motion (acceleration) is called inertia.

For rotational motion, we also need to consider how the mass is distributed around the axis. Consider two objects, A and B, with the same mass and shape but different axes of rotation: object A will require more force to produce a certain angular acceleration than object B. We say that A has a larger moment of inertia than B.

Fig 2 — Objects A and B have the same mass and shape, but different axes of rotation, so they have different moments of inertia.
Moment of inertia of a point mass $$I = mr^2$$

Moment of inertia, I, quantifies the resistance to a change of rotational motion of an object; it depends on the distribution of mass around the chosen axis of rotation. The SI unit of moment of inertia is kg m². Most spherical objects can be considered to behave like masses concentrated at their centre points — their centre of mass is at the centre of the sphere.

In principle, the moment of inertia of any real, extended mass can be found by adding the individual moments of inertia of its point masses:

Moment of inertia of a system of point masses $$I = \sum mr^2$$

The symbol Σ means "sum of". In practice, the moments of inertia of most simple-shaped objects about specific axes are well known.

📐 Standard shapes – no need to memorise

There is no need to remember these results, or to know how they were derived, because equations will be provided in the examination paper if needed. What matters is being able to select and use the right one for a given axis.

Fig 3 — Moments of inertia of standard shapes about the axes shown (r = radius, L = length). Notice how the same object gives a different I for a different axis, exactly as in Fig 2.
Rod about centre $$I = \tfrac{1}{12}mL^2$$ Rod about end $$I = \tfrac{1}{3}mL^2$$
Solid cylinder or disc $$I = \tfrac{1}{2}mr^2$$ Hoop $$I = mr^2$$
Solid sphere $$I = \tfrac{2}{5}mr^2$$ Thin spherical shell $$I = \tfrac{2}{3}mr^2$$
Solid cylinder, central diameter $$I = \tfrac{1}{4}mr^2 + \tfrac{1}{12}mL^2$$ Hoop about a diameter $$I = \tfrac{1}{2}mr^2$$

✍️ Worked example 1 – a simple pendulum (A4.6)

Determine the moment of inertia of a 24 g simple pendulum of length 90 cm.

1
Identify: assume the pendulum bob can be treated as a point mass, and that the effect of the string is negligible. Use \(I = mr^2\).
2
Data: \(m = 0.024\) kg, \(r = 0.90\) m.
3
Equation: \(I = mr^2 = 0.024 \times 0.90^2\)
4
Answer: \(I = 1.9 \times 10^{-2}\ \text{kg m}^2\).

🧮 Tool 3: Percentage change and percentage difference

Use percentage difference to compare two independent quantities, and percentage change when one quantity becomes another.

Percentage difference $$\text{\% diff} = 100 \times \frac{\text{actual difference}}{\text{mean}}$$ Percentage change $$\text{\% change} = 100 \times \frac{\text{change}}{\text{original value}}$$

Example — using Fig 3, determine the percentage difference between the moments of inertia (about a central axis) of a solid sphere of mass 1.0 kg and a thin spherical shell of mass 100 g, both of radius 12 cm.

\(I_{\text{sphere}} = \tfrac25 \times 1.0 \times 0.12^2 = 5.76\times10^{-3}\ \text{kg m}^2\)
\(I_{\text{shell}} = \tfrac23 \times 0.10 \times 0.12^2 = 9.60\times10^{-4}\ \text{kg m}^2\)
difference \(= 4.80\times10^{-3}\ \text{kg m}^2\); mean \(= 3.36\times10^{-3}\ \text{kg m}^2\)
\(\text{\% diff} = 100\times(4.80\times10^{-3})/(3.36\times10^{-3}) = 143\%\)

Percentage change example — a torque increases from 12 N m to 18 N m: change \(= +6\) N m, so \(\text{\% change} = 100\times6/12 = +50\%\). If instead the torque fell from 18 N m to 12 N m: \(\text{\% change} = 100\times(-6)/18 = -33\%\).

✍️ Worked example 2 – a 'dumb-bell' arrangement (A4.7)

Two spherical masses, each of mass \(m_1 = 2.0\) kg, rotate about an axis that is a distance \(r_1 = 35\) cm from both of their centres, connected by a rod of mass \(m_2 = 400\) g, length \(L = 56\) cm and radius \(r_2 = 1.2\) cm. (a) Determine the overall moment of inertia of the arrangement. (b) Calculate the percentage that the rod contributes to the overall moment of inertia.

1
Identify: total I = (I of the two point-mass spheres) + (I of the rod about its centre).
2
Data: \(m_1 = 2.0\) kg, \(r_1 = 0.35\) m, \(m_2 = 0.400\) kg, \(L = 0.56\) m. The rod's radius \(r_2\) is not needed here since \(I = \tfrac{1}{12}m_2L^2\) doesn't depend on it.
3
Equation: \(I = (2\times m_1r_1^2) + (\tfrac1{12}\times m_2L^2)\)
\(= (2\times2.0\times0.35^2) + (\tfrac1{12}\times0.400\times0.56^2)\)
\(= 0.490 + 1.045\times10^{-2}\)
4
Answer (a): \(I = 0.50\ \text{kg m}^2\) (0.50045… on the calculator display).
(b) \(100\times(0.01045/0.50045) = 2.1\%\).

🏺 Real-world: flywheels

Flywheels are added to the axes of rotating machinery to resist changes of motion and/or to store rotational kinetic energy. They need to have large moments of inertia. Figure 7 shows an old-fashioned example, a potter's wheel: the large wheel at the bottom is kicked for a while until it is spinning quickly. After that, because it has a large moment of inertia, there is no need to keep kicking the wheel continuously to maintain its motion.

Fig 7 — A flywheel on a potter's wheel. If it had a mass of 20 kg and radius 50 cm, spinning at 2 Hz, it would store about 200 J of rotational kinetic energy.
✓ Examiner tip: modern flywheels are used in vehicles with regenerative braking and in wind turbines for electricity generation. Compare and contrast the two uses if asked — both store rotational kinetic energy, but a vehicle flywheel repeatedly charges and discharges quickly, while a turbine flywheel smooths out a more continuous supply.

🚀 HL extension – a thick-walled tube

The standard shapes in Fig 3 don't cover every geometry you might meet. A thick-walled cylindrical tube (an annulus) of inner radius \(r_1\) and outer radius \(r_2\), rotating about its central axis, has:

$$I = \tfrac{1}{2}m\left(r_1^2 + r_2^2\right)$$

This reduces to the solid disc formula when \(r_1 = 0\), and to the hoop formula when \(r_1 \approx r_2\) — a useful check that a formula is being applied correctly.
Challenge: a thick-walled tube of mass 2.57 kg has inner radius 5.6 cm and outer radius 6.4 cm. What is its moment of inertia about its central axis? Answer: \(I = \tfrac12\times2.57\times(0.056^2+0.064^2) = 9.3\times10^{-3}\) kg m² — and note the length, L, of the tube doesn't appear in the formula at all.

⚠️ Trap corner – common mistakes

MisconceptionCorrect view
A bigger mass always means a bigger moment of inertiaI depends on both mass and how far it sits from the axis (\(I=mr^2\)). A small mass far from the axis can have a larger I than a large mass close to it
Moment of inertia is a fixed property of an object, like its massI depends on the chosen axis of rotation. The same object has a different I for every different axis (Fig 2 and Fig 3)
r in \(I = mr^2\) means the object's radiusr is the distance of the mass from the axis of rotation — for a point mass that's just its distance from the axis, not a "size"
The standard formulas (Fig 3) must be memorised for the examThey will be provided in the exam paper if needed — what's examined is choosing and applying the right one
Extending an object's mass further from the axis always changes I by the same factor as the distanceI depends on \(r^2\), not r — doubling the radius quadruples the moment of inertia for a point mass (see Question 13b)
⚠️ Most common exam slip: writing \(I = mr^2\) for an extended object without checking whether a more specific standard formula (rod, disc, sphere, shell…) actually applies to its shape and axis.
I = mr² (point mass) I = Σmr² (system) SI unit: kg m² I depends on the axis, not just mass formulas given in the exam flywheel = large I % diff = 100 × diff / mean % change = 100 × change / original

Exam‑style questions
Exam question · Estimate
Q12. Estimate the moment of inertia of the Earth in its orbit around the Sun (mass of Earth ≈ 6.0 × 10²⁴ kg, distance to the Sun ≈ 1.5 × 10⁸ km). [3 marks]
✓ Treat the Earth as a point mass at orbital radius r, so \(I = mr^2\).
✓ \(r = 1.5\times10^{8}\ \text{km} = 1.5\times10^{11}\) m.
✓ \(I = (6.0\times10^{24})\times(1.5\times10^{11})^2 \approx 1.4\times10^{47}\ \text{kg m}^2\).
Exam question · Calculate
Q13. A rotating dumb-bell arrangement consists of two masses of 800 g, each 20 cm from the axis of rotation; assume the connecting rod has no significant effect. (a) Calculate the moment of inertia of the arrangement. (b) By what factor would the moment of inertia change if 20 cm was increased to 30 cm? [4 marks]
✓ (a) \(I = 2\times mr^2 = 2\times0.800\times0.20^2\)
✓ \(I = 0.064\ \text{kg m}^2\).
✓ (b) \(I \propto r^2\), so the factor is \((30/20)^2 = 2.25\).
Exam question · Suggest / Estimate
Q14. (a) Suggest why the equation I = mr² could be used to determine an approximate value for the moment of inertia of a bicycle wheel. (b) Estimate a value for the moment of inertia of a typical bicycle wheel. [3 marks]
✓ (a) Most of a bicycle wheel's mass is in the rim and tyre, at approximately the same radius from the axle; the spokes contribute negligible mass, so the wheel behaves like a hoop / ring of point masses at that radius.
✓ (b) Reasonable estimate: \(m \approx 1.5\) kg, \(r \approx 0.33\) m, so \(I = mr^2 \approx 1.5\times0.33^2 \approx 0.16\ \text{kg m}^2\) (any answer of the right order of magnitude with a justified estimate is acceptable).
Exam question · Estimate
Q15. A flywheel may be considered to be a steel cylinder (density 7800 kg m⁻³) of outer radius 40 cm and thickness 12 cm. Use this simplification to estimate its moment of inertia, ignoring other features. [4 marks]
✓ Volume \(= \pi r^2 L = \pi\times0.40^2\times0.12 = 0.0603\ \text{m}^3\).
✓ Mass \(= \text{density}\times\text{volume} = 7800\times0.0603 \approx 470\) kg.
✓ Solid cylinder about its central axis: \(I = \tfrac12mr^2 = 0.5\times470\times0.40^2\)
✓ \(I \approx 38\ \text{kg m}^2\).
Exam question · Determine (HL)
Q16. A thick-walled tube has an axis of rotation Y, length L = 20.0 cm, mass m = 2.57 kg, inner radius 5.6 cm and outer radius 6.4 cm. Determine its moment of inertia about axis Y. [4 marks]
✓ A thick-walled tube about its central axis: \(I = \tfrac12m(r_1^2+r_2^2)\).
✓ \(I = \tfrac12\times2.57\times(0.056^2+0.064^2)\)
✓ \(I = \tfrac12\times2.57\times0.007232\)
✓ \(I = 9.3\times10^{-3}\ \text{kg m}^2\) (the length L does not appear in the formula).
Exam question · Estimate
Q17. A boy of mass 25 kg stands at the rail, 1.86 m from the centre, of a playground merry-go-round of mass 370 kg and radius 1.25 m. Estimate a value for the moment of inertia of the system. [4 marks]
✓ Model the platform as a solid disc: \(I_{\text{platform}} = \tfrac12\times370\times1.25^2 = 289\ \text{kg m}^2\).
✓ Model the boy as a point mass at the rail: \(I_{\text{boy}} = 25\times1.86^2 = 86.5\ \text{kg m}^2\).
✓ Total \(I = 289 + 86.5 \approx 380\ \text{kg m}^2\) (2 s.f.).
