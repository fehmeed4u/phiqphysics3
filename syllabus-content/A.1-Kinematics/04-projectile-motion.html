Projectile Motion
How do we predict the path of a thrown ball, a fired bullet, or any object moving under gravity alone? This lesson builds on vertical motion to handle 2D trajectories.

🎯 Hook – The bullet and the dropped ball

Imagine firing a bullet horizontally from a gun at the same instant as you drop another bullet from the same height. Which hits the ground first? Surprisingly — they both land at exactly the same time. The horizontal motion does not affect the vertical fall. This is the core insight of projectile motion: horizontal and vertical components are completely independent.

Fig 1 — A dropped ball (left) and a horizontally projected ball (right) fall equal vertical distances in the same time. Horizontal motion does not affect vertical acceleration.
💡 Diagnostic question (think – then click) — A stone is thrown horizontally from a cliff. If you ignore air resistance, what happens to its horizontal speed as it falls? Answer: It stays constant — no horizontal force means no horizontal acceleration.

📐 Resolving velocity into components

A projectile's velocity at any instant can be split into horizontal and vertical components. These components are independent — they can be analysed separately.

Components of velocity $$v_{\mathrm{v}} = v\sin\theta \qquad v_{\mathrm{H}} = v\cos\theta$$

Here \(v\) is the magnitude of the velocity, and \(\theta\) is the angle it makes with the horizontal. The horizontal component remains constant (if air resistance is negligible), while the vertical component changes at \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

Fig 2 — Resolving velocity into horizontal and vertical components. The tennis player example shows a ball hit at 6° below horizontal.
🔑 Key idea: The horizontal and vertical motions are independent. The vertical acceleration is always \(g\) downward, regardless of the horizontal motion.

⏱️ Time of flight and range

The time of flight is determined entirely by the vertical motion. For a projectile launched horizontally from height \(h\):

Time to fall from height h $$h = \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad t = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}$$

The range (horizontal distance) is then:

$$x = v_{\mathrm{H}} \cdot t = v_{\mathrm{H}} \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}$$

So the range is proportional to the square root of the height: \(x \propto \sqrt{h}\). This is a key result you will be expected to graph and linearise.

Fig 3 — Experimental setup for measuring range \(x\) for different drop heights \(h\).

✍️ Worked example – Bullet fired horizontally

1
Identify: A bullet is fired horizontally from height \(h = 22.0\,\mathrm{m}\) with speed \(v_{\mathrm{H}} = 524\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). We need the horizontal range. Air resistance is negligible. The time of flight comes from vertical motion.
2
Data: \(h = 22.0\,\mathrm{m}\), \(v_{\mathrm{H}} = 524\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).
3
Equation: From \(h = \frac{1}{2}gt^2\): \(t = \sqrt{2h/g}\). Then \(x = v_{\mathrm{H}} t\).
4
Answer: \(t = \sqrt{2(22.0)/9.81} = 2.12\,\mathrm{s}\). \(x = 524 \times 2.12 = 1.11 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{m}\) (1.11 km). The bullet lands 1.11 km away.
Fig 5 — Setup for the bullet example: range \(x = 1.11\) km from height 22.0 m.

🎯 Real-world: Sports projectile analysis

In sports like tennis, golf, and basketball, players intuitively adjust launch angle and speed. The optimal angle for maximum range on level ground is \(45^\circ\). However, air resistance (drag) reduces range, so real athletes often launch lower or higher depending on the sport.

✓ Examiner tip: In IB exam questions, always state assumptions about air resistance. If the question says "negligible air resistance", you must use the constant horizontal velocity model.

🚀 HL Extension – Projectile with drag

In reality, air resistance adds a force opposite to velocity. This makes the trajectory non‑parabolic — the range is shorter, the time of flight is different, and the descent is steeper than the ascent.

Challenge: If air resistance is included, does the time of flight increase, decrease, or stay the same for a projectile launched upwards? (Answer: It increases, because drag slows the upward motion and reduces the peak height, but the total fall time is longer due to the reduced downward acceleration.)

⚠️ Trap corner – common mistakes

MisconceptionCorrect view
A projectile's horizontal velocity changes as it falls.Horizontal velocity is constant (if no air resistance). Only the vertical component changes.
Heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones.In the absence of air resistance, all objects fall with the same acceleration \(g\), regardless of mass.
The time of flight depends on the horizontal speed.The time of flight is determined only by vertical motion. A faster horizontal speed does not keep the projectile in the air longer.
The trajectory of a projectile is always a full parabola.Only the path between launch and landing is parabolic. If the ground is not level, the visible arc may be only part of a parabola.
⚠️ Most common slip: Using the initial velocity as the horizontal component. Always resolve the velocity into components first using \(v_{\mathrm{H}} = v\cos\theta\) and \(v_{\mathrm{v}} = v\sin\theta\).
\(v_{\mathrm{H}} = v\cos\theta\) \(v_{\mathrm{v}} = v\sin\theta\) \(t = \sqrt{2h/g}\) \(x = v_{\mathrm{H}}t\) \(x \propto \sqrt{h}\) \(a_{\mathrm{H}} = 0\)

Exam‑style questions
Exam question · Calculate
A stone is thrown horizontally from the top of a cliff 45 m high with a speed of 12 m/s.
(a) Calculate the time taken to reach the bottom of the cliff. [2 marks]
(b) Calculate the horizontal distance from the base of the cliff where the stone lands. [2 marks]
(a) \(t = \sqrt{2h/g} = \sqrt{2(45)/9.81} = 3.03\,\mathrm{s}\) ✓
(b) \(x = v_{\mathrm{H}}t = 12 \times 3.03 = 36.4\,\mathrm{m}\) ✓
Exam question · Explain
A ball is projected horizontally from a table. Describe and explain how the horizontal and vertical components of its velocity change as it falls. [3 marks]
✓ Horizontal component: remains constant (no horizontal force, assuming negligible air resistance).
✓ Vertical component: increases uniformly at \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) (constant downward acceleration).
Exam question · Discuss
Two projectiles are launched with the same initial speed. One is launched at 30° and the other at 60° to the horizontal. Discuss whether they have the same range. [4 marks]
✓ Range formula \(R = \frac{v^2 \sin 2\theta}{g}\).
✓ \(\sin 60^\circ = \sin 120^\circ\), so ranges are equal.
✓ Therefore, complementary angles (30° and 60°) give the same range for the same initial speed (on level ground, neglecting air resistance).
