Kinetic and Gravitational Potential Energy
Work done on an object becomes stored energy. The three mechanical stores — kinetic, gravitational and elastic — are the accounting system of mechanics.

🎯 Hook – the cost of speed

A car travelling at 60 km h⁻¹ that doubles its speed to 120 km h⁻¹ does not need twice the braking distance. It needs four times the distance, because kinetic energy depends on the square of the speed. This single fact explains most of the physics behind speed limits.

Fig 1 — Double the speed, quadruple the kinetic energy — and quadruple the distance needed to remove it.
💡 Diagnostic question (think – then read on) — A book rests on a table. Is its gravitational potential energy zero? The question is incomplete. \(\Delta E_{\text{p}} = mg\Delta h\) only ever gives a change relative to some other position, so "zero" depends entirely on where you measure from.

📐 Mechanical energy

Mechanical energy is the energy of a macroscopic object that can do useful work. It is the sum of three stores:

Mechanical energy $$E_{\text{mech}} = E_{\text{k}} + E_{\text{p}} + E_{\text{H}}$$

Work is the mechanism that moves energy between stores, which gives the central statement of this topic: the work done by the resultant force on a system equals the change in the energy of the system. Do work on a body and it gains energy; let it do work on something else and it loses energy.

✓ The symbol \(E\) always carries a subscript naming the store: \(E_{\text{k}}\) kinetic, \(E_{\text{p}}\) gravitational potential, \(E_{\text{H}}\) elastic potential.

🏃 Kinetic energy

Consider a mass \(m\) accelerated from rest across a horizontal distance \(s\) by a constant resultant force \(F\). From \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\) with \(u = 0\) we get \(s = v^2/2a\), so the work done is

$$W = Fs = ma \times \frac{v^2}{2a} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$$

That energy has been transferred to the moving mass as kinetic energy. Rewriting \(\tfrac12 mv^2\) as \((mv)^2/2m\) and using \(p = mv\) gives a second, equivalent form, most often used for atomic particles:

Translational kinetic energy $$E_{\text{k}} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m}$$
Fig 2 — Doing work to increase movement: all the work done becomes kinetic energy.

These equations give translational kinetic energy only — the energy of travelling from place to place. Vibrating and rotating objects also have kinetic energy, but need different equations (Topics A.4 and C.1).

ObjectOrder of magnitude of \(E_{\text{k}}\) / J
A molecule in air at 20 °C10⁻²⁰
A falling snowflake10⁻⁷
A boy running a 100 m race10³
A bullet from a rifle10⁴
A car on an open road10⁵
A large meteor approaching Earth10¹⁷
The Earth orbiting the Sun10³³
💡 These are orders of magnitude — values rounded to the nearest power of ten. A boy in a 100 m race could plausibly have anything from 90 J to 2800 J, so quoting three significant figures would be misleading. Estimation to 1 significant figure, or to the nearest power of ten, is the honest answer.

✍️ Worked example – work becoming kinetic energy

1
Identify: A constant resultant horizontal force of 40 N accelerates a box over 50 cm. Find the work done, the kinetic energy gained, and the mass of the box if it started at rest and reached 2.9 m s⁻¹.
2
Data: \(F = 40\ \text{N}\), \(s = 0.50\ \text{m}\), \(u = 0\), \(v = 2.9\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). Assumption: the force acts in the same direction as the motion, so \(\theta = 0\).
3
Equation: \(W = Fs = 40 \times 0.50 = 20\ \text{J}\). Since the box started at rest and this is the resultant force, all 20 J becomes kinetic energy: \(E_{\text{k}} = 20\ \text{J}\). Then \(20 = \tfrac12 m (2.9)^2\).
4
Answer: \(m = 2 \times 20 / 2.9^2 = 4.8\ \text{kg}\).
✓ Examiner tip: the phrase "resultant force" is what licenses \(W = \Delta E_{\text{k}}\). If friction also acted, only part of the applied work would appear as kinetic energy.

⛰️ Gravitational potential energy

To raise a mass \(m\) through a vertical height \(\Delta h\) at constant velocity, the minimum force needed equals the weight, \(mg\). The work done is therefore \(W = mg\Delta h\), and that energy is stored as gravitational potential energy:

Gravitational potential energy near the Earth's surface $$\Delta E_{\text{p}} = mg\Delta h$$

Allow the mass to fall back the same distance and the same energy becomes available to do useful work. A hydroelectric power station is exactly this principle at scale.

Fig 3 — Only the vertical height matters. The path taken is irrelevant.
⚠️ Two limits on this equation. First, \(g\) must be effectively constant over the distance involved — \(g = 9.8\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\) cannot be used for a satellite launched hundreds of kilometres up. Second, it gives a change only: a mass on a table does not have "zero" gravitational potential energy, it simply has whatever value we choose to measure from.
✍️
Worked example: Estimate the gravitational potential energy gained by a 40 kg teenager riding to the 124th floor of the Burj Khalifa, taking each floor as 3.5 m. Height \(\Delta h = 124 \times 3.5 = 434\ \text{m}\), so \(\Delta E_{\text{p}} = 40 \times 9.8 \times 434 \approx 1.7 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}\).

🪢 Elastic potential energy

When a spring or elastic material is deformed from its natural shape it becomes a store of elastic potential energy, released when it returns to that shape. Recall Hooke's law from A.2, \(F_{\text{H}} = -k\Delta x\), where \(k\) is the spring constant.

If the force rises steadily from 0 to \(F_{\text{H}}\), the average force is \(\tfrac12 F_{\text{H}}\), so

$$W = \tfrac{1}{2}F_{\text{H}}\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(k\Delta x)\Delta x$$
Elastic potential energy $$E_{\text{H}} = \tfrac{1}{2}k\Delta x^{2}$$

No negative sign is needed, because work is not a vector quantity. As always, the work done also equals the area under the force–extension graph — here a triangle.

Fig 4 — For a Hooke's law spring the graph is a straight line, so the stored energy is the triangle \(\tfrac12 F_{\text{H}}\Delta x\).
✍️
Worked example: A compression spring 5.64 cm long is shortened to 5.30 cm by a 59.0 N force. (a) \(k = F/\Delta x = 59.0/0.34 = 174\ \text{N cm}^{-1} = 1.74\times10^{4}\ \text{N m}^{-1}\). (b) \(W = \tfrac12 F\Delta x = 29.5 \times 3.4\times10^{-3} = 0.100\ \text{J}\). (c) Check: \(E_{\text{H}} = \tfrac12 (1.74\times10^{4})(3.4\times10^{-3})^2 = 0.100\ \text{J}\) — the two routes agree, as they must.

🔧 Real-world: simple machines

Raising a 50 kg mass by 1 m needs about 500 J — not much work, but it demands a 500 N force. Ramps, levers and pulleys reduce that force at the cost of a longer distance moved, so the product \(Fs\) stays roughly the same. Friction means a machine always transfers more total energy than lifting directly; we accept that because ease matters more than efficiency.

⚠️ Power and strength are different things, and everyday language confuses them. A machine changes the force you need; it does not change the energy the job requires.

🔬 Nature of science: uncertainty

Because \(E_{\text{k}}\) depends on \(v^2\), the percentage uncertainty in speed counts twice when propagating into energy. In a trolley-on-a-slope experiment, a 14% uncertainty in timing alone pushed the uncertainty in kinetic energy above 30%, and the uncertainty in the difference between two energies reached 58%.

✓ A result like that is a signal to redesign the experiment — light gates instead of a stopwatch — not to quote more decimal places.

Exam‑style questions
Exam question · Derive / Calculate
(a) A constant resultant force \(F\) accelerates a mass \(m\) from rest through a distance \(s\) to a speed \(v\). Show that the work done is \(\tfrac12 mv^2\). [3]
(b) Determine the work needed to accelerate a 1800 kg car from rest to 20 m s⁻¹. [2]
(c) Calculate the average resultant force needed to do this over a horizontal distance of 100 m. [2]
7 marks
(a) ✓ Using \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\) with \(u = 0\) gives \(s = v^2/2a\). ✓ \(W = Fs = ma \times v^2/2a\). ✓ The accelerations cancel, leaving \(W = \tfrac12 mv^2\).
(b) ✓ \(W = \Delta E_{\text{k}} = \tfrac12 (1800)(20)^2\). ✓ \(= 3.6\times10^{5}\ \text{J}\).
(c) ✓ \(F = W/s = 3.6\times10^{5}/100\). ✓ \(F = 3.6\times10^{3}\ \text{N}\).
Exam question · Calculate / Explain
A cable car of mass 1800 kg carries six passengers of average mass 47 kg. It rises a vertical height of 700 m over a total distance travelled of 6.0 km.
(a) Show that approximately 15 MJ of gravitational potential energy is transferred during the journey. [3]
(b) Suggest why considerably more energy than this must actually be supplied. [2]
5 marks
(a) ✓ Total mass \(= 1800 + 6(47) = 2082\ \text{kg}\). ✓ \(\Delta E_{\text{p}} = mg\Delta h = 2082 \times 9.8 \times 700\). ✓ \(= 1.43\times10^{7} \approx 15\ \text{MJ}\) (using the vertical height, not the 6.0 km).
(b) ✓ Energy is dissipated as thermal energy by friction in the cables, pulleys and bearings, and by air resistance. ✓ Kinetic energy must also be supplied to get the car moving, and the motor itself is not 100% efficient.
Exam question · Outline / Predict
A rocket launches a 500 kg satellite to a height of 400 km above the Earth's surface.
(a) Outline why \(\Delta E_{\text{p}} = mg\Delta h\) with \(g = 9.8\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\) cannot be used to determine the gravitational potential energy transferred accurately. [2]
(b) Given that \(g\) falls from 9.8 to 8.7 N kg⁻¹ over this height, predict the gravitational potential energy gained. [3]
5 marks
(a) ✓ The equation assumes \(g\) is constant over the height involved. ✓ Over 400 km \(g\) decreases appreciably, so using 9.8 N kg⁻¹ throughout would overestimate the energy required.
(b) ✓ Use a mean value, \(g_{\text{av}} = (9.8 + 8.7)/2 = 9.25\ \text{N kg}^{-1}\). ✓ \(\Delta E_{\text{p}} = 500 \times 9.25 \times 4.00\times10^{5}\). ✓ \(= 1.9\times10^{9}\ \text{J}\).
