The Doppler Effect for Sound
Why does an ambulance siren drop in pitch the instant it passes you — even though the siren itself never changes its note?
Hook & Diagnostic

🎯 Hook – The siren that changes its mind

Picture yourself standing on a pavement as an ambulance races towards you, siren blaring. The pitch sounds high and urgent. The moment it passes, the pitch drops to a lower, almost disappointed tone — yet the driver hears the same note the entire time. The siren never changed; your ears did the interpreting.

Fig 1 — Wavefronts bunch up ahead of a moving source and stretch out behind it. Observer A (in front) hears a higher pitch than Observer B (behind).
💡 Diagnostic question (think first): A loudspeaker on a car emits a constant-frequency tone. Observer A is ahead, Observer B is behind. Which is correct? (A) Sound waves travel faster towards A. (B) The car moves towards A and away from B. (C) Wavefronts compress ahead and stretch behind. (D) Both B and C. — Answer: D. The speed of sound in air is unchanged; it is the wavefront spacing (wavelength) that changes.
Core Knowledge

📐 What is the Doppler effect?

The Doppler effect is the change in observed frequency (and wavelength) of a wave when there is relative motion between the source and the observer. The key variable is not speed of sound — that stays constant in a given medium — but the spacing between wavefronts.

Emitted frequency \(f\) is the number of wavefronts the source produces per second (Hz). Observed frequency \(f'\) is the number of wavefronts the observer detects per second. The speed of sound \(v\) remains constant for a given medium (≈ 340 m s⁻¹ in air at 20 °C). Source speed \(u_s\) and observer speed \(u_o\) are measured relative to the medium.

Fig 2 — Stationary source: wavefronts are concentric circles with equal spacing λ. The observer receives wavefronts at the same rate they are emitted, so \(f' = f\).

📐 Case 1 — Moving source, stationary observer

When the source moves, each successive wavefront is emitted from a new position. Ahead of the source the wavefronts are compressed (shorter wavelength → higher frequency). Behind the source they are stretched (longer wavelength → lower frequency).

Fig 3 — Moving source: wavefronts bunch up in the direction of travel (shorter λ′, higher f′) and spread apart behind (longer λ′, lower f′).

In one period \(T = 1/f\), the source moves a distance \(u_s T\) while the wavefront expands by \(vT\). The apparent wavelength ahead becomes \(\lambda' = (v - u_s)/f\), so:

Source approaching observer $$f' = f\,\frac{v}{v - u_s}$$
Source receding from observer $$f' = f\,\frac{v}{v + u_s}$$
⚠️ Notice the denominator: when \(u_s \to v\), the observed frequency shoots towards infinity — this is the onset of a sonic boom. The formula breaks down at and beyond the speed of sound.

📐 Case 2 — Stationary source, moving observer

When the observer moves but the source stays still, the wavefronts remain evenly spaced in the medium — the wavelength \(\lambda\) does not change. Instead, the observer runs into wavefronts faster (or slower), altering the rate of detection.

Fig 4 — Moving observer: the wavefronts are still evenly spaced, but the observer intercepts them at a higher rate when moving towards the source.

The effective speed at which wavefronts pass the observer is \(v + u_o\) (approaching) or \(v - u_o\) (receding). Since \(f' = \text{relative speed} / \lambda\):

Observer approaching source $$f' = f\,\frac{v + u_o}{v}$$
Observer receding from source $$f' = f\,\frac{v - u_o}{v}$$
💡 Key asymmetry: For the same closing speed, a moving source produces a larger frequency shift than a moving observer. This is because sound has a medium — the medium provides an absolute reference frame. For light (no medium), this asymmetry disappears.

Worked Example

✍️ Worked example – Train approaching a station

A train approaches a station at 25.0 m s⁻¹ sounding a horn of frequency 450 Hz. The speed of sound in air is 340 m s⁻¹. Calculate the frequency heard by a stationary observer on the platform.

1
Identify: The source (train) moves towards a stationary observer. We use the moving-source equation with the minus sign (approaching).
2
Data: \(f = 450\) Hz, \(v = 340\) m s⁻¹, \(u_s = 25.0\) m s⁻¹.
3
Equation: \(f' = f\,\dfrac{v}{v - u_s} = 450 \times \dfrac{340}{340 - 25.0} = 450 \times \dfrac{340}{315} = 450 \times 1.0794\)
4
Answer: \(f' \approx 486\) Hz. The observer hears a higher-pitched note because the wavefronts are compressed ahead of the moving train. As a check, the receding frequency would be \(450 \times 340/365 \approx 419\) Hz — lower, as expected.
Fig 7 — Geometry: the train (source) moves at us = 25.0 m s⁻¹ towards the stationary observer. Compressed wavefronts reach the observer at a higher rate.
Applications & Extension

🩺 Real-world: Medical ultrasound blood-flow

Doctors aim an ultrasound beam (typically 6 MHz) at a blood vessel at angle θ. Red blood cells reflect the sound with a Doppler-shifted frequency. The shift depends on blood speed and the cosine of the beam angle: \(\Delta f = 2f_0 \, v_{\text{blood}} \cos\theta \,/\, v_{\text{tissue}}\). By measuring Δf, doctors can map blood flow speed — a non-invasive window into the circulatory system.

✓ Examiner tip: If asked why the angle θ matters, state that only the component of velocity along the beam contributes to the Doppler shift. At θ = 90°, cos 90° = 0 and no shift is detected — the blood moves perpendicular to the beam.

🚀 HL Extension – Radar speed guns & the factor of 2

For electromagnetic waves where the source speed is much less than \(c\), the fractional frequency shift is \(\Delta f / f \approx v/c\). A police radar gun uses a round trip — the wave hits the car and bounces back — so the shift doubles:

$$\frac{\Delta f}{f} = \frac{2v_{\text{car}}}{c}$$

Challenge: A radar gun transmits at 20.6 GHz. A car approaches at 130 km h⁻¹ (36.1 m s⁻¹). What is the frequency shift? (Answer: Δf = 2 × 20.6 × 10⁹ × 36.1 / (3 × 10⁸) ≈ 4.96 kHz.)

Trap Corner

⚠️ Trap corner – common mistakes

MisconceptionCorrect view
"Pitch rises because sound travels faster towards the observer."The speed of sound in air is fixed for given conditions. What changes is the wavefront spacing (wavelength), not the wave speed.
"The Doppler shift is the same whether the source or observer moves at the same speed."For sound, the medium provides an absolute reference. The source-moving formula and observer-moving formula give different shifts for the same closing speed.
"When the observer moves, the wavelength changes."A moving observer does not alter the wavelength in the medium. Only the rate at which wavefronts are intercepted changes (frequency of detection).
"Doppler shift depends on the distance between source and observer."The shift depends only on relative velocity (and the speed of sound), not on separation distance. For uniform motion, f′ is constant at all distances.
⚠️ Most common slip: Using the wrong formula — applying the moving-source equation when the observer moves (or vice versa). Always ask: "Who is moving relative to the medium?" before choosing your equation.
Key Points
f′ = f · v/(v − u_s) f′ = f · (v ± u_o)/v v = 340 m s⁻¹ in air λ changes (source moves) λ unchanged (observer moves) Δf/f ≈ 2v/c (radar)

Exam question · Calculate
A small speaker emitting a constant 2000 Hz tone is mounted on a cart moving at 12 m s⁻¹. The speed of sound is 340 m s⁻¹.
(a) Calculate the frequency heard by a stationary observer when the cart approaches. [2 marks]
(b) Calculate the frequency heard when the cart recedes. [1 mark]
(a) f′ = f × v/(v − us) = 2000 × 340/(340 − 12) ✓
f′ = 2000 × 340/328 = 2073 Hz ✓

(b) f′ = 2000 × 340/(340 + 12) = 2000 × 340/352 = 1932 Hz ✓

Exam question · Calculate (two-step Doppler)
A bat flies at 6.0 m s⁻¹ directly towards a wall, emitting ultrasound at 45.0 kHz. The speed of sound in air is 340 m s⁻¹.
(a) Calculate the frequency of the sound wave incident on the wall. [2 marks]
(b) The wall reflects the sound. The bat now acts as a moving observer approaching the reflected wave. Calculate the frequency of the echo detected by the bat. [2 marks]
(c) State the total frequency shift Δf between the emitted and received frequencies. [1 mark]
(a) Bat is a moving source approaching the wall:
fwall = 45.0 × 340/(340 − 6.0) = 45.0 × 340/334 = 45.81 kHz ✓✓

(b) Wall acts as a stationary source re-emitting 45.81 kHz. Bat is a moving observer approaching:
fecho = 45.81 × (340 + 6.0)/340 = 45.81 × 346/340 = 46.62 kHz ✓✓

(c) Δf = 46.62 − 45.0 = 1.62 kHz ✓
