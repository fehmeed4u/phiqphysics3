Doppler Effect and Light
Explore how tiny shifts in the colour of light reveal the motion of stars, galaxies, and even the expansion of the Universe.

🎯 Hook – The cosmic speed gun

A police radar gun can measure the speed of a car using reflected microwaves. Astronomers use exactly the same Doppler physics to measure the speed of galaxies billions of light-years away. A tiny shift in wavelength reveals whether a star is approaching or receding from Earth. The colour shift of light becomes a cosmic speedometer.

Fig 1 — Doppler measurements connect radar speed detection on Earth to the expansion of distant galaxies.
💡 Diagnostic question (think – then click) — If a star moves toward Earth, what changes about its light: brightness, colour, or the speed of light itself? Answer: the colour/frequency changes — the speed of light remains constant.

📐 Spectral fingerprints of atoms

Every element emits light at specific frequencies, producing a unique line spectrum. These spectral lines act like fingerprints that allow astronomers to identify the composition and motion of stars.

Wave relationship $$c = f\lambda$$

If the source moves relative to the observer, the observed wavelength \(\lambda\) changes while the speed of light \(c\) remains constant.

Fig 2 — Emission spectra produce bright lines, while absorption spectra show dark lines where wavelengths are absorbed.
$$\frac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda} \approx \frac{v}{c}$$

🌌 Redshift and blueshift

A redshift occurs when wavelengths increase because the source is moving away. A blueshift occurs when wavelengths decrease because the source moves closer.

Approximation for low speeds $$\frac{\Delta f}{f} = \frac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda} \approx \frac{v}{c} \qquad (v \ll c)$$

This approximation is only valid when the relative speed is much less than the speed of light.

Fig 3 — Spectral lines shift toward the red end when receding and toward the blue end when approaching.
💡 Astronomers compare observed spectral lines with laboratory values to determine recession speed.

✍️ Worked example – Measuring a galaxy's recession speed

1
Identify: This is a Doppler redshift problem. We compare emitted and observed wavelengths to determine recession speed.
2
Data: Laboratory wavelength \(= 587.6 \text{ nm}\). Observed wavelength \(= 612.0 \text{ nm}\). Therefore \(\Delta\lambda = 24.4 \text{ nm}\).
3
Equation: Use \(v = \frac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda}c\). Substituting gives \(v = \frac{24.4}{587.6}(3.00 \times 10^8)\).
4
Answer: \(v \approx 1.25 \times 10^7 \text{ m s}^{-1}\). The galaxy is moving away because the wavelength increased.
Fig 6 — Comparing laboratory and galactic spectra reveals the recession speed.

🌍 Real-world: The expanding universe

Most galaxies show a redshift in their spectra, meaning they are moving away from Earth. Edwin Hubble discovered that more distant galaxies move faster, providing evidence that the universe itself is expanding.

Fig 7 — The recession of galaxies provides evidence for the Big Bang model.
✓ Examiner tip: Always connect redshift evidence to expansion of space, not galaxies moving through empty space alone.

🚀 HL Extension – Relativistic Doppler effect

At speeds approaching \(c\), the approximation formula fails. The relativistic Doppler equation accounts for time dilation effects.

$$f_{\text{obs}} = f_s \frac{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}}{1+\beta\cos\theta}$$

Challenge: Why does the observed frequency still change when motion is perpendicular to the observer? (Answer: time dilation causes the transverse Doppler effect.)

⚠️ Trap corner – common mistakes

Misconception Correct view
Redshift means the star appears red. Only the spectral lines shift toward longer wavelengths.
The speed of light changes when the source moves. \(c\) remains constant; frequency and wavelength change instead.
The approximation works at all speeds. \(\frac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda} \approx \frac{v}{c}\) only works when \(v \ll c\).
All galaxies are redshifted. Nearby galaxies like Andromeda show blueshift because they approach Earth.
⚠️ Most common slip: forgetting to convert \(\text{km s}^{-1}\) into \(\text{m s}^{-1}\) before using \(c = 3.00 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}\).
