Doppler Effect for Light and Other Electromagnetic Waves
The same shift that changes the pitch of a siren also runs a police speed gun, an airport radar and the measurement that told us the Universe is expanding.

🎯 Hook – the beam that comes back changed

A police officer points a microwave transmitter at your car. The beam of known frequency \(f\) hits the car, a small fraction reflects, and it arrives back at the gun a few thousand hertz different from the frequency that left. Nobody measured your car directly — the frequency shift alone gave your speed. Point the same trick at a galaxy and you measure the expansion of the Universe.

Fig 1 — Microwaves of known frequency \(f\) travel to the object and back, so the shift is doubled: the round trip is why a factor of 2 appears.
💡 Diagnostic question (think – then click) — A source of light races towards you at \(0.01c\). Does the light itself arrive faster? No. The speed of electromagnetic waves measured by any observer is always the same — it is unaffected by the speed of the source or of the observer. Only the frequency and wavelength change.

📐 The Doppler effect for electromagnetic waves

The Doppler effect occurs for electromagnetic waves as well as for mechanical waves like sound, but the situation is more complicated, because the speed of electromagnetic waves as measured by any observer is always the same. It is not affected by the speed of the source or the speed of the observer. This is a relativistic effect (Topic A.5), but that theory is not of concern here.

The shift in frequency is defined as \(\Delta f =\) received frequency \(-\) emitted frequency. The equivalent shift in wavelength is \(\Delta \lambda\). For a relative speed \(v\) between source and observer:

Relative Doppler shift for light \((v \ll c)\) $$\frac{\Delta f}{f} = \frac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda} \approx \frac{v}{c}$$

The equation may only be used when the relative speed \(v\) is very much less than the speed of the electromagnetic waves, \(c\). Because \(c\) is so large, this is usually a valid assumption.

⚠️ These are not the sound equations with \(c\) written in. The moving-source and moving-observer formulas on the HL page below cannot be applied to electromagnetic waves.

📡 Using the Doppler effect to determine speeds

Microwaves of known frequency \(f\) are easily produced and transmitted as a beam which can be directed at a moving object. A small fraction of the radiation arrives back at the source after reflecting off the object. The change in frequency, \(\Delta f\), due to the Doppler effect can then be used to determine the speed of the object. Examples include:

• Speed 'guns' used by police for checking the speed of moving vehicles.
• Radar used for monitoring aircraft, or boat movements.
• Radar used for tracking the movement of storms.

In all of these the microwaves travel from the source to the object and then back to the source. For this reason a factor of 2 is added to the right-hand side of the equation:

Reflected (round-trip) shift $$\frac{\Delta f}{f_0} = \frac{2v}{c}$$

Radar (RAdio Detection And Ranging) is a system which uses microwaves to detect the distance, direction and speed of moving objects. Pulses of microwaves are sent from a rotating aerial. After a very small fraction of a second, some microwaves reflected off the aircraft are received back at the aerial. The time delay gives the distance to the aircraft, and the orientation of the aerial gives the direction to it. Using the Doppler effect with radar also enables a direct calculation of the aircraft's speed, and has the advantage of being able to ignore reflections from objects which are not moving, or are only moving slowly.

Fig 2 — Radar: the time delay gives distance, the aerial orientation gives direction, and the Doppler shift gives the component of velocity along the beam.

🌈 Spectra: continuous, emission and absorption

Everything emits thermal radiation, and this emitted radiation is a continuous spectrum with a wide range of frequencies (Topic B.1). A continuous light spectrum can be displayed on a screen using a prism to disperse the radiation (Topic C.2).

Individual atoms and simple molecules instead emit electromagnetic radiation of certain precise frequencies, rather than a continuous range. Topic E.1 explains how this is connected to changing energy levels within the atoms. When elements in the form of gases are given enough energy, the spectra they emit are seen as a series of bright lines on black backgrounds — called line spectra. Each line corresponds to a precise frequency.

Fig 3 — The principal lines of the line spectrum of hydrogen. Each bright line is one precise frequency.

When a continuous spectrum passes through a gas, the atoms in the gas absorb the same frequencies as they would emit when given energy. The result is a spectrum with black absorption lines. Atoms of the elements present in the outer layers of a star absorb light of certain frequencies from the continuous spectrum emitted by the star. Each different element produces a unique set of lines and frequencies, so the pattern can be used to identify the element emitting the radiation. The analysis of spectra using spectroscopes or spectrometers is called spectroscopy.

🌌 Redshift, blueshift and the expanding Universe

The line spectra received from stars within distant galaxies are received at lower frequencies than light from similar elements on Earth. This can be considered to be a Doppler effect, and it suggests that the source and the observer are moving apart from each other.

💡 Measurements of the Doppler effect in line spectra from stars in distant galaxies show that most galaxies are moving apart from each other — the Universe is expanding.

Compare two spectra of the same element: one produced on Earth, one received from a distant galaxy. The continuous spectra are identical, and the pattern of black lines is unchanged — but every line has been 'shifted' towards the red end of the spectrum. This change to lower frequencies (larger wavelengths) is called a redshift. A change to higher frequency is called a blueshift; blueshifts only occur for a few stars relatively close to the Earth.

Fig 4 — Redshift in line spectra. The pattern is identical; the whole set of lines is displaced towards red.

BlueshiftDecrease in wavelength of electromagnetic radiation (from a 'nearby' star) due to the fact that the observer and the source are moving closer together.

StarMassive sphere of plasma held together by the forces of gravity. Because of the high temperatures, thermonuclear fusion occurs and radiation is emitted.
GalaxyA very large number of stars (and other matter) held together in a group by the forces of gravity.
Expansion of the universeThe redshift of light (similar to the Doppler effect) from distant galaxies provides evidence of an expanding universe.
Big Bang modelCurrently accepted model of the Universe, in which matter, space and time began at a point 13.7 billion years ago. The Universe has been expanding ever since.
SpectroscopyThe analysis of spectra using instruments called spectroscopes or spectrometers.
RadarA system which uses microwaves to detect the distance, direction and speed of moving objects.

Measurements on a large number of galaxies confirm that those with the greatest speeds are those which are furthest away. There is an obvious conclusion: they are further away because they are travelling faster. Moving back in time, they all started at the same place and time. This is the central concept of the Big Bang model — the Universe was created about fourteen billion years ago and has been expanding ever since.

⚠️ Astronomers believe that all space itself is expanding, rather than stars and galaxies moving apart into pre-existing space. The full explanation of the Doppler effect with light from distant galaxies is therefore not the same as the Doppler effect used to describe wave effects confined to Earth.

✍️ Worked example C5.3 – recession speed of a galaxy

A line in the hydrogen spectrum has a wavelength of \(4.86 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}\). When detected on Earth from a distant galaxy, the same line has a wavelength of \(5.21 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}\). Determine the speed with which the galaxy is moving away from Earth (its recession speed).

1
Identify: The wavelength has increased, so this is a redshift and the galaxy is receding. Use the low-speed Doppler relation for light.
2
Data: \(\lambda = 4.86 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}\)
\(\lambda' = 5.21 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}\)
\(\Delta\lambda = 3.5 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{m}\)
3
Equation: \(\dfrac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} \approx \dfrac{v}{c}\)
\(\dfrac{3.5 \times 10^{-8}}{4.86 \times 10^{-7}} = \dfrac{v}{3.00 \times 10^{8}}\)
4
Answer: \(v = 2.16 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\), moving away from Earth. That is about 7% of \(c\), so the approximation still holds.
Fig 7 — The same hydrogen line, measured on Earth and received from the galaxy. The gap between them is \(\Delta\lambda\).

🌍 Real-world: air traffic control and weather radar

Airport radar tracks aircraft with microwave pulses: delay for range, aerial bearing for direction, Doppler shift for speed — and the Doppler filter lets the system ignore reflections from stationary or slow-moving objects. Doppler weather radar tracks the movement of storms the same way.

✓ Examiner tip: whenever the waves reflect off the moving object and return, use \(\Delta f / f_0 = 2v/c\). Dropping the 2 halves your answer and loses the mark.

🚀 HL Extension – why light is the awkward case

For sound, the source's motion physically compresses the waves in the medium, so source-motion and observer-motion give different formulas. For light there is no medium and \(c\) is the same for every observer, so only the relative speed \(v\) enters, through \(\Delta f/f \approx v/c\).

Challenge: a galaxy shows \(\Delta\lambda/\lambda = 0.10\). What recession speed does the approximation give, and why should you be cautious? (Answer: \(v = 0.10c = 3.0 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\); at this fraction of \(c\) the approximation is starting to lose accuracy, and for very distant galaxies the redshift is caused by the expansion of space itself, not motion through space.)

🚀 HL only – Doppler equations for sound and other mechanical waves

Consider waves of frequency \(f\) and wavelength \(\lambda\) travelling at speed \(v\) between a stationary source S and a stationary observer O. In the time \(t\) taken for the first wavefront to reach the observer, the wave has travelled \(vt\), and the number of waves between source and observer is \(t/T = ft\). So

$$\lambda = \frac{vt}{ft} = \frac{v}{f}$$

Now let the source move towards the stationary observer with speed \(u_{\text{s}}\), from \(S_1\) to \(S_2\) in the same time \(t\). The number of waves is unchanged, but they are now compressed into the length \(vt - u_{\text{s}}t\), so the observed wavelength is

Observed wavelength $$\lambda' = \frac{vt - u_{\text{s}}t}{ft}$$ $$\lambda' = \frac{v - u_{\text{s}}}{f}$$
Fig 8 — (a) Stationary source and observer. (b) The source moves a distance \(u_{\text{s}}t\), so the same number of waves occupies \(vt - u_{\text{s}}t\).

The observed frequency \(f' = v/\lambda'\), giving

Source moving towards the observer $$f' = \frac{vf}{v - u_{\text{s}}}$$
Source moving away from the observer $$f' = \frac{vf}{v + u_{\text{s}}}$$
Moving source, general form $$f' = f\left(\frac{v}{v \pm u_{\text{s}}}\right)$$
Moving observer, stationary source $$f' = f\left(\frac{v \pm u_{\text{o}}}{v}\right)$$

\(u_{\text{s}}\) is added when the source is moving away from the observer, and subtracted when the motion is towards the observer. \(u_{\text{o}}\) is added when the observer is moving towards the source, and subtracted when the motion is away from the source. These equations assume that the motion is along the straight line joining the source and the observer.

⚠️ Remember that these equations cannot be applied to electromagnetic waves \((c \gg v)\).

✍️ Worked example C5.4 – sound from a moving source

(a) A source of sound emitting \(480\ \text{Hz}\) moves directly towards a stationary observer at \(50.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). On a hot day the speed of sound is \(350\ \text{m s}^{-1}\); calculate the frequency received. (b) What frequency is heard on a cold day when the speed of sound is \(330\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)? (c) Explain why the speed of sound is less on a colder day.

1
Identify: Moving source, stationary observer, motion towards — so subtract \(u_{\text{s}}\).
2
Data: \(f = 480\ \text{Hz}\), \(u_{\text{s}} = 50.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)
hot day \(v = 350\ \text{m s}^{-1}\); cold day \(v = 330\ \text{m s}^{-1}\)
3
Equation: \(f' = f\left(\dfrac{v}{v - u_{\text{s}}}\right)\)
(a) \(f' = \dfrac{480 \times 350}{350 - 50}\)
(b) \(f' = \dfrac{480 \times 350}{330 - 50}\)
4
Answer: (a) \(560\ \text{Hz}\). (b) \(566\ \text{Hz}\). (c) Sound is transferred through air by moving air molecules; on a colder day the molecules have a lower average speed.
💡 Note the wording of (b) in the source: the emitted frequency and the numerator \(480 \times 350\) are kept, with only the denominator changed to \(330 - 50\).

🔭 TOK and linking questions

Knowledge and the knower — how do the tools that we use shape the knowledge that we produce? Nearly everything that we know about the Universe has been deduced only from electromagnetic radiation arriving at the Earth from space, detected by various types of telescope and analysed using the techniques of spectroscopy. Astronomy is based solely on observation: we can choose what to observe, but we cannot design and carry out the type of laboratory experiments that characterize much of the rest of science. Our knowledge of the Universe is limited by the instruments that we design to collect and analyse the radiation reaching the Earth. The James Webb Telescope was launched in December 2021.

Knowledge and technology — to what extent are technologies such as the microscope and telescope merely extensions to the human senses, or do they introduce radically new ways of seeing the world? Doppler first identified the effect that bears his name about 180 years ago, but he could not have foreseen the many useful applications that his discovery would lead to. This is mainly because the relatively small changes of frequency involved require a high level of technology.

✓ \(\dfrac{f''}{f} = \dfrac{v + u}{v - u} = \dfrac{32.7}{30.0}\)
✓ \(u = 14.4\ \text{m s}^{-1}\) towards the emitter.
✓ (b) The approximation gives \(\dfrac{2.7}{30.0} \times 335 = 30\ \text{m s}^{-1}\), roughly twice the correct value, because it omits the factor of 2 for the round trip.
✓ Halving it gives \(15\ \text{m s}^{-1}\), close to \(14.4\ \text{m s}^{-1}\); the small remaining difference arises because \(u\) is not negligible compared with the wave speed.
Exam question · Determine · HL
Q15. A boat travels directly towards a jetty at \(37\ \text{cm s}^{-1}\), creating waves on the water surface of original wavelength \(59\ \text{cm}\). If the water waves travel at \(94\ \text{cm s}^{-1}\), determine the frequency and wavelength of the waves reaching the jetty. [4 marks]
✓ Emitted frequency \(f = \dfrac{94}{59} = 1.59\ \text{Hz}\)
✓ Moving source, towards: \(\lambda' = \dfrac{v - u_{\text{s}}}{f} = \dfrac{94 - 37}{1.59}\)
✓ \(\lambda' = 36\ \text{cm}\)
✓ \(f' = \dfrac{94}{36} = 2.6\ \text{Hz}\) (the frequency rises, the wavelength shortens).
Exam question · Tool 3 · Propagating uncertainties
Tool 3. A motorist accused of driving over the speed limit might claim that their speed was within the 'margin of error' of the police equipment. Using the data of Q5: if the microwave frequency was accurate to \(\pm 0.2\ \text{GHz}\) and the change of frequency accurate to \(\pm 0.05\ \text{kHz}\), what was the uncertainty in the determination of the car's speed (in \(\text{km h}^{-1}\))? [4 marks]
✓ \(v = \dfrac{\Delta f \, c}{2f_0}\), so fractional uncertainties add.
✓ \(\dfrac{0.05}{2.85} = 1.8\%\) and \(\dfrac{0.2}{32.8} = 0.6\%\), total \(2.4\%\)
✓ \(v = 13.0\ \text{m s}^{-1} = 46.9\ \text{km h}^{-1}\), and \(2.4\%\) of this is about \(1.1\ \text{km h}^{-1}\)
✓ Speed \(= 46.9 \pm 1.1\ \text{km h}^{-1}\). Even the upper limit \((48.0\ \text{km h}^{-1})\) is below \(50\ \text{km h}^{-1}\), so the claim fails.
