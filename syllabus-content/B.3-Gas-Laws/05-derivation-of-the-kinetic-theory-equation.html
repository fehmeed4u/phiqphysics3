Derivation of the Kinetic Theory Equation
A single molecule bouncing off a wall, multiplied by an enormous number of molecules and averaged over every direction, is enough to derive \(PV = \tfrac13 Nm\overline{v^2}\) from nothing but Newton's laws.

🧊 Hook – pressure is just a lot of tiny collisions

Every time a gas molecule bounces off the wall of its container, it gives the wall a tiny push. One push is far too small to notice. But a normal container holds something like 10²³ molecules, each one colliding with the walls billions of times a second. Add up all those pushes and you get something very noticeable: gas pressure is nothing more than the combined effect of countless individual molecular collisions.

Fig 1 — A molecule of mass \(m\) hits a wall and rebounds elastically. Its momentum changes by \(2mv_x\) — and that momentum has to go somewhere.
💡 Diagnostic question — we can never predict the velocity of one particular molecule, yet we can predict the pressure of the whole gas precisely. How is that possible? Pressure depends on the average behaviour of an enormous number of molecules, not on any individual one. Random motion is unpredictable molecule by molecule, but statistically predictable in bulk — this is why the kinetic theory equation works with mean square speed, \(\overline{v^2}\), rather than the speed of any one molecule.

📋 The model and its assumptions

The kinetic theory of gases is a model: a mathematical simplification, not a direct observation of molecules. It lets us connect microscopic mechanics to macroscopic quantities like pressure, volume and temperature. The derivation that follows rests on eight assumptions.

  1. A gas consists of a very large number of identical molecules.
  2. The molecules are in continuous, random motion.
  3. The molecules collide elastically with each other and with the walls of the container.
  4. The time of a collision is negligible compared with the time between collisions.
  5. The forces between molecules are negligible except during collisions.
  6. The volume of the molecules is negligible compared with the volume of the container.
  7. Between collisions, the molecules move in straight lines with constant velocity.
  8. The motion is isotropic: on average, molecular velocities are equally likely in all directions.

💥 Step 1–6: one molecule, one wall, one pressure

Take an ideal gas of \(N\) identical molecules, each of mass \(m\), inside a cubical container of side \(L\). Then \(V = L^3\) and each face has area \(A = L^2\). Follow one molecule with velocity components \(v_x, v_y, v_z\) as it strikes the face perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis.

1
Set up the container. \(V = L^3\), \(A = L^2\). Only the \(x\)-component of velocity matters for a collision with this face; \(v_y\) and \(v_z\) are unchanged by it.
2
One collision. The collision is elastic, so \(v_x \to -v_x\). Change in the molecule's momentum: \(\Delta p_{\text{molecule}} = -2mv_x\). By conservation of momentum, momentum transferred to the wall: \(\Delta p_{\text{wall}} = +2mv_x\).
3
Time between collisions. After rebounding, the molecule crosses the cube, hits the opposite face, and returns — a round trip of \(2L\). So \(\Delta t = \dfrac{2L}{v_x}\).
4
Average force from this molecule. Force is rate of change of momentum: \(F_{\text{one}} = \dfrac{\Delta p_{\text{wall}}}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{2mv_x}{2L/v_x} = \dfrac{mv_x^2}{L}\).
5
Add up all \(N\) molecules. Different molecules have different \(v_x\), so sum their contributions. Using the definition \(\overline{v_x^2} = \tfrac1N\sum v_{x,i}^2\): \(F_{\text{total}} = \dfrac{Nm\overline{v_x^2}}{L}\).
6
Turn force into pressure. \(P = F_{\text{total}}/A\), and \(A = L^2\), so \(P = \dfrac{Nm\overline{v_x^2}}{L^3} = \dfrac{Nm\overline{v_x^2}}{V}\), giving:
$$PV = Nm\overline{v_x^2}$$
Fig 2 — The cube used for the derivation. Only the shaded face is under consideration; the result at the end applies to any face, and — despite starting from a cube — to a container of any shape.

🎯 Step 7–8: escaping the x-direction with isotropy

The equation above still singles out the \(x\)-direction, which was only ever a convenient choice of face. Assumption 8 — isotropy — removes that asymmetry: on average, no direction is preferred, so \(\overline{v_x^2} = \overline{v_y^2} = \overline{v_z^2}\).

Since \(v^2 = v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2\), taking averages gives \(\overline{v^2} = \overline{v_x^2} + \overline{v_y^2} + \overline{v_z^2} = 3\overline{v_x^2}\), so:

$$\overline{v_x^2} = \tfrac{1}{3}\overline{v^2}$$

Substituting into \(PV = Nm\overline{v_x^2}\) gives the kinetic theory equation:

The kinetic theory equation $$PV = \tfrac{1}{3}Nm\overline{v^2}$$
Fig 3 — Resolving \(v\) into \(v_x, v_y, v_z\). For random, isotropic motion, the three squared components average out equally — that single step is what turns a one-direction result into a formula for the whole gas.

🌡️ From pressure to temperature and internal energy

The average random translational kinetic energy of one molecule is \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}} = \tfrac12 m\overline{v^2}\). Rewriting the kinetic theory equation:

$$PV = \tfrac{2}{3}N\overline{E_{\text{k}}}$$

For an ideal monatomic gas, the internal energy is entirely this random translational kinetic energy, \(U = N\overline{E_{\text{k}}}\), so \(U = \tfrac32 PV\). Equating the kinetic theory equation with the microscopic ideal gas equation \(PV = Nk_{\text{B}}T\) and cancelling \(N\):

$$k_{\text{B}}T = \tfrac{1}{3}m\overline{v^2}$$

Multiplying by \(\tfrac32\) gives one of the most important results in this topic — the average kinetic energy of a molecule depends on nothing except its absolute temperature:

Average translational kinetic energy $$\overline{E_{\text{k}}} = \tfrac{3}{2}k_{\text{B}}T$$

and therefore, using \(U = N\overline{E_{\text{k}}}\), \(N = nN_{\text{A}}\) and \(k_{\text{B}} = R/N_{\text{A}}\):

Internal energy of an ideal monatomic gas $$U = \tfrac{3}{2}Nk_{\text{B}}T = \tfrac{3}{2}nRT$$

The root-mean-square speed, \(v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\overline{v^2}}\), follows from \(\tfrac12 m\overline{v^2} = \tfrac32 k_{\text{B}}T\):

$$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{3k_{\text{B}}T}{m}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{3RT}{M}}$$

✍️ Worked example B3.D1 – using the kinetic theory equation

A sealed container of volume \(2.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3\) contains \(5.0 \times 10^{22}\) helium atoms, each of mass \(6.65 \times 10^{-27}\) kg. The mean square speed of the atoms is \(1.2 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}^2\text{s}^{-2}\). Calculate the pressure of the helium gas.

1
Identify: use the kinetic theory equation, rearranged for pressure: \(P = \dfrac{Nm\overline{v^2}}{3V}\).
2
Data: \(N = 5.0\times10^{22}\), \(m = 6.65\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg}\), \(\overline{v^2} = 1.2\times10^{6}\ \text{m}^2\text{s}^{-2}\), \(V = 2.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3\).
3
Equation: numerator \(= (5.0\times10^{22})(6.65\times10^{-27})(1.2\times10^{6})\)
\(= 399\). Denominator \(= 3 \times 2.0\times10^{-3} = 6.0\times10^{-3}\).
4
Answer: \(P = \dfrac{399}{6.0\times10^{-3}} = 6.65\times10^{4}\ \text{Pa} \approx 6.7\times10^{4}\ \text{Pa}\).

✍️ Worked example B3.D2 – root-mean-square speed

Oxygen molecules at 300 K each have a mass of \(5.31\times10^{-26}\) kg. Calculate their root-mean-square speed.

1
Identify: find \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}}\) from \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}} = \tfrac32 k_{\text{B}}T\), then use \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}} = \tfrac12 mv_{\text{rms}}^2\).
2
Data: \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}} = 1.5 \times (1.38\times10^{-23}) \times 300 = 6.21\times10^{-21}\ \text{J}\).
3
Equation: \(v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2\overline{E_{\text{k}}}}{m}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2(6.21\times10^{-21})}{5.31\times10^{-26}}}\)
\(= \sqrt{2.34\times10^{5}}\).
4
Answer: \(v_{\text{rms}} \approx 4.8\times10^{2}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).

🌍 Real-world: why the model has limits

Every assumption in this derivation is an idealisation. Real molecules do have volume, and they do attract each other between collisions — both effects are ignored to keep the maths tractable. At low pressure and high temperature, molecules are far apart and moving fast, so both approximations are excellent and the real gas tracks \(PV = nRT\) closely, as in Fig 4. Compress the gas or cool it towards a phase change, and the same approximations start to fail.

✓ Examiner tip: if a question asks why a real gas deviates from ideal behaviour, name the specific assumption that breaks down — "molecular volume becomes significant" or "intermolecular forces are no longer negligible" — rather than just "it's not a perfect gas".

🚀 HL Extension – how fast, really?

The molar-mass form \(v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3RT/M}\) lets you compare gases directly: at the same temperature, every gas has the same average kinetic energy per molecule, but lighter molecules move faster to achieve it, since \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}} = \tfrac12 mv_{\text{rms}}^2\) is fixed by \(T\) alone.

Challenge: by what factor does \(v_{\text{rms}}\) change if the absolute temperature of a fixed gas is quadrupled? Answer: \(v_{\text{rms}} \propto \sqrt{T}\), so quadrupling \(T\) doubles \(v_{\text{rms}}\).

⚠️ Trap corner – common mistakes

The pressure formula should use the average speed, \(\overline{v}\)Average velocity is zero for random motion (components cancel), but \(\overline{v^2}\) is never zero — it is the quantity that survives averaging, and the one that enters momentum transfer and kinetic energy
\(\overline{v^2}\) is the same as \((\overline{v})^2\)They are different quantities. \(\overline{v^2}\) is the mean of the squares; \((\overline{v})^2\) is the square of the mean — squaring first and averaging first give different numbers for any spread of speeds
The result \(PV=\tfrac13Nm\overline{v^2}\) only works for a cubeThe cube is chosen purely because its geometry is easy to set up. The same result holds for a container of any shape — pressure cannot depend on the shape of its container

Exam‑style questions
Exam question · State
Q1. State the assumptions of the kinetic theory of gases that are used in the derivation of \(PV = \tfrac13Nm\overline{v^2}\). [4 marks]
✓ A gas consists of a very large number of identical molecules, in continuous random motion.
✓ Molecules collide elastically with each other and the walls; collision time is negligible compared with time between collisions.
✓ Intermolecular forces are negligible except during collisions; molecular volume is negligible compared with the container's volume.
✓ Between collisions molecules travel in straight lines at constant velocity, and the motion is isotropic.
Exam question · Explain
Q2. Explain why the pressure exerted by a gas on a wall is proportional to the mean square speed \(\overline{v^2}\) and not to the average speed \(\overline{v}\). [3 marks]
✓ Molecular velocities are random and isotropic, so the average velocity (a vector) is zero — the positive and negative components cancel.
✓ \(\overline{v^2}\) does not cancel, because every squared term is positive, whatever the direction of motion.
✓ Momentum transfer and kinetic energy both depend on \(v^2\), not \(v\), so it is \(\overline{v^2}\) that appears in the derivation.
Exam question · Show that
Q3. A molecule of mass \(m\) moves with velocity component \(v_x\) perpendicular to a wall. Show that the average force it exerts on the wall is \(F = mv_x^2/L\), where \(L\) is the length of the cubical container. [3 marks]
✓ Elastic collision reverses \(v_x\), so momentum transferred to the wall per collision is \(2mv_x\).
✓ Time between collisions with the same wall is \(\Delta t = 2L/v_x\) (round trip across the container).
✓ \(F = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{2mv_x}{2L/v_x} = \dfrac{mv_x^2}{L}\).
Exam question · Derive
Q4. Starting from the expression for the total force on one face of a cubical container, derive \(PV = \tfrac13Nm\overline{v^2}\). [4 marks]
✓ \(F_{\text{total}} = \dfrac{Nm\overline{v_x^2}}{L}\), and \(P = F_{\text{total}}/A\) with \(A = L^2\), so \(PV = Nm\overline{v_x^2}\).
✓ By isotropy, \(\overline{v_x^2} = \overline{v_y^2} = \overline{v_z^2}\), and \(\overline{v^2} = \overline{v_x^2}+\overline{v_y^2}+\overline{v_z^2} = 3\overline{v_x^2}\).
✓ So \(\overline{v_x^2} = \tfrac13\overline{v^2}\).
✓ Substituting: \(PV = Nm\left(\tfrac13\overline{v^2}\right) = \tfrac13Nm\overline{v^2}\).
Exam question · Show that
Q5. Use the kinetic theory equation and the ideal gas equation \(PV = Nk_{\text{B}}T\) to show that \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}} = \tfrac32 k_{\text{B}}T\). [3 marks]
✓ Equate: \(Nk_{\text{B}}T = \tfrac13Nm\overline{v^2}\), cancel \(N\): \(k_{\text{B}}T = \tfrac13m\overline{v^2}\).
✓ Multiply both sides by \(\tfrac32\): \(\tfrac32k_{\text{B}}T = \tfrac12m\overline{v^2}\).
✓ Since \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}} = \tfrac12m\overline{v^2}\), this gives \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}} = \tfrac32k_{\text{B}}T\).
Exam question · Show that
Q6. Show that the internal energy of an ideal monatomic gas can be written as \(U = \tfrac32PV\). [2 marks]
✓ \(U = N\overline{E_{\text{k}}}\) for a monatomic gas, and \(PV = \tfrac13Nm\overline{v^2} = \tfrac23N\overline{E_{\text{k}}}\).
✓ So \(PV = \tfrac23U\), which rearranges to \(U = \tfrac32PV\).
Exam question · Calculate
Q7. A sample of neon gas has a volume of 0.025 m³ and a pressure of \(1.5\times10^{5}\) Pa. Calculate its internal energy. [2 marks]
✓ \(U = \tfrac32PV = 1.5 \times (1.5\times10^{5} \times 0.025)\)
✓ \(U = 1.5 \times 3750 = 5625\ \text{J} \approx 5.6\times10^{3}\ \text{J}\).
Exam question · Calculate
Q8. Nitrogen molecules at 350 K have a molar mass of 28.0 g mol⁻¹. Calculate (a) the average random translational kinetic energy of one molecule, (b) the root-mean-square speed of the molecules. [4 marks]
✓ (a) \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}} = \tfrac32k_{\text{B}}T = 1.5 \times (1.38\times10^{-23}) \times 350\)
✓ \(\approx 7.25\times10^{-21}\ \text{J}\).
✓ (b) \(v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3RT/M} = \sqrt{(3 \times 8.31 \times 350)/0.0280}\)
✓ \(= \sqrt{3.12\times10^{5}} \approx 5.6\times10^{2}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
Exam question · Explain
Q9. Explain why the root-mean-square speed of carbon dioxide molecules is lower than that of oxygen molecules at the same temperature. [2 marks]
✓ At the same temperature, both gases have the same average kinetic energy per molecule, \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}} = \tfrac32k_{\text{B}}T\), independent of molecular mass.
✓ Since \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}} = \tfrac12m\overline{v^2}\), the more massive CO₂ molecule (44 g mol⁻¹ vs 32 g mol⁻¹ for O₂) must have a smaller \(\overline{v^2}\), so a lower \(v_{\text{rms}}\).
