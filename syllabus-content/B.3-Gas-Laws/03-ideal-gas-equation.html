Ideal Gas Equation
The three separate gas laws combine into one equation, PV/T = constant, and that in turn generalises to any amount of gas as PV = nRT. This lesson shows you how to use both forms, and how to read a state of a gas off a pressure–volume diagram.

🤿 Hook – trusting your life to PV/T = constant

A scuba tank holds gas compressed to over 200 times atmospheric pressure. As a diver breathes it out, the surrounding pressure keeps falling as she rises toward the surface. The gas laws are the reason her exhaled air doesn't stay the same size as it rises — it must expand, or her lungs would be damaged. Every dive computer, every gas gauge, and every safety rule about "never hold your breath while ascending" comes down to PV/T = constant.

Fig 1 — As exhaled bubbles rise, the surrounding water pressure falls. What happens to their volume?
💡 Diagnostic question (think – then read on) — a bubble leaves the diver's regulator at depth, where the pressure is high, and rises toward the surface, where the pressure is roughly atmospheric. Assuming its temperature doesn't change much, what happens to its volume? It expands. Since PV/T = constant and T is roughly fixed, P falling as the bubble rises means V must increase — which is exactly why divers are trained to breathe out continuously while ascending, rather than holding a lungful of air that would expand inside them.

🔬 Nature of science – empirical laws, then a theory

The three separate gas laws (constant pressure, constant volume, constant temperature) are empirical — based only on direct observation and experiment. On their own they let us make accurate predictions without explaining why gases behave that way.

📖 In science, a theory is an explanation that has been extensively tested by further experimentation and is widely accepted — not a casual guess, which is how the word is often used in everyday language. The kinetic theory of gases, met in the next lesson, is a theory used to explain these empirical results. More generally, empiricism is the view that all knowledge originates from experience.

For a fixed amount of gas, the three empirical laws combine into one relationship, PV ∝ T:

Combined gas law · fixed amount of gas $$\frac{PV}{T} = \text{constant}$$ $$\frac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$$

🧪 From a fixed amount of gas to any amount

So far every law has assumed a fixed amount of gas. To extend the discussion, investigations show that the pressure of a fixed volume of gas at constant temperature is proportional to the amount of gas — exactly what molecular reasoning predicts: if we doubled the number of molecules in a fixed volume, the frequency of collisions with the walls would double, if they still had the same average speed.

This leads to \(PV \propto nT\), or the ideal gas law (also called the equation of state for an ideal gas):

Ideal gas law $$PV = nRT$$

R, the universal (molar) gas constant, has the value \(8.31\ \text{J K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}\). It is the macroscopic equivalent of the Boltzmann constant, \(k\), met in the microscopic interpretation of temperature in Topic B.1.

This equation is a summary of empirical results, and it predicts the behaviour of most real gases accurately under most conditions. A gas which obeys it perfectly is called an ideal gas. A piston — a solid cylinder fitting tightly inside a hollow cylinder, trapping a fluid — is the standard way of letting a fixed amount of gas change volume under a controlled pressure.

Fig 2 — Oxygen trapped in a cylinder by a movable piston. No gas can pass the piston, so its position fixes the volume.

✍️ Worked example 1 – combined gas law (B3.7)

Gas in a container at a temperature of 289 K was heated in an oven to 423 K. If the volume expanded from 0.27 m³ to 0.35 m³, and the initial pressure was \(1.1\times10^{5}\)Pa, determine the final pressure.

1
Identify: all three variables change, so use the combined gas law for a fixed amount of gas.
2
Data: \(P_1=1.1\times10^{5}\)Pa, \(V_1=0.27\)m³, \(T_1=289\)K, \(V_2=0.35\)m³, \(T_2=423\)K.
3
Equation: $$\frac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$$ $$\frac{(1.1\times10^{5})\times0.27}{289} = \frac{P_2\times0.35}{423}$$
4
Answer: \(P_2 = 1.2\times10^{5}\ \text{Pa}\).

✍️ Worked example 2 – the ideal gas equation with a piston (B3.8)

Oxygen, O₂, is in the cylinder of Fig 2. No gas can move past the piston. The gas is at 298 K, its volume is 27 cm³ and its pressure equals atmospheric pressure, \(1.0\times10^{5}\)Pa. (a) Calculate the amount of gas. (b) What is the mass of oxygen? (c) The piston is suddenly pulled out, increasing the volume to 32 cm³; the pressure falls to \(8.2\times10^{4}\)Pa. Determine the new temperature.

1
Identify: parts (a)–(b) use \(PV=nRT\) with SI units (m³, Pa, K); part (c) can reuse \(PV=nRT\) or the combined gas law \(PV/T=\text{constant}\).
2
Data (a): \(P=1.0\times10^{5}\)Pa, \(V=27\times10^{-6}\)m³, \(T=298\)K, \(R=8.31\)J K⁻¹mol⁻¹.
3
Equation (a): $$(1.0\times10^{5})\times(27\times10^{-6}) = n\times8.31\times298$$
4
Answer (a): \(n = 0.0011\ \text{mol}\).
(b) The molar mass of O₂ is 32 g mol⁻¹, so mass \(= 0.0011 \times 32 = 0.035\ \text{g}\).
(c) \((8.2\times10^{4})\times(32\times10^{-6}) = 0.0011\times8.31\times T\), giving \(T = 287\ \text{K}\) — an 11 K fall as the gas expanded.

📈 Pressure–volume diagrams

A known state of a gas — a value each of P, V and T — can be marked as a single point on a pressure–volume (PV) diagram. Since a 2-axis graph can only show two of the three variables directly, different temperatures are shown as separate curved isothermal lines, from \(T_1\) (lowest) to \(T_8\) (highest) in Fig 3.

🌍 Real-world: the scuba tank, quantitatively

A typical scuba cylinder holds gas at over 200 times atmospheric pressure inside a fixed volume. \(PV=nRT\) is exactly how a dive shop calculates how much air (in moles, or in litres at atmospheric pressure) is really available to breathe from a small metal cylinder — the volume of the tank itself barely changes.

✓ Examiner tip: "amount of gas" in these questions always means moles (n), found from \(PV=nRT\) — don't confuse it with volume or mass unless the question specifically asks for those, and convert them using the molar mass only when asked.

🚀 HL extension – where PV = nRT comes from

The ideal gas law above is presented as a summary of empirical results — it works, but doesn't yet explain why. The next lesson derives it from a molecular model: pressure arises from the change in momentum of particles colliding with a surface, giving \(P = \tfrac13\rho v^2\), which combines with the kinetic theory of gases to predict \(PV=nRT\) from first principles.

Challenge: if \(P=\tfrac13\rho v^2\), and density \(\rho\) is mass per unit volume, what must happen to a gas's average particle speed for its pressure to double at constant density? Answer: \(v^2\) must double, so the average speed increases by a factor of \(\sqrt2\).

Exam‑style questions
Exam question · Calculate / Explain
Q12. The volume of a scuba diving tank (Fig 1) is \(11\times10^{-3}\)m³. The air inside is compressed to a pressure of 210 × atmospheric pressure. (a) What is that air pressure in pascals? (b) What volume of air at atmospheric pressure was pumped into the tank? (c) The water temperature decreases with depth below the surface. Would you expect that to significantly affect the pressure in the tank? (d) As she breathes out, the diver releases air bubbles into the water. Explain why the bubbles get bigger as they rise towards the surface. [6 marks]
✓ (a) \(210 \times 1.0\times10^{5} = 2.1\times10^{7}\ \text{Pa}\).
✓ (b) At constant T, \(P_1V_1 = P_2V_2\): \((2.1\times10^{7})(11\times10^{-3}) = (1.0\times10^{5})V_2\), so \(V_2 \approx 2.3\ \text{m}^3\) of atmospheric-pressure air.
✓ (c) The tank's temperature is far less variable and important than its pressure or volume here — a modest change in water temperature has only a small effect on the (already huge) pressure compared with the effect of depth on the surrounding water pressure itself.
✓ (d) As a bubble rises, the surrounding water pressure falls (less depth of water above it).
✓ Temperature changes little over the ascent, so by PV/T = constant, falling P means V must increase.
✓ The bubble therefore expands as it nears the surface.
Exam question · Explain / Calculate
Q13. The volume of a gas in a cylinder such as in Fig 2 was 38 cm³ when the temperature was 20°C. (a) The apparatus was heated. Explain why the piston moved to the right as thermal energy flowed into the gas through the cylinder. (b) What was the temperature (°C) of the gas when the volume had increased to 50 cm³? [4 marks]
✓ (a) Heating increases the average kinetic energy (and speed) of the gas molecules, so they collide with the piston more frequently and with greater force, increasing the pressure on it; since the piston is free to move, it is pushed outward (to the right) until the gas has expanded enough to restore the original (atmospheric) pressure.
✓ (b) At constant pressure, \(V_1/T_1 = V_2/T_2\): \(38/293 = 50/T_2\).
✓ \(T_2 = 386\ \text{K} = 113°\text{C}\).
Exam question · Estimate
Q14. Helium gas is widely used to fill balloons (Fig 5). The density of the helium in a balloon is \(2.0\times10^{-4}\)g cm⁻³. Taking a typical party balloon volume of 2.0 dm³, estimate its (a) volume in m³ (b) mass (c) amount in moles (d) number of atoms. [4 marks]
✓ (a) \(2.0\ \text{dm}^3 = 2.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3 = 2000\ \text{cm}^3\) (a size must be assumed — the source question gives only a density, so an estimate of balloon size is required).
✓ (b) mass \(= \rho V = 2.0\times10^{-4} \times 2000 = 0.40\ \text{g}\).
✓ (c) helium's molar mass is 4 g mol⁻¹, so \(n = 0.40/4 = 0.10\ \text{mol}\).
✓ (d) number of atoms \(= nN_A = 0.10 \times 6.02\times10^{23} = 6.0\times10^{22}\) atoms.
Exam question · Determine
Q15. A container of gas has a volume of 120 cm³. At a temperature of 300 K the gas has a pressure of \(1.5\times10^{6}\)Pa. Determine the amount of gas in the container. [3 marks]
✓ \(V = 120\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3\).
✓ \(n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{(1.5\times10^{6})(120\times10^{-6})}{8.31\times300}\).
✓ \(n = 0.072\ \text{mol}\).
Exam question · Explain
Q16. How can you be sure that isotherm T5 on Fig 3 represents a higher temperature than T2? [2 marks]
✓ At the same volume, T5 has a higher pressure than T2.
✓ Since \(PV=nRT\) at fixed n and V, pressure is proportional to temperature — so the curve with the higher pressure at that volume must be the higher-temperature isotherm.
Exam question · Discuss
Q17. Consider Fig 3. What changes must be made to a fixed amount of gas at point S on the diagram to move it to point X? [2 marks]
✓ X is not on the same isotherm as S, and lies off the constant-pressure and constant-volume lines through S, so all three quantities must change simultaneously.
✓ From S to X the gas must be compressed to a smaller volume and heated to a higher temperature, with the pressure rising to a value consistent with PV = nRT throughout.
Exam question · Describe
Q18. Describe how you would represent the following changes to a fixed mass of gas on a PV diagram: (a) a gas expanding at constant pressure, as its temperature increases (b) a compression at constant temperature (c) a gas cooled so that its volume and pressure both decrease. [3 marks]
✓ (a) A horizontal line moving to the right (increasing V at constant P) — like line B in Fig 3, moving away from S.
✓ (b) A curve following a single isotherm, moving to smaller V and larger P — like line A, moving toward higher P along the same isotherm.
✓ (c) A diagonal path moving down and to the left, crossing from a higher isotherm to a lower one as both P and V fall together.
