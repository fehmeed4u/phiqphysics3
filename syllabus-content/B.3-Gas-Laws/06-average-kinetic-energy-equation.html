Average Kinetic Energy Equation
Temperature is nothing more than a measure of the average random kinetic energy of molecules. This lesson connects that microscopic idea, through the Boltzmann constant, to the internal energy of a gas — and shows exactly where the ideal gas model starts to fail.

🎲 Hook – why a die roll and a gas molecule are both "random"

Roll a six-sided die and you cannot predict the outcome, although you know there is a one-in-six chance of any particular number. A gas molecule's motion is random in the same sense: its direction and speed are unpredictable, and some speeds are simply more likely than others — at room temperature, a molecular speed of 500 m s⁻¹ is far more likely than 50 m s⁻¹. Individual molecules behave independently, with disordered energies.

Fig 1 — Rolling a die is random in the everyday sense: unpredictable per roll, but statistically well-behaved over many rolls. Gas molecules are random in exactly this way.

The random behaviour of a very large number of individual molecules on the microscopic scale leads to complete predictability in our everyday macroscopic world. The same idea appears in radioactive decay (Topic E.1): one atom's decay time is unknowable, but the activity of a whole sample is predictable — just as insurance and betting companies profit from the statistics of probability without knowing what any one individual will do.

💡 Diagnostic question (think – then read on) — if we can never know the energy of one individual gas molecule, how can physicists calculate the pressure of a gas cylinder to three significant figures? Because pressure and temperature are statistical averages taken over an enormous number of molecules. Individual unpredictability washes out at the macroscopic scale — the same reason a casino can rely on its long-run odds without knowing the outcome of any single spin.

🔬 Internal energy of an ideal gas

The internal energy of an ideal (monatomic) gas, symbol U, is the total random translational kinetic energy of its particles. Ideal monatomic gas particles have no potential energy, and no vibrational or rotational kinetic energy — translational motion is all there is.

Internal energy from particle kinetic energy $$U = N \times \tfrac{1}{2}m\overline{v^2}$$

Comparing the ideal gas equation \(PV=nRT\) with the kinetic theory result \(PV=\tfrac13Nm\overline{v^2}\) gives \(nRT=\tfrac13Nm\overline{v^2}\). Since \(\tfrac13Nm\overline{v^2}=\tfrac23\left(\tfrac12Nm\overline{v^2}\right)=\tfrac23U\):

Internal energy of an ideal monatomic gas · macroscopic form $$U = \tfrac{3}{2}nRT$$

Most real gases are made of molecules, not single atoms. They still approximate the macroscopic behaviour of an ideal gas well, but their internal energy is more complicated because their molecules also have vibrational and rotational kinetic energy. The forces between particles in solids and liquids make calculating internal energy far more complicated still.

⚛️ From R to kB: the Boltzmann constant

Dividing \(U=\tfrac32nRT\) by the number of particles, \(N\), gives the average random translational kinetic energy of a single particle, \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}}=U/N\):

$$\overline{E_{\text{k}}} = \tfrac{3}{2}\frac{nRT}{N}$$

Since \(N/n\) is the Avogadro constant, \(N_{\text{A}}\):

$$\overline{E_{\text{k}}} = \tfrac{3}{2}\frac{RT}{N_{\text{A}}}$$

\(R/N_{\text{A}}\) — the molar gas constant divided by the number of particles in a mole — is called the Boltzmann constant, \(k_{\text{B}}\) (unit J K⁻¹):

Boltzmann constant $$k_{\text{B}} = \frac{R}{N_{\text{A}}}$$ $$k_{\text{B}} = \frac{8.31}{6.02\times10^{23}} = 1.38\times10^{-23}\ \text{J K}^{-1}$$
Average random translational kinetic energy of a particle $$\overline{E_{\text{k}}} = \tfrac{3}{2}k_{\text{B}}T = \tfrac{1}{2}m\overline{v^2}$$

This equation is not restricted to ideal gases. Whenever particles collide or interact, translational kinetic energy is exchanged, so particles in good thermal contact eventually share the same average translational kinetic energy. In other words:

✓ At the same temperature, all gases contain molecules with the same average random translational kinetic energy.

Using \(k_{\text{B}}=R/N_{\text{A}}\), the internal energy and the ideal gas equation can also be written with the microscopic quantities \(N\) and \(k_{\text{B}}\) instead of \(n\) and \(R\):

Internal energy · microscopic form $$U = \tfrac{3}{2}Nk_{\text{B}}T$$
Ideal gas equation · microscopic form $$PV = Nk_{\text{B}}T$$

📐 Reasoned argument — for a given gas sample, why must the area under each of the curves in Fig 2 be the same? The area represents the total number of molecules in the sample. That number cannot change, so as the curve broadens and lowers with rising temperature, the area beneath it must stay constant.

✍️ Worked example 1 – internal energy of one mole of gas

Calculate the total internal energy of one mole of a monatomic ideal gas at 300 K.

1
Identify: a monatomic ideal gas, quantity given as an amount in moles, so use the macroscopic form \(U=\tfrac32nRT\).
2
Data: \(n=1.0\ \text{mol}\), \(R=8.31\ \text{J K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}\), \(T=300\ \text{K}\).
3
Equation: \(U = \tfrac32nRT\)
\(=1.5\times1.0\times8.31\times300\)
4
Answer: \(U = 3.7\times10^{3}\ \text{J}\).

✍️ Worked example 2 – internal energy and pressure of a sealed gas

(a) A sample of an ideal monatomic gas in a closed container at 18°C has a total internal energy of 380 J. Determine how many particles (atoms) it contains. (b) If the container has a volume of 435 cm³, calculate the pressure of the gas.

1
Identify: the quantity is given as a number of particles, so use the microscopic forms \(U=\tfrac32Nk_{\text{B}}T\) for (a), then \(PV=Nk_{\text{B}}T\) for (b).
2
Data: \(U=380\ \text{J}\), \(T=291\ \text{K}\), \(k_{\text{B}}=1.38\times10^{-23}\ \text{J K}^{-1}\), \(V=4.35\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3\).
3
Equation (a): \(U=1.5Nk_{\text{B}}T\)
\(380 = 1.5N(1.38\times10^{-23})(291)\)
4
Answer: \(N = 6.3\times10^{22}\) atoms.
(b) \(PV=Nk_{\text{B}}T\)
\(P(4.35\times10^{-4}) = (6.3\times10^{22})(1.38\times10^{-23})(291)\)
\(P = 5.8\times10^{5}\ \text{Pa}\)
Fig 4 — The given values for worked example 2: T = 18°C (291 K), V = 435 cm³, U = 380 J. N and P are the unknowns being solved for.

🌍 Real-world: why cylinders carry a temperature warning

A sealed gas cylinder has fixed \(N\) and \(V\). Raising its temperature raises the average kinetic energy of every molecule inside, \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}}=\tfrac32k_{\text{B}}T\), which increases both the frequency and the force of molecular collisions with the walls — and therefore the pressure, exactly as \(PV=Nk_{\text{B}}T\) predicts. This is why aerosol cans and gas cylinders carry warnings against being left near heat sources.

✓ Examiner tip: don't just write "the molecules move faster, so the pressure increases" — quote \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}}=\tfrac32k_{\text{B}}T\) and \(PV=Nk_{\text{B}}T\) explicitly to show the mechanism.

🚀 HL Extension – when the ideal gas model breaks down

Real gases obey \(PV=nRT\) under most circumstances, but not when the model's assumptions fail: at high densities and pressures the particles are closer together than assumed; the forces between particles may no longer be negligible; at low temperatures those forces have a greater effect, because the particles are moving more slowly; and most real gases eventually turn into liquids or solids if cooled enough.

Fig 5 — Liquid nitrogen boils at 77 K. Near this temperature, nitrogen gas is a poor fit to the ideal gas model.

Challenge: why does the ideal gas equation become increasingly inaccurate for nitrogen gas as it is cooled toward 77 K, even while it is still a gas? As T falls the molecules move more slowly, so the constant intermolecular attractive forces between them have proportionally more effect on their motion — the "forces are negligible" assumption breaks down, and close to the boiling point the gas starts to condense, violating the ideal-gas assumptions further.

🤔 TOK – models always have limitations

How do our interactions with the material world shape our knowledge?

We cannot directly perceive molecules, so we rely on a model — both visual and mathematical — to understand a gas. Can modelling a system too small to see ever be "good enough" to count as true knowledge, when we can never observe its events directly? One purpose of a model is to simplify reality, to make it easier to understand — so does it matter if the model is not a "true" representation, provided it is useful?

A model of the Solar System, an orrery, represents planets we can directly observe and record. Is that therefore knowledge of a higher order than a model of an ideal gas, whose particles we can never see?

Exam‑style questions
Exam question · Calculate
Q24. (a) Calculate the average random translational kinetic energy of oxygen (O₂) molecules at 300 K. (b) Oxygen molecules each have a mass of \(5.31\times10^{-26}\ \text{kg}\). Determine their average speed at 300 K. (c) Explain why an average carbon dioxide (CO₂) molecule travels slower than oxygen molecules (O₂) at the same temperature. [5 marks]
✓ (a) \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}}=\tfrac32k_{\text{B}}T=1.5\times1.38\times10^{-23}\times300\)
\(=6.21\times10^{-21}\ \text{J}\).
✓ (b) \(\overline{v^2}=2\overline{E_{\text{k}}}/m = (2\times6.21\times10^{-21})/(5.31\times10^{-26})\)
✓ \(=2.34\times10^{5}\ \text{m}^2\text{s}^{-2}\), so \(v\approx484\ \text{m s}^{-1}\).
✓ (c) At the same temperature both gases have the same average kinetic energy. Since \(\overline{E_{\text{k}}}=\tfrac12m\overline{v^2}\), the more massive CO₂ molecule (44 vs 32 g mol⁻¹) must have a lower average speed.
Exam question · Calculate / Explain
Q25. (a) Determine how much energy is needed to raise the temperature of two moles of a monatomic gas from 20°C to 50°C. (b) Why will more energy be needed to raise two moles of a molecular gas through the same temperature rise? [4 marks]
✓ (a) \(\Delta U=\tfrac32nR\Delta T=1.5\times2\times8.31\times30\)
✓ \(=748\ \text{J}\).
✓ (b) A molecular gas also has rotational (and, at higher temperatures, vibrational) kinetic energy modes that must absorb energy as \(T\) rises.
✓ More total energy is therefore needed for the same temperature rise, since translational KE is only part of the molecule's energy.
Exam question · Determine
Q26. At what temperature will \(1.0\times10^{23}\) atoms of an ideal gas have a total internal energy of 1000 J? [3 marks]
✓ \(U=\tfrac32Nk_{\text{B}}T \Rightarrow T=\dfrac{2U}{3Nk_{\text{B}}}\)
✓ \(T=\dfrac{2\times1000}{3\times1.0\times10^{23}\times1.38\times10^{-23}}\)
✓ \(T = 483\ \text{K}\).
Exam question · Estimate / State / Explain
Q27. Some nitrogen gas was cooled from a temperature of 300 K to 100 K. (a) Estimate by what percentage its pressure was reduced. (b) State two assumptions you had to make in order to answer part (a). (c) Explain why the actual final pressure was less than that predicted by theory. [6 marks]
✓ (a) At constant volume, \(P\propto T\), so the percentage reduction \(=\dfrac{300-100}{300}\times100\%\approx67\%\).
✓ (b) Assumed: the volume of the container stayed constant, and the amount of gas (\(n\) or \(N\)) stayed constant (no leaks).
✓ (c) At 100 K, nitrogen (boiling point 77 K) is close to condensing: intermolecular forces are no longer negligible and the gas begins to deviate from ideal behaviour.
✓ Both effects reduce the volume available to freely-moving molecules and reduce the collision rate/force below the ideal-gas prediction, so the measured pressure is lower than theory predicts.
Exam question · Calculate
Q28. What is the pressure of an ideal gas which has a temperature of 47°C and contains \(4.2\times10^{25}\) particles in a volume of 0.037 m³? [3 marks]
✓ \(P=\dfrac{Nk_{\text{B}}T}{V}=\dfrac{(4.2\times10^{25})(1.38\times10^{-23})(320)}{0.037}\)
✓ numerator \(=1.85\times10^{5}\ \text{kg m}^2\text{s}^{-2}\)
✓ \(P = 5.0\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa}\).
Exam question · Explain / Discuss / Calculate
Q29. (a) Explain why the internal energy of an ideal gas can be determined from \(1.5\times\text{pressure}\times\text{volume}\). (b) Discuss why temperature does not appear in this equation. (c) What volume (cm³) of an ideal gas has an internal energy of 10 J and exerts a pressure of twice normal atmospheric pressure? [6 marks]
✓ (a) Since \(PV=Nk_{\text{B}}T=nRT\) and \(U=\tfrac32Nk_{\text{B}}T=\tfrac32nRT\), substituting \(nRT=PV\) gives \(U=1.5PV\).
✓ (b) Temperature has not disappeared — it is hidden inside the product \(PV\), which equals \(Nk_{\text{B}}T\); the relation holds for any \(P\), \(V\), \(T\) combination that gives the same \(PV\).
✓ (c) \(V=\dfrac{U}{1.5P}=\dfrac{10}{1.5\times(2\times101325)}\)
✓ \(=3.29\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^3\)
✓ \(\approx33\ \text{cm}^3\).
Exam question · Calculate / Explain
Q30. The molar heat capacity of a gas is a similar concept to specific heat capacity — the thermal energy needed to raise the temperature of one mole of gas by one kelvin. (a) Calculate the molar heat capacity of argon (assume constant volume). (b) Explain why the molar heat capacity of a molecular gas, oxygen for example, will be greater. (c) Why will the molar heat capacity of argon be greater than your answer to (a) if it is allowed to expand? [6 marks]
✓ (a) For one mole, \(U=\tfrac32RT\), so molar heat capacity \(=\tfrac32R=1.5\times8.31\)
\(=12.5\ \text{J K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}\). Argon is monatomic, so this is its full molar heat capacity.
✓ (b) Oxygen molecules can also store energy in rotational (and, at higher \(T\), vibrational) kinetic energy, not translational KE alone, so more energy is needed to raise the temperature by the same amount.
✓ (c) If allowed to expand at constant pressure, extra energy is needed to do the work of expansion (\(P\Delta V\)) in addition to raising the internal energy, so \(C_P=C_V+R\) is greater than the constant-volume value from (a).
