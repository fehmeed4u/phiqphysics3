Standing Waves and Resonance
Formation of standing waves, harmonics in strings and pipes, natural frequency, damping, and resonance — with full SL and HL content.
Concept 1 — The nature of standing waves

From travelling waves to standing waves

You already know that travelling waves transfer energy progressively away from a source. A standing wave (also called a stationary wave) is fundamentally different: its wave pattern stays fixed in position and transfers no net energy along the medium.

Standing waves form when two identical waves — same frequency, same wavelength, same amplitude — travel in opposite directions through the same medium and continuously superpose. In practice this almost always happens when a wave is reflected back upon itself in a confined space.

Standing wave = two identical waves travelling in opposite directions
Fig 1 — Two identical waves (blue = rightward, amber = leftward) continuously superpose. Their sum produces the standing wave pattern shown at the bottom (dashed envelope shows oscillation limits).

Nodes and antinodes

The standing wave pattern has two special types of point:

Nodes (N) — points where the two waves are always exactly out of phase (phase difference = π). Displacement is permanently zero. Adjacent nodes are separated by λ/2.

Antinodes (A) — points where the two waves are always exactly in phase. Amplitude is maximum. An antinode sits halfway between each pair of adjacent nodes.

All points between two adjacent nodes oscillate in phase with each other but with different amplitudes. Points on opposite sides of a node oscillate in antiphase (phase difference = π).

Distance between adjacent nodes = λ/2  |  Distance between alternate nodes = λ
Fig 2 — Third harmonic on a string fixed at both ends. Red dots = nodes (N), green arrows = antinodes (A). Distance between adjacent nodes = λ/2; between alternate nodes = λ.

⚠️ Standing waves do not transfer energy along the medium. Energy is stored within the system (as kinetic and potential energy of the oscillating medium) but is not propagated outward as it would be in a travelling wave.

Concept 2 — Harmonics in strings and pipes

Standing waves on strings — fixed at both ends

A stretched string fixed at both ends must have nodes at both ends. The possible standing wave modes are called harmonics. The first harmonic (fundamental) has the longest wavelength and lowest frequency f₀.

If the string has length l, the wavelength of the n-th harmonic is:

λₙ = 2l / n    fₙ = n · f₀    f₀ = v / 2l

where v is the wave speed on the string (depends on tension and mass per unit length). All integer harmonics (n = 1, 2, 3 …) are possible.

Fig 3 — First four harmonics for a string fixed at both ends. All integer harmonics are possible. Frequency is always a whole-number multiple of f₀ = v/2l.

Worked Example C4.1 — String harmonics

A stretched rope fixed at both ends has length l = 0.98 m. Wave speed v = 6.7 m s⁻¹.

1
1st harmonic wavelength: λ₀ = 2l = 2 × 0.98 = 1.96 m
2
1st harmonic frequency: f₀ = v / λ₀ = 6.7 / 1.96 = 3.4 Hz
3
4th harmonic wavelength: λ₄ = 2l / 4 = 1.96 / 4 = 0.49 m
4
4th harmonic frequency: f₄ = 4f₀ = 4 × 3.4 = 14 Hz

Standing waves in pipes — three boundary conditions

Longitudinal standing waves in air columns depend on whether each end is open (antinode) or closed (node).

Fig 4 — Boundary conditions for the three pipe types. Open ends → antinodes; closed ends → nodes. Closed–open pipes only support odd harmonics (n = 1, 3, 5 …).
Fig 5a — Open–open pipe: antinodes at both ends. All harmonics possible. λ₀ = 2l.
Fig 5b — Closed–open pipe: node at closed end, antinode at open end. Only odd harmonics. λ₀ = 4l.
⚠️ A common exam mistake: the diagrams of standing waves in pipes show the sideways displacement of air molecules — they are not transverse waves. The actual wave is longitudinal (pressure variation along the pipe axis).
✓ Exam strategy: always state the boundary conditions first (node/antinode at each end), then deduce the wavelength of the first harmonic, then use v = fλ to find frequency. Always show the formula before substituting numbers.

Worked Example C4.3 — Pipe harmonics

A tube closed at both ends, length l = 73.5 cm, shows the 6th harmonic. Loudspeaker frequency = 1410 Hz.

1
Identify harmonic: 6th harmonic → 6 loops → 3 full wavelengths fit in the tube.
2
Wavelength: λ = 2l/6 = 2 × 0.735 / 6 = 0.245 m
3
Wave speed: v = fλ = 1410 × 0.245 = 345 m s⁻¹
4
If open at one end (closed–open), λ₀ = 4l = 2.94 m → f₀ = 345/2.94 ≈ 117 Hz (1st harmonic). Next possible = 3rd harmonic = 3 × 117 = 353 Hz.
string (fixed-fixed): λₙ=2l/nopen-open: λₙ=2l/n closed-open: λₙ=4l/n (odd n)v=fλ always
Concept 3 — Natural frequency and damping

Natural frequency

When an object is disturbed and then left to oscillate without further external influence, it vibrates at its natural frequency (or natural frequencies). This frequency depends only on the physical properties of the system — its mass, stiffness, dimensions — not on how it was disturbed.

Examples: a pendulum, a mass on a spring, a tuning fork, a clamped ruler. Most objects have more than one natural frequency, though one usually dominates.

f₀ depends on mass and stiffness only — not on amplitude (for small oscillations)

Damping SL+HL

All real oscillators experience resistive forces (friction, air resistance, viscosity) that oppose motion. These convert kinetic energy into thermal energy, reducing amplitude over time. This is called damping.

Key feature: in many damped systems the frequency stays approximately constant while amplitude decreases exponentially — successive peaks are multiplied by the same constant fraction (geometric sequence).

Fig 6 — Three types of damping. Light damping: oscillates with decreasing amplitude. Critical damping: returns to equilibrium as fast as possible without overshooting. Heavy damping: very slow return, no oscillation.

🔵 Light damping

System oscillates many times before coming to rest. Amplitude decreases exponentially. Example: pendulum in air, mass on a spring.

🟡 Critical damping

System returns to equilibrium as quickly as possible without overshooting. Example: car suspension system — shock absorbers are designed to be critically damped.

🔴 Heavy damping

Resistive forces are large; the system creeps back slowly to equilibrium without ever passing through it. Takes longer than critical damping. Example: a door closer mechanism, an object oscillating in treacle.

💡 The frequency of a lightly-damped oscillation is approximately equal to its natural frequency. Greater damping reduces the resonant frequency slightly (important for HL).
natural frequency: intrinsic to systemlight: amplitude decays, oscillates critical: fastest return, no overshootheavy: slow return, no oscillation energy ∝ amplitude²
Concept 4 — Forced vibrations and resonance

Forced vibrations

A forced vibration occurs when a periodic external force continuously drives a system at a driving frequency f_d which may differ from the system's natural frequency f₀.

When f_d ≠ f₀ the system vibrates at f_d but with small amplitude. When f_d = f₀ something special happens: resonance.

Resonance

Resonance is the large increase in amplitude of oscillation that occurs when the driving frequency equals (or is very close to) the natural frequency of the system, and the driving force is in phase with the velocity of the oscillator.

At resonance, energy is transferred from the driver to the system at the maximum possible rate. The amplitude grows until the rate of energy input equals the rate of energy dissipation due to damping.

Resonance occurs when: f_driver = f_natural
Fig 7 — Frequency-response curves. Greater damping → lower and broader peak, and the resonant frequency shifts slightly below f₀. At any driving frequency, more damping means smaller amplitude. The peak always occurs close to the natural frequency f₀.

Effect of damping on resonance HL

As damping increases:

① The peak amplitude at resonance decreases (more energy dissipated per cycle)

② The resonance peak becomes broader and flatter (less selective)

③ The resonant frequency shifts slightly below f₀ (the undamped natural frequency)

Since energy ∝ A², even a moderate reduction in amplitude means a large reduction in energy stored.

E ∝ A²  →  halving A reduces energy by a factor of 4

⚠️ Unwanted resonance

Buildings damaged in earthquakes when seismic wave frequency matches natural frequency of structure. Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940) — wind-driven oscillations caused collapse. Millennium Bridge London (2000) — pedestrian footsteps caused lateral resonance.

✅ Useful resonance

MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) — radio waves flip hydrogen proton spins. Microwave ovens — microwaves match vibrational frequency of water molecules. Musical instruments — body resonates with string vibrations to amplify sound. Greenhouse effect — CO₂ absorbs IR radiation by molecular resonance.

✓ To reduce resonance risk, engineers: (1) change the shape/mass/stiffness so natural frequencies do not match driving frequencies; (2) add dampers to dissipate energy; (3) use flexible designs that allow controlled energy dissipation.

Worked Example — Resonance frequency of a mass–spring system HL

Mass m = 740 g = 0.74 kg, spring constant k = 17.2 N m⁻¹. Find natural frequency.

1
Formula for mass–spring: T = 2π√(m/k)
2
Substitute: T = 2π × √(0.74 / 17.2) = 2π × 0.2073 = 1.30 s
3
Natural frequency: f₀ = 1/T = 1/1.30 = 0.77 Hz
4
Resonance occurs when driver frequency = 0.77 Hz. In water (more damping), amplitude at resonance decreases and peak broadens.
