Oscillations & Defining SHM
Terminology, definitions, and the core condition for simple harmonic motion

What is an oscillation?

An oscillation is a repetitive to-and-fro motion about a fixed mean position. The object retraces the same path, taking a fixed time for each complete cycle. Oscillations always involve an interchange between kinetic energy and potential energy.

SystemKinetic energyPotential energy
Mass on horizontal springMoving massElastic PE in spring
Mass on vertical springMoving massElastic PE + gravitational PE
Simple pendulumMoving bobGravitational PE of bob
Buoy in waterMoving buoyGravitational PE of buoy + water

Defining SHM — the core condition

Simple harmonic motion occurs when the acceleration is always directed towards the mean position and is directly proportional to the displacement from that position.

a ∝ −x    or    a = −ω²x

The negative sign is critical — it tells us the acceleration always opposes the displacement (i.e. points back towards equilibrium). This is caused by a restoring force: F = −kx (Hooke's law for a spring; equivalently F ∝ −x for any SHM system).

The time period T of SHM is isochronous — it does NOT depend on the amplitude A. Doubling the amplitude does not change T.
a = −ω²x F = −kx ω = 2π/T T = 1/f
Fig 2 — Acceleration vs displacement graph. The straight line through the origin with negative gradient (−ω²) is the hallmark of SHM. The gradient magnitude gives ω².
Not all oscillations are SHM. SHM requires F ∝ −x strictly — a pendulum only approximates SHM for small angles (< 5° or 0.1 rad).

Exam-style questions — Chunk 1
Exam question · Theory / Explain
a) State the two conditions that must be satisfied for an oscillation to be classified as simple harmonic motion. [2 marks]
1. The acceleration must be directed towards the fixed mean position (restoring force acts towards equilibrium). 2. The magnitude of the acceleration (or restoring force) must be directly proportional to the displacement from the mean position. Both conditions must be stated — saying "F = −kx" alone is insufficient without explaining what each term represents.
Exam question · Proportional reasoning
b) A student claims: "If I look at an acceleration–displacement graph and the line does not pass through the origin, the motion cannot be SHM." Assess this claim. [3 marks]
The student is correct. For SHM, a = −ω²x, meaning a = 0 when x = 0 (at the mean position). An a–x graph that does not pass through the origin would imply a non-zero acceleration when displacement is zero — this contradicts the definition. Such motion might still be oscillatory, but it would NOT be SHM. Award 1 mark for agreeing with the claim, 1 for the reasoning (a must be zero at x = 0), 1 for identifying that oscillation without this condition is not SHM.

SHM Equations & Physical Systems
Angular frequency, mass-spring system, simple pendulum, and how to identify SHM

The SHM equation and angular frequency

The defining equation for SHM uses the angular frequency ω (rad s⁻¹) as the constant of proportionality between acceleration and displacement:

a = −ω²x

Angular frequency relates to period and ordinary frequency as:

ω = 2π/T = 2πf

To identify SHM in a system, find the resultant force using Newton's second law. If F ∝ −x, write the equation of motion in the form a = −(k/m)x to read off ω² = k/m directly.

System 1 — Mass on a vertical spring

Conditions: spring mass negligible, Hooke's law obeyed (spring constant k), air friction negligible, g constant, fixed support.

ω² = k/m   ⟹   T = 2π√(m/k)

Note: T increases with greater mass m (heavier ⟹ slower) and decreases with stiffer spring k (stiffer ⟹ faster). These are the critical IB proportional relationships.

Fig 3 — Mass on a spring. When displaced a distance x from equilibrium, the restoring force F = −kx always points back towards the mean position.

System 2 — Simple pendulum

Conditions: string mass negligible, air friction negligible, angle of swing < 5° (0.1 rad), g constant, fixed pivot, string length l constant.

ω² = g/l   ⟹   T = 2π√(l/g)
The mass of the pendulum bob does NOT appear in the formula — it cancels when applying Newton's second law. T depends only on l and g.
Fig 4 — Simple pendulum. For small angles, the restoring force component mg sinθ ≈ mgθ, making it proportional to displacement — the condition for SHM.

Comparing the two systems

Mass–spring

T = 2π√(m/k)

T ∝ √m (heavier ⟹ longer T)
T ∝ 1/√k (stiffer ⟹ shorter T)
Works in any orientation (gravity cancels)

Simple pendulum

T = 2π√(l/g)

T ∝ √l (longer ⟹ longer T)
T ∝ 1/√g (stronger g ⟹ shorter T)
Mass of bob is irrelevant

Worked example

A 600 g mass is attached to a spring with k = 30 N m⁻¹. Calculate the frequency of oscillation.

Step 1 — Identify SHM: when displaced x, net force = kx upward ⟹ F ∝ −x ✓

T = 2π√(m/k) = 2π√(0.6/30) = 2π√(0.02) = 0.889 s
f = 1/T = 1/0.889 ≈ 1.1 Hz

Exam-style questions — Chunk 2
Exam question · Mathematical
A 0.250 kg mass is suspended from a spring of spring constant 40.0 N m⁻¹ and displaced 0.050 m from equilibrium.

(i) Show that the system undergoes SHM. [2 marks]
(ii) Calculate the period of oscillation. [2 marks]
(iii) Calculate the angular frequency ω. [1 mark]
(i) When displaced x from equilibrium, net upward force = kx = 40x. Since F ∝ −x and is directed back towards mean position, the motion is SHM. ✓✓

(ii) T = 2π√(m/k) = 2π√(0.250/40.0) = 2π√(0.00625) = 2π × 0.0791 = 0.497 s ≈ 0.50 s ✓✓

(iii) ω = 2π/T = 2π/0.497 = 12.6 rad s⁻¹ (or ω = √(k/m) = √(40/0.25) = √160 = 12.6 rad s⁻¹) ✓
Exam question · Reason/Explain
A student argues that a pendulum released from 30° is undergoing SHM because it oscillates. Explain why the student is incorrect and state the condition under which a pendulum does approximate SHM. [3 marks]
The student is incorrect. At 30°, the restoring force is mg sin30° = 0.5mg. The small angle approximation sin θ ≈ θ (in radians) only holds for angles below ~5° (0.1 rad). At 30°, sinθ ≠ θ, so F ≇ mgθ = mg(x/l), meaning F is NOT proportional to x. Therefore the condition for SHM (F ∝ −x) is not met at large angles. A pendulum approximates SHM only for maximum angles less than 5° (or 0.1 rad), where sin θ ≈ θ to within ~0.5%.

Energy in SHM
Kinetic energy, potential energy, total energy, and their variation with displacement and time

Energy overview

In SHM, energy continuously interchanges between kinetic (KE) and potential (PE) forms, while the total mechanical energy remains constant (assuming no damping).

The velocity of an object in SHM varies with displacement as:

v² = ω²(A² − x²)   ⟹   vmax = ωA (at x = 0)

From this, kinetic energy is:

KE = ½mω²(A² − x²)

Potential energy (stored in whichever store provides the restoring force):

PE = ½mω²x²

Total energy (constant, independent of x):

Etotal = ½mω²A²
Fig 5 — Energy vs displacement for SHM. KE is maximum at x = 0 (mean position) and zero at x = ±A (extremes). PE is maximum at x = ±A and zero at x = 0. Total energy (KE + PE) is constant.

Total energy E = ½mω²A². Since ω = 2π/T and T = 2π√(m/k), we get E = ½kA². Total energy is proportional to the square of the amplitude — doubling A quadruples the energy.

Exam question · Mathematical / Multi-step
A particle of mass 0.150 kg undergoes SHM with amplitude 0.080 m and angular frequency 5.0 rad s⁻¹.

(i) Calculate the total energy of the system. [2 marks]
(ii) Calculate the speed of the particle when its displacement is 0.060 m. [2 marks]
(iii) Calculate the displacement at which KE = PE. [3 marks]
(i) E = ½mω²A² = ½ × 0.150 × 25 × 0.0064 = 0.012 J ✓✓

(ii) v = ω√(A²−x²) = 5.0 × √(0.0064 − 0.0036) = 5.0 × √(0.0028) = 5.0 × 0.0529 = 0.265 m s⁻¹ ≈ 0.26 m s⁻¹ ✓✓

(iii) Set KE = PE:
½mω²(A²−x²) = ½mω²x²
A²−x² = x²
A² = 2x²
x = A/√2 = 0.080/√2 = 0.0566 m ≈ 0.057 m ✓✓✓
Exam question · Theory / Explain
Explain, with reference to energy, why the speed of a particle in SHM is greatest at the mean position and zero at the extremes. Your answer should refer to the energy graph. [4 marks]
At the extreme positions (x = ±A), all energy is stored as potential energy (elastic PE in a spring, gravitational PE in a pendulum). Since total energy is fixed and PE is at its maximum (= ½mω²A²), the kinetic energy must be zero (KE = E−PE = 0). With KE = ½mv² = 0, the speed must be zero. ✓✓

At the mean position (x = 0), the restoring force is zero so no further work is done against it, and all the energy has been transferred to kinetic energy (PE = ½mω²×0² = 0, so KE = Etotal = ½mω²A²). Speed is therefore maximum: vmax = ωA. ✓✓

On the energy–displacement graph, the KE curve (inverted parabola) peaks at x = 0 while the PE curve (upward parabola) peaks at x = ±A; their sum is the flat total energy line.
Exam question · Proportional reasoning (Hard)
Two SHM systems A and B have the same mass m and the same total energy. System A has twice the amplitude of system B.

(i) Show that ωA = ωB/2. [2 marks]
(ii) Hence determine the ratio of their maximum speeds vA,max : vB,max. [2 marks]
(iii) What does this tell you about the relationship between ω and A for equal-energy systems? [1 mark]
(i) EA = EB ⟹ ½mωA²(2AB)² = ½mωB²AB²
ωA² × 4AB² = ωB² × AB²
4ωA² = ωB² ⟹ ωA = ωB/2 ✓✓

(ii) vmax = ωA, so:
vA,max = ωA × 2AB = (ωB/2) × 2AB = ωBAB = vB,max
Ratio vA,max : vB,max = 1 : 1 ✓✓

(iii) For equal-energy systems, ωA = constant (ω is inversely proportional to A). Maximum speed is the same regardless of how the same energy is partitioned between frequency and amplitude. ✓
