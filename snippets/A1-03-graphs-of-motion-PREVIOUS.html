Graphs of Motion
Master displacement–time, velocity–time, and acceleration–time graphs — the visual language that unlocks every kinematics problem in IB Physics.

🎯 Hook – The crash that tells a story

During a controlled crash test, engineers don't just watch the collision — they read it from a graph. A velocity–time graph of the impact shows the car accelerating on the test track, travelling at constant speed, then in just 80 milliseconds, velocity plunging to zero. Hidden inside that graph is the displacement, the deceleration force, and the exact moment the airbag must fire. Every detail of the crash is encoded in the shape, gradient, and area of a single graph.

Fig 1 — Velocity–time graph of a crash-test vehicle. Phase A (rising): constant acceleration on the track. Phase B (flat): constant speed approach. Phase C (steep drop): violent deceleration on impact. The area of each phase = displacement for that phase.
💡 Diagnostic question (think first — answer below) — Looking at Phase C on the graph above, the line is nearly vertical (extremely steep negative gradient). What does this tell you about the acceleration during the crash? Answer: a very large negative acceleration (deceleration). Gradient = Δv/Δt — a near-vertical line means a huge change in velocity in a tiny time interval, i.e. enormous deceleration force.

📐 Displacement–time (s–t) graphs

A displacement–time graph plots position \(s\) (metres) on the vertical axis against time \(t\) (seconds) on the horizontal. It records where the object is at every moment.

The gradient of an s–t graph equals the velocity. For a straight-line segment the velocity is constant; for a curved section, draw a tangent at the point of interest — its gradient gives the instantaneous velocity (key HL skill).

Velocity from an s–t graph $$v = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = \text{gradient of the } s\text{–}t \text{ graph}$$
Fig 2 — Straight line → constant velocity. Steeper = faster. Negative slope = moving in −ve direction. Horizontal = stationary (v = 0).
Fig 3 — Curved s–t graph. Steepening curve = speeding up. Flattening curve = slowing down. Horizontal end = object has stopped.
💡 Key rule: On an s–t graph the y-value tells you position; the gradient tells you velocity. A steep graph ≠ large acceleration — it means large speed. Acceleration requires a change in gradient (a curve).

📐 Velocity–time (v–t) graphs

The velocity–time graph is the most information-rich motion graph. From it you can extract both acceleration (gradient) and displacement (area).

Acceleration from a v–t graph $$a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \text{gradient of the } v\text{–}t \text{ graph}$$
Displacement from a v–t graph $$s = \text{area between graph line and time axis}$$

Area above the time axis = positive displacement. Area below the time axis = negative displacement (object moving in reverse). The line crossing the axis means the object momentarily has zero velocity and is reversing direction.

Fig 4 — Composite v–t graph. Rising segment (gradient = +a): acceleration. Flat segment (gradient = 0): constant velocity. Falling segment (gradient = −a): deceleration. Each shaded area = displacement for that phase. Sum all three areas for total displacement.
⚠️ Area trap: When the v–t line crosses the x-axis, split the total area at the crossing point. The area above = positive displacement; the area below = negative displacement. Adding both as positive values gives total distance, not net displacement.

📐 Acceleration–time (a–t) graphs

An acceleration–time graph plots \(a\) (m s\(^{-2}\)) against time. Its defining property: the area under the graph equals the change in velocity.

Change in velocity from an a–t graph $$\Delta v = \text{area between the } a\text{–}t \text{ graph and the time axis}$$
Fig 5 — Constant acceleration: horizontal line. Shaded area = Δv gained. If starting from rest, final velocity = area.
Fig 6 — Linked graphs for uniform acceleration: s–t parabola (blue), v–t straight line (purple). The gradient chain connects them: grad(s–t) → v–t; grad(v–t) → a–t.
✓ The gradient–area chain (HL essential): grad(s–t) = v · grad(v–t) = a · area(v–t) = s · area(a–t) = Δv. This chain underpins every kinematics calculation in IB Physics.

✍️ Worked example – Reading a composite v–t graph

A car starts from rest. It accelerates uniformly to 24 m s⁻¹ in 6 s, travels at constant velocity for 4 s, then decelerates uniformly to rest in 3 s. Calculate (a) the acceleration in phase 1, (b) the deceleration in phase 3, (c) the total displacement.

1
Identify: Three-phase v–t graph. Acceleration = gradient of each phase. Displacement = area under each phase (triangles and rectangle). We need gradient of phase 1, gradient of phase 3, and sum of all three areas.
2
Data: Phase 1: \(u = 0\), \(v = 24\) m s⁻¹, \(\Delta t = 6\) s. Phase 2: \(v = 24\) m s⁻¹ (constant), \(\Delta t = 4\) s. Phase 3: \(u = 24\) m s⁻¹, \(v = 0\), \(\Delta t = 3\) s.
3
Equations and substitution:
(a) \(\;a_1 = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{24-0}{6} = +4\) m s\(^{-2}\)

(b) \(\;a_3 = \dfrac{0-24}{3} = -8\) m s\(^{-2}\) (deceleration magnitude: 8 m s\(^{-2}\))

(c) \(\;s = \tfrac{1}{2}(6)(24) + (4)(24) + \tfrac{1}{2}(3)(24) = 72 + 96 + 36 = 204\) m
4
Answer: Acceleration = \(+4\) m s\(^{-2}\); deceleration magnitude = \(8\) m s\(^{-2}\); total displacement = 204 m. The steeper phase-3 gradient (magnitude) confirms a larger deceleration — the same speed change occurs in half the time.
Fig 9 — v–t graph for the worked example. Phase 1 triangle (area = 72 m), phase 2 rectangle (area = 96 m), phase 3 triangle (area = 36 m). Total shaded area = 204 m. Note phase 3 has a steeper slope than phase 1 — larger deceleration magnitude.

🚗 Real-world: Airbag deployment timing

Airbag control units use an accelerometer to record the vehicle's deceleration as an a–t signal. The processor continuously integrates this signal (computing the area under the a–t graph) to find \(\Delta v\). When \(\Delta v\) exceeds a threshold in under 15 ms — a tiny rectangular area on the a–t graph — the airbag ignites. Engineers design for decelerations of 30–50 g over ≈ 6 ms. A v–t graph of the crash shows the velocity collapsing from ≈ 50 km h⁻¹ to zero in that 6 ms window.

✓ Examiner tip: When asked to "determine the change in velocity from an a–t graph," always state the geometric shape (rectangle, triangle, or trapezium), write out the area calculation explicitly, and include units (m s⁻¹). Each of these steps earns a mark.

🚀 HL Extension – Instantaneous values from curved graphs

At HL, v–t graphs can be curved (non-uniform acceleration). The instantaneous acceleration equals the gradient of the tangent to the curve. Similarly, displacement from a curved v–t graph is estimated by counting grid squares under the curve.

The kinematic equations \(v = u + at\), \(s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2\) etc. are valid only for uniform acceleration (straight-line v–t). For a curved v–t you must use graphical methods.

Challenge: A v–t graph is a straight line from \((0,\,0)\) to \((T,\,v_0)\). Show that the area formula gives \(s = \tfrac{1}{2}v_0 T\) and identify which kinematic equation this corresponds to. (Answer: area of triangle = \(\tfrac{1}{2}(u+v)t = \tfrac{1}{2}(0+v_0)T\) — this IS the kinematic equation \(s = \tfrac{1}{2}(u+v)t\).)

⚠️ Trap corner – common mistakes

MisconceptionCorrect view
"Steep s–t graph = large acceleration"Steep s–t gradient = large velocity. Acceleration requires a change in gradient — only a curve on the s–t graph shows acceleration.
"Gradient of v–t = displacement"Gradient of v–t = acceleration. Area under v–t = displacement. These are completely different operations.
"When v = 0, acceleration = 0"At the peak of a throw, v = 0 but a = −g ≠ 0. Velocity and acceleration are independent quantities.
"Area under v–t always = distance"Area below the x-axis counts as negative displacement. For net displacement use signed areas; for total distance take all areas as positive.
"Horizontal a–t line means object is stopped"A horizontal a–t line at a = 0 means constant velocity — the object could be moving fast. It is stopped only if v = 0 as well.
⚠️ Most common exam slip: Swapping gradient and area on a v–t graph — writing "area = acceleration" or "gradient = displacement." This single error loses marks on both Paper 1 MCQs and Paper 2 data questions. Always ask yourself: Is the question asking about the gradient or the area?
grad(s–t) = v grad(v–t) = a area(v–t) = s area(a–t) = Δv tangent → instantaneous value area below axis = −ve displacement

Exam‑style questions
Exam question · State and Explain
(a) State what is represented by the gradient of a velocity–time graph. [1]
(b) A v–t graph shows a straight line of negative gradient that does not cross the time axis. Explain what this tells us about the object's motion. [2]
(a) ✓ The gradient of a v–t graph equals the acceleration of the object (m s⁻²).

(b) ✓ The object has a constant (uniform) deceleration / constant negative acceleration.
✓ The object continues to move in the positive direction throughout (v remains positive, never reaching zero), but its speed decreases at a constant rate.
Exam question · Calculate
A cyclist starts from rest and accelerates uniformly for 8 s reaching 12 m s⁻¹. She cycles at this constant speed for 15 s, then decelerates uniformly to rest in 6 s.

(a) Calculate her acceleration during the first phase. [1]
(b) Calculate the total distance travelled. [3]
(c) Sketch and label the corresponding acceleration–time graph for the entire journey. [3]
(a) ✓ \(a = \Delta v/\Delta t = 12/8 = 1.5\) m s⁻²

(b) ✓ Phase 1 (triangle): \(s_1 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 8 \times 12 = 48\) m
✓ Phase 2 (rectangle): \(s_2 = 15 \times 12 = 180\) m
✓ Phase 3 (triangle): \(s_3 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 12 = 36\) m · Total = 264 m

(c) ✓ Horizontal line at +1.5 m s⁻² from t = 0 to t = 8 s.
✓ Horizontal line at a = 0 from t = 8 s to t = 23 s.

Exam question · Explain and Calculate (HL)
The s–t graph for an object is a smooth curve that becomes progressively less steep, eventually becoming horizontal.

(a) Describe the motion. [2]
(b) Sketch the corresponding v–t graph and explain its shape with reference to the s–t graph. [3]
(c) A tangent to the s–t curve at t = 4 s has gradient 3.2 m s⁻¹; at t = 6 s its gradient is 1.4 m s⁻¹. Estimate the average acceleration between t = 4 s and t = 6 s. [2]
(a) ✓ The object moves in the positive direction (displacement increasing) but decelerates (curve flattening).
✓ It eventually comes to rest (horizontal section → v = 0).

(b) ✓ v–t graph starts at a positive value, slopes downward (decelerating), and reaches zero.
✓ Shape is a curve (not a straight line) because the gradient of the s–t graph changes non-linearly.
✓ The gradient of the s–t graph at each instant equals the y-value of the v–t graph at the same instant.

(c) ✓ \(\bar{a} = \Delta v / \Delta t = (1.4 - 3.2)/(6 - 4) = -1.8/2\)
✓ \(\bar{a} = -0.9\) m s⁻² (negative → decelerating; accept 0.9 m s⁻² if direction stated separately)
